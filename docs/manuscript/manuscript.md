## Abstract

### Objective

Are ARBITER's ratings closer to blinded experts than the same model unassisted, and does published-label agreement give the same comparison?

### Materials and Methods

Both arms used the same model and identical trial documents: 102 randomised trials from 16 meta-analyses. Unassisted received a minimal prompt without RoB 2 guidance; ARBITER enforced official rules and checked quotations against stored sources. Two blinded experts independently rated 510 trial–domain cells before choosing among blinded labels. The prespecified primary endpoint measured ARBITER preference among agreed choices where arms disagreed.

### Results

Experts preferred ARBITER in 53/60 cells (88.3%; 95% CI 79.7–96.3). Paired agreement gains against Experts 1 and 2 were 7.6 percentage points (95% CI 5.1–10.2) and 6.5 (3.5–9.4); ARBITER's weighted κ values (0.51, 0.69) fell either side of expert-to-expert κ 0.59. Published-label agreement showed no clear difference: −1.8 points (−5.1 to 1.6). Stricter ARBITER ratings matched the experts: sensitivity gains for Some concerns or High were 16.3 points (95% CI 10.6–22.1) and 19.5 (12.8–26.4), respectively, without lower specificity. Rules-only recomputation did not close the gap. Unassisted had 65/509 domain rule breaks; ARBITER had zero by design; 1407/1407 quotations were found in the stored source. Median runtime was 7.6 versus 0.5 minutes/trial; API list-price equivalents were US$3.78 versus US$0.09.

### Discussion

Against published labels, ARBITER's stricter ratings looked like lost specificity; against blinded experts, they did not. The minimal-prompt comparator and two-expert reference limit generalisation.

### Conclusion

ARBITER agreed more often with blinded experts than the unassisted model. Evaluations need expert assessment alongside published labels.

## Background and significance

An AI risk-of-bias tool needs judgements a reviewer can check and judgements close to careful expert assessment. The latter requirement is commonly measured against published review labels, although these references can disagree. The Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials, RoB 2, structures assessment of a specific trial result through signalling questions, domain algorithms and an Overall judgement.[1] Its judgements inform assessments of evidence certainty, yet applying it is demanding. Minozzi and colleagues found low agreement even among experienced assessors; implementation instructions improved reliability but required substantial preparation.[2,3] Published reviews also disagree when rating the same trials, as Bertizzolo and colleagues and Yao and colleagues documented for the earlier instrument.[4,5] These findings caution against treating published ratings as an unquestioned reference. Evidence-synthesis organisations require human oversight and transparent reporting when AI makes or suggests judgements.[6,7]

Automated risk-of-bias assessment has a lineage of linking ratings to text. RobotReviewer paired predictions with extracted sentences and opportunities for human correction.[8] More recent RoB 2 studies report modest agreement with published labels and a tendency toward stricter ratings. Nyrhi, Hevia and colleagues' ROBoto2, Gandhi, Taneri and Lai describe this pattern, often interpreting strictness as model error.[9–13] That interpretation depends on the reference being dependable. The pattern is specific to the RoB 2 literature considered here; it should not be generalised to other appraisal instruments.

Existing systems address parts of the problem. Huang derived ratings from model answers in a spreadsheet, Choi recomputed ratings with coded algorithms, and ROBoto2 integrated flowchart aggregation with retrieval and human feedback.[10,14,15] Liang checked generated claims against retrieved passages after assessment.[16] Saadi evaluated structured agent-based implementations for ROBINS-I, although external tool use was not established.[17] In clinical informatics, CPGPrompt translates guidelines into structured decision trees that an LLM navigates; structured paths still leave interpretation to the model.[18] Eisele-Metzger and Sahu call for purpose-built expert references rather than relying solely on published review labels.[19,20]

The remaining gap concerns whether enforced rules and checked evidence improve the judgements themselves. An algorithm can faithfully transform mistaken answers, and a quotation can exist without supporting an answer. An LLM workflow in which rules and evidence checks are the only route to a rating might give answers closer to expert judgement than an unassisted model or published labels. To our knowledge, this has not been tested against a blinded expert reference covering every RoB 2 domain, distinct from published labels.

## Objective

We asked whether ARBITER's ratings were closer to blinded experts than those of the same model given a minimal prompt and identical trial sources, and whether agreement with published labels gave the same comparison. The prespecified forced-choice hypothesis was that experts would prefer ARBITER when the arms disagreed.

## Materials and methods

### System design

ARBITER separates evidence interpretation from deterministic checks. The AI model reads trial documents, identifies the result and answers signalling questions; rule-based software stores a content-hashed copy of each source, extracts quoted spans, binds cited evidence to its source and applies the official algorithms (Figure 1). A reviewer approves the proposed result before domain assessment and can inspect the answers and cited evidence afterward. Each required answer must have an evidence basis. Text quotations are server-extracted spans; image regions are rendered and linked to source pages. Model-written image descriptions are not independently checked against image content. The exported HTML report and audit file retain sources, answers, evidence and decisions for independent verification.

ARBITER computes proposed ratings and permits a reasoned, recorded override with its rationale and evidence. Thus, the software guarantees a checked proposal and an auditable route for any departure; it does not make interpretation infallible. We checked proposed and adopted ratings separately. AVATAR illustrates the workflow for all-cause death after early surgery versus watchful waiting in asymptomatic severe aortic stenosis (Figure 2; complete AVATAR trace in the supplement). It was selected to explain the process, rather than represent typical performance. Its stored documents did not establish a dated analysis plan for the assessed result.

### Benchmark and assessment arms

The benchmark comprised trials from published meta-analyses, with source reviews, specialties and label distributions documented in the supplementary benchmark table. Assessment prompts targeted the published review's labelled result using its outcome, time point, contrast and printed result, rather than automatically choosing the trial's primary outcome. The binding audit identified 33 explicit result matches, 11 matches within several covered outcomes, 32 inferred matches, 7 partial matches, 9 different results and 10 unknown bindings. Twelve label sets were trial-wide, seven jointly covered pain and disability, and ten had unknown result binding; these categories were retained rather than relabelled as exact result matches. De Waal's supplied registry record belongs to another trial; its presence does not establish relevant information about the assessed result.

Both arms received identical underlying trial sources: articles, supplements, registry projections, protocols and statistical analysis plans available in the captured input set. Unassisted received copies of the registry projections and acquired documents from the ARBITER run; nothing was fetched anew. Document availability varied by trial. Unassisted was a minimal-prompt condition: its prompt contained the assessment task sentence followed by one fixed output-format sentence, with no RoB 2 guidance. It could read and search local documents, but had no ARBITER service, assessment skill or web access. ARBITER additionally received the assessment skill and its guidance. Input parity therefore concerns trial evidence, not identical instructions or processing. The comparison tests the complete system; it cannot isolate the effects of guidance, software or additional processing time.

Both arms used `claude-sonnet-5-5`, medium effort, in Claude Code 2.1.278 with isolated settings, connectors and memory disabled. ARBITER ran on 12 October 2026 and Unassisted on 14 October 2026. The reported software is ARBITER 1.0.0, public commit [2f549ae](https://github.com/AliSalman-et-al/ARBITER/tree/2f549ae30f056ff441ea61a04b719968716f2219). Each arm assessed each trial once. The ARBITER harness approved the proposed result unchanged. The protocol allowed “Continue.” at most twice per phase without coaching. The prompt templates and the Unassisted output-format instruction are reproduced in eMethods, including the ARBITER skill invocation and a filled example. The prompts were distinct. Several prior studies instead used structured RoB 2 prompts, so this comparator does not represent all prior prompting designs.[11–14]

### Expert reference standard

Two blinded human experts assessed all 102 trials × D1–D5 using a structured form, with no AI used for any judgement. Each expert recorded signalling answers and a domain rating for each cell and froze both as the independent rating before seeing any label. Each then saw three blinded labels—published, Unassisted and ARBITER—in an order randomised for each cell, without rationales, and chose the most defensible label. Each subsequently gave a revised rating, which could differ from all offered labels, and recorded answer retention or change. Experts could consult sources beyond the arms' inputs. Gartlehner's blinded adjudication of discordant data-extraction items supplied the design precedent.[21] [Expert qualifications, experience and training]. The assessment form and procedures are in eMethods.

### Outcomes

The prespecified primary endpoint was the share of cells where the arms disagreed and both experts made the same forced choice whose chosen label matched ARBITER. A cell was a trial–domain pair. Credit followed label equality: every source matching the chosen label received credit; neither arm received credit if neither label matched. Cells where all three labels matched provided no preference information. Primary sensitivities used each expert on all arm disagreements and counted split choices as ARBITER losses without assigning Unassisted wins.

Prespecified secondary outcomes were agreement with each expert's independent domain ratings, expert-to-expert agreement, signalling-answer agreement, published-label agreement, rule conformance, evidence checks, runtime and cost. These outcomes were specified in the version-controlled analysis plan of 18 October 2026, before the expert ratings reported here were analysed; because the experts assessed every cell, the planned comparisons were applied to all 510 cells. Linearly weighted Cohen's κ was the main independent-rating agreement measure, accompanied by exact agreement and paired differences. Published labels were scored as another source against each expert. We also reported confusion matrices, unweighted κ, expert means and agreement restricted to cells where the experts' independent ratings agreed.

Signalling-answer comparisons used explicit answers matched by trial, domain, question and component. Five-level matching distinguished yes, probably yes, no, probably no and no information; collapsed matching combined affirmative and negative levels, leaving no information separate. Missing, inactive, not applicable, unmatched and unresolved answers were excluded; paired contrasts required evaluable answers in both arms. Published-label comparisons used weighted κ, exact agreement, sensitivity for Some concerns or High and specificity, with Gwet's AC2 supplementary. The All-Low rule described label prevalence; its κ was suppressed because predictions had no variance. Missing predictions counted as disagreement.

Rule checks applied the official RoB 2 algorithms to each arm's own answers, using effect of assignment for the deviations domain.[1] The domains cover randomisation, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement and selection of the reported result. A rating contradicting its answers or a skipped required question formed a rule break; absent ratings and answers were unevaluable. A sensitivity resolved invalid required answers recursively as negative or no information. Overall checks allowed escalation when multiple domains had Some concerns. Evidence coverage counted required active answers citing a passage or image; quotation and bundle audits checked source binding and structural integrity, rather than whether evidence justified an answer. Runtime summed wall-clock turn durations. Cost reconstruction used recorded token components and frozen provider rates, with cache and side-call handling detailed in eMethods. Runs used a subscription; dollar figures were API list-price equivalents rather than expenditure.

Exploratory analyses comprised derived Overall against experts, direction of disagreement, rules-only recomputation, relative-severity strata and severity distributions, including binary sensitivity and specificity against each expert. Derived Overall applied the official rule to independent domains, taking the worst domain without optional escalation for multiple Some concerns. Main-table Overall comparisons used the 81 trials with published Overall; the other 21 were excluded. Additional comparisons used all 102 trials, with denominators stated. Directional analyses partitioned stricter or more lenient ratings relative to published labels into expert matches to the arm, published label or neither. Rules-only recomputation replaced Unassisted ratings contradicting their answers with the official output, retaining invalid paths and missing ratings. Original disagreement populations stayed fixed; new ties could credit both arms. Forced choices across all disputed cells and revised ratings were descriptive; seeing labels could anchor revised responses.

### Statistical analysis

We used 10,000 stratified trial-cluster bootstrap resamples, seed 20261018, retaining the number of trials within each meta-analysis and all cells from each sampled trial. Percentile intervals describe 95% uncertainty; paired contrasts used identical resampling weights. Expert means averaged expert-specific estimates within each shared resample rather than pooling independent observations. No p-values or multiplicity adjustments were used. Only the primary endpoint was confirmatory; the other prespecified outcomes were secondary and the exploratory analyses were labelled accordingly. Reporting follows TRIPOD-LLM and GRRAS, with an accompanying item map and author-completion fields.[22,23]

## Results

Of 105 benchmark trials, 3 were excluded for unsupported designs, leaving 102 trials from 16 meta-analyses and 510 domain cells (Figure 3). Both experts rated Low less often than any other source in the exploratory severity analysis (Table 1): Published 427/510 (83.7%); ARBITER 375/510 (73.5%); Unassisted 407/510 (79.8%); Expert 1 277/510 (54.3%); Expert 2 346/510 (67.8%). The trials spanned 15 specialties; source and document availability are summarised with benchmark characteristics (eTable 1). The experts agreed on 388/510 domain ratings (76.1%; weighted κ 0.59), with the lowest agreement in D3 (54/102; eTable 2).

The experts preferred ARBITER in the prespecified forced-choice comparison: their agreed choice matched its rating in 53/60 cells (88.3%; 95% CI 79.7–96.3) where the arms disagreed, versus 7/60 for Unassisted (Figure 4; eTable 3).

ARBITER agreed more often with both experts in the prespecified independent-rating comparisons. Expert 1 agreed with ARBITER in 371/510 cells (72.7%), Unassisted in 332/510 (65.1%) and Published in 301/510 (59.0%). Expert 2 agreed with ARBITER in 433/510 cells (84.9%), Unassisted in 400/510 (78.4%) and Published in 362/510 (71.0%). The paired ARBITER − Unassisted differences (95% CIs) were 7.6 (5.1–10.2) and 6.5 (3.5–9.4) percentage points, respectively. ARBITER’s weighted κ values (0.51, 0.69) fell either side of expert-to-expert κ 0.59. Unassisted (0.35, 0.53) and Published (0.21, 0.29) fell below that yardstick. Exploratory derived Overall comparisons on the 81 trials with a published Overall gave paired differences of 13.6 (6.2–21.0) and 17.3 (8.6–25.9) percentage points. Absolute Overall agreement was lower for every source; the experts agreed with each other on 42/81 matched Overall ratings (κ 0.33), and ARBITER’s matched Overall agreement was 32/81 against Expert 1 and 60/81 against Expert 2 (Table 2; Figure 5A; eTables 4–6; eFigure 1).

ARBITER gained sensitivity for Some concerns or High without lower specificity against either expert in the exploratory binary comparisons. Specificity was 97.8% versus 95.7% against Expert 1 and 95.1% versus 95.1% against Expert 2 for ARBITER versus Unassisted. Against Expert 1, sensitivity was 55.4% versus 39.1% for ARBITER versus Unassisted; paired sensitivity and specificity differences were 16.3 (10.6–22.1) and 2.2 (0.7–3.9) percentage points, respectively. Against Expert 2, sensitivity was 72.0% versus 52.4% for ARBITER versus Unassisted; paired sensitivity and specificity differences were 19.5 (12.8–26.4) and 0.0 (−2.5 to 2.6) percentage points, respectively (95% CIs; eTables 7–8). When ARBITER was stricter than Published, Expert 1 matched ARBITER in 64/88 cells and Published in 5/88; Expert 2 matched ARBITER in 65/88 and Published in 14/88 (Figure 5B).

Agreement with published labels, the prespecified benchmark, showed no clear difference between the arms in exact agreement or κ. The paired exact-agreement difference was −1.8 (−5.1 to 1.6) percentage points and the weighted κ difference 0.06 (−0.03 to 0.14) (95% CIs; Table 2; Figure 5A; eTables 9–10). Against published labels, ARBITER’s higher sensitivity came with lower specificity: paired differences were +15.7 (6.8–24.4) and −4.4 (−7.6 to −1.2) percentage points, respectively (95% CIs); against the experts it did not.

ARBITER’s signalling answers agreed more often with the experts in the prespecified comparisons; exploratory rules-only recomputation did not raise Unassisted’s rating agreement. Paired collapsed signalling differences were 5.0 (3.6–6.4) and 3.3 (1.9–4.7) percentage points against Experts 1 and 2, respectively (Figure 6; eTable 11; eFigure 2). Recomputation changed 26 Unassisted domain ratings, including 6 original arm disagreements. Among 60 agreed forced choices, Unassisted matched 7 before and 8 after recomputation. Its independent-rating agreement changed from 332/510 to 324/510 against Expert 1 and from 400/510 to 391/510 against Expert 2 (eTable 12).

ARBITER enforced the domain rules, while the prespecified process audit found rule breaks in Unassisted ratings. Unassisted had 65/509 evaluable domain ratings with a rule break (12.8%; 95% CI 10.0–15.6). ARBITER had zero domain rule breaks by design and no recorded domain overrides; all 1538 required answers cited evidence, 1407/1407 quotations were found in the stored source, and all 102 audit files passed the independent verifier (Table 3; eTables 13–14).

ARBITER required more time and computing resources in the prespecified analyses. Median [IQR] runtime was 7.6 [6.1–10.5] minutes per trial for ARBITER and 0.5 [0.4–0.5] for Unassisted, with a paired mean difference of 11.0 (8.4–14.1) minutes. API list-price equivalents were US$3.78 [3.46–4.10] and US$0.09 [0.07–0.13] per trial, respectively, with a paired mean difference of US$3.68 (3.61–3.75) (95% CIs; Table 3; eTable 15). Descriptive forced choices on disputed cells and revised ratings are reported separately (eTables 16–17). Invalid-path sensitivity, cost reconstruction, token reconciliation and derived Overall validation are also supplied (eTables 18–21). Supplementary plots show review-specific published-label agreement and domain- and question-level signalling comparisons (eFigures 3–5).

## Discussion

ARBITER's complete workflow produced ratings closer to blinded experts than the same model without the workflow, and its ratings were preferred in the prespecified forced-choice comparison. Agreement with published labels showed no clear difference between the arms. The comparison therefore depended on the reference, even though the model ratings were unchanged.

Published labels made ARBITER's stricter ratings appear less dependable, whereas independent experts usually shared those ratings. Earlier RoB 2 studies often interpreted strictness against published labels as model error.[9–13] Against the experts, ARBITER gained sensitivity for Some concerns or High without losing specificity; against published labels, the same ratings looked like lost specificity. Gandhi's blinded panel also assigned more severe ratings than the published reviews, pointing in the same direction despite a different design.[11] These findings concern the present benchmark, with its result-matching limitations; they do not establish that published reviews generally underestimate bias. Effects on certainty judgements or clinical recommendations were not measured.

The answer comparisons suggest a contribution beyond final rule calculation. Rules-only recomputation reduced Unassisted's agreement with both experts, while ARBITER's collapsed signalling answers matched the experts more often. Huang reported benefits from deriving ratings from model answers, while Choi demonstrated the importance of checking algorithm conformance.[14,15] Eisele-Metzger found Overall-rule inconsistencies.[19] These studies identify a shared failure mode, but instruments, paths and denominators differ. Enforcing arithmetic prevented rule breaks, while the workflow and RoB 2 guidance together may have improved the elicited answers. Their individual contributions require a factorial comparison.

ARBITER combines features that prior systems developed separately (Table 4). RobotReviewer established the link between automated ratings and reviewable text.[8] ROBoto2 computes flowchart ratings and retains original responses and human edits; it is a substantive precursor.[10] Huang and Choi examined algorithm-derived judgements, while Liang added post hoc source-support evaluation.[14–16] Saadi's structured agent-based work concerns ROBINS-I; its description does not establish external tool calling.[17] CPGPrompt contributes the broader informatics pattern of formal guideline paths, although an LLM navigates its trees.[18] The present evaluation combines software-controlled answer acceptance, source-bound quotations, an independently verifiable audit file and blinded dual expert assessment of all domains. Several prior matched comparisons also used identical underlying sources; input parity is not an exclusive contribution. Evidence and quotation locations for the comparison are in eTable 22.

Deployment requires accounting for human review as well as computing. The API list-price equivalent provides a reproducible computational reference; the study's subscription expenditure cannot be allocated from these token counts. In the authors' experience, flat-rate plans can carry usage whose API list-price equivalent far exceeds the subscription price; that anecdote is plan-dependent and is not an economic evaluation. Human checking time was not measured. System runtime is not staff time, and comparison with published human assessment times would mix denominators and tasks. ARBITER should serve as an auditable assistant. Prospective use should measure checking time, corrections and automation bias, since a detailed audit file may encourage unwarranted acceptance as well as scrutiny.

The design supports a system-level claim and cannot separate the contributions of guidance, software and additional processing time. No structured-prompt comparator was tested; Unassisted received a minimal prompt without RoB 2 guidance, whereas ARBITER's assessment skill carries that guidance. Each arm ran once, so run-to-run variability is unknown. Trial reports or source reviews may have appeared in training; publication dates cannot establish exposure.

Reference reliability limits interpretation: only two experts contributed, with domain κ 0.59, lowest agreement in D3 and agreement on 42/81 derived Overall ratings; they could access sources beyond the arms' inputs. Expert-to-expert agreement is a yardstick, not a maximum any model can achieve against an individual expert. Forced choice offered only three labels and may have anchored responses; independent ratings were frozen beforehand. Overall was derived from domains rather than rated directly. Some published labels were broad, inferred or mismatched to the assessed result, so disagreements may partly reflect outcome mismatch. De Waal's supplied registry record belonged to another trial, illustrating the need to check document identity and applicability. Strictness strata were small. Whether quotations supported the answers was not assessed, and model-written image descriptions were not verified semantically. Structural verification cannot establish clinical validity or performance on other appraisal instruments.

### Conclusion

ARBITER's auditable RoB 2 workflow agreed more often with blinded experts than the unassisted model, while agreement with published labels did not distinguish the arms. Evaluations of AI risk-of-bias tools need a blinded expert reference alongside published labels, because the two can rank systems differently.
