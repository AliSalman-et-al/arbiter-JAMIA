## Abstract

### Objective

Evaluate whether a workflow enforcing rules and checking sources can make AI risk-of-bias assessment auditable while preserving judgement quality.

### Materials and Methods

We compared ARBITER with a minimal-prompt Unassisted model on identical trial documents. Two blinded adjudicators independently rated disputed domains before choosing among randomised labels.

### Results

Adjudicators preferred ARBITER in 54/62 arm disagreements (87.1%; 95% CI 79.0–94.3). They upheld stricter ratings in 73/77 ARBITER and 48/59 Unassisted disagreements with published labels. Unassisted broke domain rules in 65/509 evaluable ratings; every ARBITER quotation was found in its stored source. Agreement with published labels was poor (weighted κ 0.34 and 0.28). Post hoc rule recomputation changed 3 of 62 arm disagreements. Question 5.1 alone changed the rating in 18 Unassisted losses; 13 had supplied registry records, protocols or analysis plans, but none of the justifications mentioned such documents. Median system runtime was 7.6 versus 0.5 minutes/trial; API list-price equivalents were US$3.78 versus US$0.09.

### Discussion

Stricter ratings were mostly upheld, making agreement with published labels a misleading benchmark in these disputed cells. The gain came from elicited answers rather than arithmetic alone. Selection on disagreement limits generalisation.

### Conclusion

ARBITER provided auditable assessments that adjudicators usually preferred when the arms disagreed. The system-level findings warrant evaluation with broader independent reference ratings and measured human checking.

## Background and significance

AI judgements used in evidence synthesis must be checkable because they can affect how much confidence clinicians place in research. The Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials, RoB 2, structures assessment of a specific trial result through signalling questions, domain algorithms and an Overall judgement.[1] Its judgements inform assessments of evidence certainty, yet applying it is demanding. Minozzi and colleagues found low agreement even among experienced assessors; implementation instructions improved reliability but required substantial preparation.[2,3] Published reviews also disagree when rating the same trials, as Bertizzolo and colleagues and Yao and colleagues documented for the earlier instrument.[4,5] These findings caution against treating published ratings as an unquestioned reference. Evidence-synthesis organisations require human oversight and transparent reporting when AI makes or suggests judgements.[6,7]

Automated risk-of-bias assessment has a lineage of linking ratings to text. RobotReviewer paired predictions with extracted sentences and opportunities for human correction.[8] More recent RoB 2 studies report modest agreement with published labels and a tendency toward stricter ratings. Nyrhi, Hevia and colleagues' ROBoto2, Gandhi, Taneri and Lai describe this pattern, often interpreting strictness as model error.[9–13] That interpretation depends on the reference being correct. The pattern is specific to the RoB 2 literature considered here; it should not be generalised to other appraisal instruments.

Existing systems address parts of the problem. Huang derived ratings from model answers in a spreadsheet, Choi recomputed ratings with coded algorithms, and ROBoto2 integrated flowchart aggregation with retrieval and human feedback.[10,14,15] Liang checked generated claims against retrieved passages after assessment.[16] Saadi evaluated structured agent-based implementations for ROBINS-I, although external tool use was not established.[17] In clinical informatics, CPGPrompt translates guidelines into structured decision trees that an LLM navigates; structured paths still leave interpretation to the model.[18] Eisele-Metzger and Sahu call for purpose-built expert references rather than relying solely on published review labels.[19,20]

The remaining gap concerns the combination of enforced rules, checked evidence records and a reference that can challenge published labels. A rating may follow an algorithm while using mistaken answers; an authentic quotation may be irrelevant to its question. Conversely, disagreement with a published rating may reflect a defensible correction. To our knowledge, the literature reviewed through 19 October 2026 did not combine assessment-time answer and source checks, an independently verifiable audit file, and blinded adjudication of disputed RoB 2 ratings. Whether enforcing rules and checking evidence changes the answers a model gives, and whether stricter model ratings are errors or corrections, remains an open question for blinded adjudication.

## Objective

We asked whether ARBITER could make RoB 2 assessment auditable while preserving judgement quality, compared with the same model given a minimal prompt and identical trial sources. We hypothesised that adjudicators would prefer ARBITER when the arms disagreed, and that differences in elicited answers would contribute beyond rule arithmetic alone.

## Materials and methods

### System design

ARBITER separates evidence interpretation from deterministic checks. The AI model reads trial documents, identifies the result and answers signalling questions; rule-based software stores a content-hashed copy of each source, extracts quoted spans, binds cited evidence to its source and applies the official algorithms (Figure 1). A reviewer approves the proposed result before domain assessment and can inspect the answers and cited evidence afterward. Each required answer must have an evidence basis. Text quotations are server-extracted spans; image regions are rendered and linked to source pages. Model-written image descriptions are not independently checked against image content. The exported HTML report and audit file retain sources, answers, evidence and decisions for independent verification.

ARBITER computes proposed ratings and permits a reasoned, recorded override with its rationale and evidence. Thus, the software guarantees a checked proposal and an auditable route for any departure; it does not make interpretation infallible. We checked proposed and adopted ratings separately. AVATAR illustrates the workflow for all-cause death after early surgery versus watchful waiting in asymptomatic severe aortic stenosis (Figure 2; eTable 3). It was selected to explain the process, rather than represent typical performance. Its stored documents did not establish a dated analysis plan for the assessed result.

### Benchmark and assessment arms

The benchmark comprised trials from published meta-analyses, with source reviews, specialties and label distributions documented in eTables 1–2. Assessment prompts targeted the published review's labelled result using its outcome, time point, contrast and printed result, rather than automatically choosing the trial's primary outcome. The binding audit identified 33 explicit result matches, 11 matches within several covered outcomes, 32 inferred matches, 7 partial matches, 9 different results and 10 unknown bindings. Twelve label sets were trial-wide, seven jointly covered pain and disability, and ten had unknown result binding; these categories were retained rather than relabelled as exact result matches. De Waal's supplied registry record belongs to another trial; its presence does not establish relevant information about the assessed result.

Both arms received identical underlying trial sources: articles, supplements, registry projections, protocols and statistical analysis plans available in the captured input set. Unassisted received copies of the registry projections and acquired documents from the ARBITER run; nothing was fetched anew. Document availability varied by trial. Unassisted was a minimal-prompt condition: its prompt contained the assessment task sentence followed by one fixed output-format sentence, with no RoB 2 guidance. It could read and search local documents, but had no ARBITER service, assessment skill or web access. ARBITER additionally received the assessment skill and its guidance. Input parity therefore concerns trial evidence, not identical instructions or processing.

Both arms used `claude-sonnet-5-5`, medium effort, in Claude Code 2.1.278 with isolated settings, connectors and memory disabled. ARBITER ran on 12 October 2026 and Unassisted on 14 October 2026. The reported software is ARBITER 1.0.0, public commit [2f549ae](https://github.com/AliSalman-et-al/ARBITER/tree/2f549ae30f056ff441ea61a04b719968716f2219). Each arm assessed each trial once. The ARBITER harness approved the proposed result unchanged. The protocol allowed “Continue.” at most twice per phase without coaching. The prompt templates and the Unassisted output-format instruction are reproduced in eMethods, including the ARBITER skill invocation and a filled example. The prompts were distinct. Several prior studies instead used structured RoB 2 prompts, so this comparator does not represent all prior prompting designs.[11–14]

### Reference standards and endpoints

Published review labels supplied the common comparator, but were not assumed to be correct. Two blinded adjudicators assessed every domain cell where either arm differed from its published label. They used no AI. Each rated the domain from the documents and froze that independent rating before seeing any candidate label. They then saw only the three source labels in an order randomised for each cell, chose the best label and supplied a reason. They did not see the arms' rationales or quotations. Gartlehner's blinded adjudication of discordant data-extraction items supplied the design precedent.[21] Adjudicator credentials and preparation require author completion: [Adjudicator qualifications, experience and training].

The primary endpoint was the share of adjudicated disagreement cells where the adjudicators preferred ARBITER's rating. A cell was a trial–domain pair. The primary population required agreement between adjudicators and disagreement between arms. Credit followed label equality, irrespective of the blinded letter selected. Unassisted received credit when its label matched the agreed choice; neither arm received credit when the agreed choice matched neither. Sensitivities used each adjudicator separately and counted all split arm disagreements against ARBITER without assigning them to Unassisted.

Secondary endpoints included agreement with published labels, direction of disagreement, agreement with frozen independent ratings, rule consistency, audit metrics, system runtime and API list-price equivalent. Agreement used linearly weighted Cohen's κ, exact agreement and sensitivity and specificity for Some concerns or High; Gwet's AC2 was supplementary. Overall agreement used trials with an available published Overall label and was not adjudicated. Missing predictions counted as disagreement. The All-Low rule exposed the contribution of label prevalence; its κ was suppressed because predictions had no variance.

Rule checks applied the official RoB 2 algorithms to each arm's own answers, using effect of assignment for the deviations domain.[1] The domains cover randomisation, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement and selection of the reported result. A rating could contradict its answers, or a required question could be skipped, preventing a valid algorithm output. These categories formed any rule break. An absent rating and answer set was unevaluable for rule consistency. Overall checks allowed escalation when multiple domains had Some concerns. Evidence coverage counted required active answers citing at least one passage or image; audit verification assessed structural integrity rather than clinical correctness. System runtime summed wall-clock turn durations. Cost reconstruction used recorded token components and frozen provider rates, with cache and side-call handling in eMethods. Runs used a subscription; dollar figures were API list-price equivalents rather than expenditure.

Error localisation compared losing ratings with the adjudicators' independent signalling answers. The primary analysis required both independent ratings to equal the agreed choice. Yes/probably yes and no/probably no were collapsed; no information and not applicable remained distinct. A question alone changed the rating when replacing its answer with the adjudicators' agreed answer changed the official algorithm output while other answers stayed fixed. Invalid substituted paths were not counted. This counterfactual identifies an answer-sensitive rating, not a causal mechanism of model behaviour.

### Statistics and reporting

We used 10,000 stratified trial-cluster bootstrap resamples, seed 20261018, retaining the number of trials within each meta-analysis and all cells from each sampled trial. Percentile intervals describe 95% uncertainty; paired contrasts used identical resampling weights. No p-values or multiplicity correction were used. The primary endpoint alone was confirmatory under the analysis plan, which was fixed after data collection and before inferential analysis. The presentation and literature framing were subsequently revised.

Post hoc analyses recomputed Unassisted ratings from its own answers, replacing contradictions but retaining invalid paths; stratified the primary endpoint by relative severity; added intervals to the prespecified direction counts; and reported unweighted domain κ for comparison with prior work. Further post hoc analyses estimated paired independent-rating agreement differences, partitioned the supplementary composite reference and described document availability and justifications for analysis-plan-question losses. Original disagreement populations were retained after recomputation, allowing both arms credit when corrected labels tied. Reporting follows TRIPOD-LLM and GRRAS, with an accompanying item map and author-completion fields.[22,23]

## Results

The analysis included 102 trials from 16 meta-analyses, yielding 510 domain cells per arm and 81 trials with published Overall labels (Figure 3; eTable 1). Three unsupported trial designs were excluded from both arms. Adjudicators reviewed 154 disputed domain cells, agreed on a preferred rating in 129 and split in 25. The arms differed in 80 reviewed cells, comprising 62 with an agreed choice and 18 with split decisions.

Both models agreed poorly with published review labels: weighted κ was 0.34 (95% CI 0.26–0.42) for ARBITER and 0.28 (0.21–0.35) for Unassisted (Table 1; Figure 4A). Exact agreement was 386/510 (75.7%) and 395/510 (77.5%), compared with 83.7% for the All-Low rule. Where they disagreed with published labels, they were usually stricter: ARBITER in 88/124 cells and Unassisted in 69/115. ARBITER had greater sensitivity for Some concerns or High and lower specificity. Overall κ was 0.44 (0.32–0.56) and 0.42 (0.27–0.56), respectively; these ratings were not adjudicated.

Adjudicators usually upheld stricter model ratings among cells with an agreed choice (Figure 4B). They agreed with stricter ARBITER ratings in 73/77 cells (94.8%; 95% CI 90.4–98.7) and stricter Unassisted ratings in 48/59 (81.4%; 73.2–89.4). For more lenient ratings, the corresponding counts were 22/28 (78.6%; 63.6–91.7) and 19/38 (50.0%; 34.4–65.8). Split decisions were excluded from these denominators. The counts were prespecified; their intervals were added post hoc.

ARBITER was preferred in most adjudicated cells where the arms disagreed: 54/62 (87.1%; 95% CI 79.0–94.3), compared with 7/62 for Unassisted and 1/62 for neither (Figure 5). ARBITER's share was 60/80 for Adjudicator 1, 64/80 for Adjudicator 2 and 54/80 when all splits counted against ARBITER. In post hoc severity strata, ARBITER was preferred in 44/45 cells where it was stricter than Unassisted and 10/17 where it was more lenient. Before seeing any label, the adjudicators' independent ratings agreed with ARBITER in 107/154 and 120/154 cells, versus 69/154 and 76/154 with Unassisted (Table 2). Post hoc paired differences were 24.7 (16.2–32.9) and 28.6 (19.8–37.4) percentage points. Between adjudicators, weighted κ was 0.59 for independent ratings and 0.69 for preferred ratings.

Unassisted losses often hinged on an individual signalling answer. Among 47 eligible losing ratings, 29 had at least one question that alone changed the rating; the analysis-plan question (5.1) did so in 18 (Figure 6). In the post hoc document description, 13 of these 18 had a supplied registry record, protocol or statistical analysis plan, but none of the Unassisted justifications mentioned such documents (eTable 21). Availability did not establish relevance, including the De Waal mismatch. In the post hoc rules-only recomputation, 3 of the original 62 arm disagreements changed to ARBITER's preferred rating and 59 were unchanged (eTable 23). These corrections created ties: Unassisted label-equality credit rose from 7 to 10, while ARBITER retained 54. Across the benchmark, recomputation reduced Unassisted agreement with published labels from 395 to 385 of 510 and with the agreed choice from 69 to 64 of 129.

Unassisted broke the RoB 2 domain rules in 65/509 evaluable ratings (12.8%; 95% CI 10.0–15.6): 26 contradicted their own answers and 39 skipped a required question (Table 3). One domain lacked both a rating and answers. ARBITER had zero domain rule breaks by design and no recorded overrides in 510 domains; every quotation was found in its content-hashed stored source. All 1538 required active answers cited evidence, all 1407 text-span records were source-linked, and all 14 image regions were page-linked. All 102 audit files passed the independent verifier. Both arms' Overall ratings followed the rules in 102/102 trials, including a permitted ARBITER escalation. These checks establish structural consistency and source occurrence, rather than semantic justification.

ARBITER required more system runtime and a higher API list-price equivalent per trial. Median [IQR] runtime was 7.6 [6.1–10.5] minutes versus 0.5 [0.4–0.5] for Unassisted; API list-price equivalents were US$3.78 [3.46–4.10] versus US$0.09 [0.07–0.13]. Paired mean differences were 11.0 (95% CI 8.4–14.1) minutes and US$3.68 (3.61–3.75) (eTable 28). The runs used a subscription.

## Discussion

Adjudicators preferred ARBITER in most disagreements between the arms, while both models' stricter ratings were usually upheld against published labels. ARBITER coupled these judgements with rule-consistent ratings and quotations found in stored sources, making the assessment route independently checkable. These findings concern the complete system and the selected disputed cells; they do not isolate software, guidance, tool use or additional processing time.

Stricter RoB 2 ratings should not automatically be interpreted as model mistakes. Earlier studies often made that interpretation against published labels.[9–13] Here, both models' stricter ratings commonly matched the adjudicators' agreed choice. Gandhi's masked panel also assigned more severe ratings than the published reviews, pointing in the same direction despite a different evaluation design.[11] The present finding is narrower than a claim that published reviews generally underestimate bias: it concerns disputed cells in this benchmark, with small severity strata and imperfect result matching. The independent ratings, frozen before candidate labels were shown, provide complementary evidence without the subsequent forced choice.

ARBITER combines features that prior systems developed separately (Table 4). RobotReviewer established the link between automated ratings and reviewable text.[8] ROBoto2 computes flowchart ratings and retains original responses and human edits; it is a substantive precursor.[10] Huang and Choi examined algorithm-derived judgements, while Liang added post hoc source-support evaluation.[14–16] Saadi's structured agent-based work concerns ROBINS-I; its description does not establish external tool calling.[17] CPGPrompt contributes the broader informatics pattern of formal guideline paths, although an LLM navigates its trees.[18] ARBITER's contribution is the assessed combination of software-controlled answer acceptance, source-bound quotations, an independently verifiable audit file and disputed-cell adjudication. Several prior matched comparisons also used identical underlying sources; input parity is not an exclusive contribution.

The rules-only analysis suggests that the benefit extended beyond correcting the final calculation. Recomputing Unassisted ratings changed few original arm disagreements and created ties where ARBITER was already preferred. Huang reported benefits from deriving ratings from model answers, and Choi demonstrated the importance of checking conformance.[14,15] Eisele-Metzger also found overall-rule inconsistencies.[19] These observations establish a shared failure mode, but differences in instruments, answer paths, samples and denominators prevent direct comparisons of their rates. In this benchmark, enforcing the rules mattered for consistency, while differences in the elicited answers accounted for most adjudicated separation. The answer differences may reflect the RoB 2 guidance and the workflow together. A factorial comparison would be needed to separate their contributions.

Having a document available does not ensure that a model uses it appropriately. Both arms received the same registry and protocol material, yet the analysis-plan question dominated localised Unassisted losses. The post hoc absence of document mentions in those justifications is consistent with different use of shared inputs, but does not prove that the model ignored or misunderstood them. Some records could be irrelevant or lack a timely plan; De Waal demonstrates that risk directly. Reviewers must check identity, timing and applicability before treating a registry or protocol as evidence of prespecification.

Evaluation conclusions depended strongly on the chosen reference. Against published labels, exact agreement numerically favoured Unassisted; against the supplementary composite reference, the same ratings yielded weighted κ of 0.94 for ARBITER and 0.69 for Unassisted (eTables 11–14). The post hoc partition shows why absolute values need caution: both arms matched all 356 cells that retained published labels, while agreement on the 129 agreed-choice cells was 116 for ARBITER and 69 for Unassisted. The composite excludes split decisions and assumes unaudited agreed cells are correct. It is optimistic in absolute terms, and the direction of bias in the paired difference is not guaranteed. Evaluations should report published-label agreement alongside an independent reference, rule conformance and evidence checks. More defensible risk-of-bias ratings could affect evidence certainty, but effects on certainty judgements or clinical recommendations were not measured.

Deployment requires accounting for human review as well as computing. The API list-price equivalent provides a reproducible computational reference; the study's subscription expenditure cannot be allocated from these token counts. In the authors' experience, flat-rate plans can carry usage whose API list-price equivalent far exceeds the subscription price; that anecdote is plan-dependent and is not an economic evaluation. Human checking time was not measured. System runtime is not staff time, and comparison with published human assessment times would mix denominators and tasks. ARBITER should serve as an auditable assistant. Prospective use should measure checking time, corrections and automation bias, since a detailed audit file may encourage unwarranted acceptance as well as scrutiny.

The design supports a system-level claim and cannot separate the contributions of guidance, software and additional processing time. No structured-prompt comparator was tested; because ARBITER's assessment skill carries RoB 2 guidance, the contribution of that guidance cannot be separated from that of the software. Each arm ran once, so run-to-run variability is unknown. Trial reports or source reviews may have appeared in training; publication dates cannot establish exposure.

The reference and measurement design limits generalisation beyond disputed cells. Selecting cells on disagreement invites the discrepant-analysis critique, with unquantified bias of indeterminate direction in the preference share. Adjudicators could choose only among offered labels, although their earlier independent ratings avoided that restriction. They may have recognised an arm from labels alone; this risk was low because rationales and quotations were hidden. At the authors' decision, cells where both arms matched the published label were not audited, paralleling the limitation of discordant-only adjudication noted by Gartlehner.[21] Some published labels were broad, inferred or mismatched to the assessed result, so disagreements may partly reflect outcome mismatch rather than a different judgement. Strictness strata were small, and Overall was not adjudicated. Whether quotations support the answers was not assessed, and model-written image descriptions were not verified semantically. Structural verification therefore cannot establish clinical correctness, and these results cannot estimate performance over all benchmark cells or other appraisal tools.

### Conclusion

ARBITER made RoB 2 assessment auditable through checked source records and algorithm-proposed ratings, with adjudicators usually preferring its ratings when the arms disagreed. The findings support further evaluation of this assistant with independent reference ratings and measured human checking, while retaining human responsibility for interpretation.
