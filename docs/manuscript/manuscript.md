## Abstract

### Objective

Test whether an auditable RoB 2 workflow yields ratings closer to blinded expert assessment than an unassisted model or published labels.

### Materials and Methods

ARBITER and Unassisted assessed identical trial documents. Two blinded experts independently rated all 102 trials × five domains, then chose among three randomised, blinded labels. The prespecified primary endpoint was the share of arm-discordant cells in which both experts chose ARBITER's label; agreement with the experts' independent ratings was defined after data collection.

### Results

Experts preferred ARBITER in 53/60 agreed choices (88.3%; 95% CI 79.7–96.3). Across 510 cells, agreement for ARBITER, Unassisted and published labels was 72.7%, 65.1% and 59.0% for Adjudicator 1, and 84.9%, 78.4% and 71.0% for Adjudicator 2. Paired ARBITER − Unassisted differences were 7.6 percentage points (95% CI 5.1–10.2) and 6.5 (3.5–9.4), respectively. Stricter ARBITER ratings matched experts in 64/88 and 65/88 cells. Unassisted broke rules in 65/509 evaluable domains; every ARBITER quotation (1407/1407) was found in its content-hashed source. Published-label agreement was poor (weighted κ 0.34 versus 0.28). Median runtime was 7.6 versus 0.5 minutes/trial; API list-price equivalents were US$3.78 versus US$0.09.

### Discussion

Agreement with published labels, the usual benchmark, hid ARBITER's closer agreement with blinded experts, whose independent ratings usually matched its stricter ratings. Applying the rules to the unassisted model's own answers did not close the gap. Two experts, and their access to extra sources, limit interpretation.

### Conclusion

ARBITER paired checkable assessment records with ratings closer to blinded experts across the full assessment set. It warrants evaluation as an assistant with measured human checking.

## Background and significance

AI judgements used in evidence synthesis must be checkable because they can affect how much confidence clinicians place in research. The Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials, RoB 2, structures assessment of a specific trial result through signalling questions, domain algorithms and an Overall judgement.[1] Its judgements inform assessments of evidence certainty, yet applying it is demanding. Minozzi and colleagues found low agreement even among experienced assessors; implementation instructions improved reliability but required substantial preparation.[2,3] Published reviews also disagree when rating the same trials, as Bertizzolo and colleagues and Yao and colleagues documented for the earlier instrument.[4,5] These findings caution against treating published ratings as an unquestioned reference. Evidence-synthesis organisations require human oversight and transparent reporting when AI makes or suggests judgements.[6,7]

Automated risk-of-bias assessment has a lineage of linking ratings to text. RobotReviewer paired predictions with extracted sentences and opportunities for human correction.[8] More recent RoB 2 studies report modest agreement with published labels and a tendency toward stricter ratings. Nyrhi, Hevia and colleagues' ROBoto2, Gandhi, Taneri and Lai describe this pattern, often interpreting strictness as model error.[9–13] That interpretation depends on the reference being dependable. The pattern is specific to the RoB 2 literature considered here; it should not be generalised to other appraisal instruments.

Existing systems address parts of the problem. Huang derived ratings from model answers in a spreadsheet, Choi recomputed ratings with coded algorithms, and ROBoto2 integrated flowchart aggregation with retrieval and human feedback.[10,14,15] Liang checked generated claims against retrieved passages after assessment.[16] Saadi evaluated structured agent-based implementations for ROBINS-I, although external tool use was not established.[17] In clinical informatics, CPGPrompt translates guidelines into structured decision trees that an LLM navigates; structured paths still leave interpretation to the model.[18] Eisele-Metzger and Sahu call for purpose-built expert references rather than relying solely on published review labels.[19,20]

The remaining gap concerns whether enforced rules and checked evidence improve the judgements themselves. An algorithm can faithfully transform mistaken answers, and a quotation can exist without supporting an answer. An LLM workflow in which rules and evidence checks are the only route to a rating might give answers closer to expert judgement than an unassisted model or published labels. To our knowledge, this has not been tested against a blinded expert reference covering every RoB 2 domain, distinct from published labels.

## Objective

We asked whether ARBITER ratings were closer to blinded expert assessment than those of the same model given a minimal prompt and identical trial sources, or the published review labels. The prespecified hypothesis was that adjudicators would prefer ARBITER when the arms disagreed. The independent-rating comparison across all cells was defined after data collection.

## Materials and methods

### System design

ARBITER separates evidence interpretation from deterministic checks. The AI model reads trial documents, identifies the result and answers signalling questions; rule-based software stores a content-hashed copy of each source, extracts quoted spans, binds cited evidence to its source and applies the official algorithms (Figure 1). A reviewer approves the proposed result before domain assessment and can inspect the answers and cited evidence afterward. Each required answer must have an evidence basis. Text quotations are server-extracted spans; image regions are rendered and linked to source pages. Model-written image descriptions are not independently checked against image content. The exported HTML report and audit file retain sources, answers, evidence and decisions for independent verification.

ARBITER computes proposed ratings and permits a reasoned, recorded override with its rationale and evidence. Thus, the software guarantees a checked proposal and an auditable route for any departure; it does not make interpretation infallible. We checked proposed and adopted ratings separately. AVATAR illustrates the workflow for all-cause death after early surgery versus watchful waiting in asymptomatic severe aortic stenosis (Figure 2; complete AVATAR trace in the supplement). It was selected to explain the process, rather than represent typical performance. Its stored documents did not establish a dated analysis plan for the assessed result.

### Benchmark and assessment arms

The benchmark comprised trials from published meta-analyses, with source reviews, specialties and label distributions documented in the supplementary benchmark table. Assessment prompts targeted the published review's labelled result using its outcome, time point, contrast and printed result, rather than automatically choosing the trial's primary outcome. The binding audit identified 33 explicit result matches, 11 matches within several covered outcomes, 32 inferred matches, 7 partial matches, 9 different results and 10 unknown bindings. Twelve label sets were trial-wide, seven jointly covered pain and disability, and ten had unknown result binding; these categories were retained rather than relabelled as exact result matches. De Waal's supplied registry record belongs to another trial; its presence does not establish relevant information about the assessed result.

Both arms received identical underlying trial sources: articles, supplements, registry projections, protocols and statistical analysis plans available in the captured input set. Unassisted received copies of the registry projections and acquired documents from the ARBITER run; nothing was fetched anew. Document availability varied by trial. Unassisted was a minimal-prompt condition: its prompt contained the assessment task sentence followed by one fixed output-format sentence, with no RoB 2 guidance. It could read and search local documents, but had no ARBITER service, assessment skill or web access. ARBITER additionally received the assessment skill and its guidance. Input parity therefore concerns trial evidence, not identical instructions or processing.

Both arms used `claude-sonnet-5-5`, medium effort, in Claude Code 2.1.278 with isolated settings, connectors and memory disabled. ARBITER ran on 12 October 2026 and Unassisted on 14 October 2026. The reported software is ARBITER 1.0.0, public commit [2f549ae](https://github.com/AliSalman-et-al/ARBITER/tree/2f549ae30f056ff441ea61a04b719968716f2219). Each arm assessed each trial once. The ARBITER harness approved the proposed result unchanged. The protocol allowed “Continue.” at most twice per phase without coaching. The prompt templates and the Unassisted output-format instruction are reproduced in eMethods, including the ARBITER skill invocation and a filled example. The prompts were distinct. Several prior studies instead used structured RoB 2 prompts, so this comparator does not represent all prior prompting designs.[11–14]

### Reference standard and endpoints

Two blinded human experts assessed all 102 trials × D1–D5 using a structured form, with no AI used for any judgement. For each cell, each expert recorded signalling answers and a domain rating, then froze both as the independent rating. The expert then saw three blinded labels—published, Unassisted and ARBITER—in an order randomised for each cell, without rationales, and made a forced choice of the most defensible label. Each subsequently gave a revised rating, which could differ from all offered labels, and recorded answer retention or change. Experts could consult sources beyond the arms' inputs. Gartlehner's blinded adjudication of discordant data-extraction items supplied the design precedent.[21] [Adjudicator qualifications, experience and training].

Among cells where the arms disagreed and both adjudicators made the same forced choice, the primary endpoint was the share whose chosen label matched ARBITER. A cell was a trial–domain pair. Credit followed label equality, irrespective of the blinded letter selected: every source matching the choice received credit; neither arm received credit if neither label matched. Cells where all three labels matched provided no preference information. Key secondary endpoints were exact agreement and linearly weighted Cohen's κ against each expert's independent rating across all 510 cells, and against derived Overall. Published labels were scored as a third source. Confusion matrices, per-domain estimates, unweighted κ and paired contrasts accompany the main comparison (eTables 1–2).

Forced choices across all disputed cells, where either arm differed from the published label, were descriptive (eTable 3). Revised ratings and retained or changed answers were also descriptive because viewing labels could anchor subsequent responses (eTable 4). Signalling-answer comparisons used only explicit answers matched by trial, domain, question and component. Five-level matching distinguished yes, probably yes, no, probably no and no information; collapsed matching combined the two affirmative and two negative levels, leaving no information separate. Missing, inactive, not applicable, unmatched and unresolved answers were excluded; paired contrasts required evaluable answers in both arms (eTable 5).

Post hoc rules-only recomputation replaced Unassisted ratings contradicting their own answers with the official algorithm output, retaining invalid paths and missing ratings. Original disagreement populations stayed fixed; recomputation could create ties crediting both arms (eTable 6). The agreed-reference sensitivity retained cells where both independent ratings matched (eTable 7). Expert-to-expert agreement described reference reliability, rather than a mathematical upper bound (eTable 8). Directional analyses partitioned each arm's stricter or more lenient ratings relative to published labels into independent expert matches to the arm, the published label or neither (eTable 9). Primary sensitivities used each expert on all arm disagreements and counted split choices as ARBITER losses without assigning Unassisted wins. Relative-severity strata were post hoc (eTable 10).

Benchmark composition and label-to-result binding are documented in eTable 11. Agreement with published labels used weighted κ, exact agreement, sensitivity for Some concerns or High and specificity, with Gwet's AC2 and paired differences supplementary (eTable 12). Unweighted κ was also reported (eTable 13). The All-Low rule exposed label prevalence; κ was suppressed because its predictions had no variance. Missing predictions counted as disagreement. These published-label analyses were retained unchanged.

Rule checks applied the official RoB 2 algorithms to each arm's own answers, using effect of assignment for the deviations domain.[1] The domains cover randomisation, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement and selection of the reported result. A rating could contradict its answers, or a required question could be skipped, preventing a valid algorithm output; together these formed any rule break (eTable 14). An absent rating and answer set was unevaluable. A sensitivity resolved invalid required answers recursively as negative or no information (eTable 15). Overall checks allowed escalation when multiple domains had Some concerns.

System runtime summed wall-clock turn durations (eTable 16). Cost reconstruction used recorded token components and frozen provider rates, with cache and side-call handling in eMethods; reconstruction bounds and token reconciliation are in eTables 17–18. Runs used a subscription; dollar figures were API list-price equivalents rather than expenditure. Evidence coverage counted required active answers citing at least one passage or image. Quotation and bundle audits checked source binding and structural integrity rather than whether the cited evidence justified the answer (eTable 19).

Derived Overall used the official rule on each expert's independent domains, taking the worst domain without imposing optional escalation for multiple Some concerns. Validation against the supplied Overall worksheet is reported in eTable 20. Main-table Overall comparisons used the 81 trials with a published Overall label; the other 21 were excluded rather than counted as disagreements. Additional model and expert comparisons used all 102 trials, with denominators stated separately.

## Results

All 102 trials from 16 meta-analyses received two independent expert assessments of all 510 domain cells (Figure 3). Adjudicators preferred ARBITER in 53/60 cells where the arms disagreed and they made the same forced choice (88.3%; 95% CI 79.7–96.3), versus 7/60 for Unassisted (Figure 4). Three unsupported designs were excluded. Of 154 disputed cells, 80 differed between arms; the adjudicators split on 20 of these. ARBITER received 63/80 choices from Adjudicator 1 and 61/80 from Adjudicator 2; counting splits as losses gave 53/80. In post hoc strata, ARBITER was preferred in 43/44 cells where it was stricter than Unassisted and 10/16 where it was more lenient (eTables 3,10).

Independent-rating agreement favoured ARBITER over both other sources across the full domain population (Table 1; Figure 5A). Adjudicator 1 agreed with ARBITER on 371/510 cells (72.7%; weighted κ 0.51), Unassisted on 332/510 (65.1%; κ 0.35) and published labels on 301/510 (59.0%; κ 0.21). The paired ARBITER minus Unassisted difference was 7.6 percentage points (95% CI 5.1–10.2). Adjudicator 2 agreed on 433/510 (84.9%; κ 0.69), 400/510 (78.4%; κ 0.53) and 362/510 (71.0%; κ 0.29), respectively; the paired difference was 6.5 points (3.5–9.4). On the 81 trials with published Overall, ARBITER minus Unassisted agreement with derived Overall was 13.6 points (6.2–21.0) for Adjudicator 1 and 17.3 (8.6–25.9) for Adjudicator 2. Full contrasts, including comparisons with published labels, and the agreed-reference sensitivity are in eTables 1–2,7; revised ratings remain descriptive (eTable 4).

Stricter ARBITER ratings usually matched each independent expert: 64/88 cells for Adjudicator 1 and 65/88 for Adjudicator 2, compared with 5/88 and 14/88 matches to published labels (Figure 5B). Stricter Unassisted ratings matched the experts in 46/69 and 43/69 cells. For more lenient ratings, expert matches were 22/36 and 26/36 for ARBITER, and 19/46 and 26/46 for Unassisted. These denominators retain all directional disagreements, including split forced choices (eTable 9).

Unassisted broke domain rules in 65/509 evaluable ratings (12.8%; 95% CI 10.0–15.6): 26 contradicted their own answers and 39 skipped a required question (Table 2). One domain lacked both a rating and answers. ARBITER had zero domain rule breaks by design and no recorded overrides in 510 domains. All 1538 required active answers cited evidence; all 1407/1407 text records linked quotations found in content-hashed stored sources, and all 14 image regions were page-linked. All 102 audit files passed the independent verifier. Both arms' Overall ratings followed the rules in 102/102 trials, including a permitted ARBITER escalation (eTables 14–15,19).

Agreement with published review labels was poor and similar between arms: weighted κ was 0.34 (95% CI 0.26–0.42) for ARBITER and 0.28 (0.21–0.35) for Unassisted (Table 3). Exact agreement was 386/510 (75.7%) and 395/510 (77.5%), compared with 83.7% for the All-Low rule. ARBITER had greater sensitivity for Some concerns or High and lower specificity. Overall κ was 0.44 (0.32–0.56) and 0.42 (0.27–0.56), respectively (eTables 12–13). Review-specific estimates varied (eFigure 1).

Median [IQR] system runtime was 7.6 [6.1–10.5] minutes per trial for ARBITER versus 0.5 [0.4–0.5] for Unassisted; API list-price equivalents were US$3.78 [3.46–4.10] versus US$0.09 [0.07–0.13]. Paired mean differences were 11.0 (95% CI 8.4–14.1) minutes and US$3.68 (3.61–3.75) (eTable 16). Cost reconstruction and token reconciliation are in eTables 17–18.

Post hoc rules-only recomputation changed 26 Unassisted domain ratings, including 6 original arm disagreements. In the fixed 60-cell primary population, Unassisted matched the agreed choice in 7 cells before recomputation and 8 afterwards, while ARBITER retained 53; new ties could credit both arms. Independent-rating agreement fell from 332/510 to 324/510 for Adjudicator 1 and from 400/510 to 391/510 for Adjudicator 2 (eTable 6).

Collapsed signalling-answer agreement was 80.4% for ARBITER versus 74.9% for Unassisted against Adjudicator 1, and 90.9% versus 87.4% against Adjudicator 2. On paired answers, differences were 5.0 percentage points (95% CI 3.6–6.4) and 3.3 (1.9–4.7), respectively (Figure 6; eTable 5). Domain-level agreement and all question-level contrasts, including sparse questions and five-level matching, are in eFigures 2–3. For Adjudicator 1, the largest collapsed domain gap was D5: 11.3 points (95% CI 8.2–14.6). For Adjudicator 2, it was D3: 7.9 (2.8–13.6); the D5 gap was 3.7 (−0.3 to 7.4). Within-direction choices (yes versus probably yes or no versus probably no) accounted for about half of five-level disagreements against Adjudicator 1 and about three-quarters against Adjudicator 2; cross-direction counts included no information versus either direction (eTable 5).

The experts independently agreed on 388/510 domain ratings (76.1%; weighted κ 0.59); agreement was lowest in D3, at 54/102 (eTable 8). All independent derived Overall ratings matched the supplied worksheet; discrepancies in other columns are documented separately (eTable 20).

## Discussion

ARBITER combined an independently checkable assessment route with closer agreement to two blinded experts across the complete set of domain cells, and the experts usually chose its rating when the arms disagreed. All observed ARBITER ratings followed the RoB 2 rules, and every cited quotation was found in its stored source. Recomputing Unassisted ratings from its own answers did not recover the agreement gain, pointing to the answers elicited by the complete workflow rather than arithmetic alone.

Stricter RoB 2 ratings should not automatically be read as model mistakes. Earlier studies often interpreted strictness against published labels in that way.[9–13] Here, stricter ratings usually matched each expert's independent rating, which was frozen before labels were shown. Gandhi's masked panel also assigned more severe ratings than the published reviews, pointing in the same direction despite a different design.[11] This does not establish that published reviews generally underestimate bias: the finding concerns this benchmark and its result-matching limitations.

For pooled domain agreement, published labels matched the independent experts less often than either model. Exact agreement against published labels numerically favoured Unassisted, whereas both experts agreed more often with ARBITER across all domains. Thus, published-label agreement alone gave a misleading account of comparative performance in this dataset. Two experts cannot establish a universal reference, and their access to additional sources may partly explain disagreements with the published labels and model arms. Independent expert assessment, published-label agreement, rule conformance and evidence checks answer complementary questions. Effects on certainty judgements or clinical recommendations were not measured.

The answer comparisons suggest a contribution beyond final rule calculation. Rules-only recomputation affected few original arm disagreements and reduced agreement with both experts; collapsed signalling answers also matched the experts more often with ARBITER. Huang reported benefits from deriving ratings from model answers, while Choi demonstrated the importance of checking algorithm conformance.[14,15] Eisele-Metzger found Overall-rule inconsistencies.[19] These studies identify a shared failure mode, but instruments, paths and denominators differ. Our findings distinguish consistency from agreement: enforcing arithmetic prevented rule breaks, while the assessment workflow and RoB 2 guidance together may have improved the elicited answers. Their individual contributions require a factorial comparison.

ARBITER combines features that prior systems developed separately (Table 4). RobotReviewer established the link between automated ratings and reviewable text.[8] ROBoto2 computes flowchart ratings and retains original responses and human edits; it is a substantive precursor.[10] Huang and Choi examined algorithm-derived judgements, while Liang added post hoc source-support evaluation.[14–16] Saadi's structured agent-based work concerns ROBINS-I; its description does not establish external tool calling.[17] CPGPrompt contributes the broader informatics pattern of formal guideline paths, although an LLM navigates its trees.[18] The present evaluation combines software-controlled answer acceptance, source-bound quotations, an independently verifiable audit file and blinded dual expert assessment of all domains. Several prior matched comparisons also used identical underlying sources; input parity is not an exclusive contribution. Evidence and quotation locations for the comparison are in eTable 21.

Modest agreement between experts bounds the interpretation of every agreement estimate. Their independent domain agreement was lowest for missing outcome data, so differences from either expert can reflect legitimate judgement variation as well as model limitations. Expert-to-expert agreement provides a reference line, not a maximum any model can achieve against an individual expert. The agreed-reference sensitivity describes the subset with less reference uncertainty, rather than replacing the complete independent comparisons.

Deployment requires accounting for human review as well as computing. The API list-price equivalent provides a reproducible computational reference; the study's subscription expenditure cannot be allocated from these token counts. In the authors' experience, flat-rate plans can carry usage whose API list-price equivalent far exceeds the subscription price; that anecdote is plan-dependent and is not an economic evaluation. Human checking time was not measured. System runtime is not staff time, and comparison with published human assessment times would mix denominators and tasks. ARBITER should serve as an auditable assistant. Prospective use should measure checking time, corrections and automation bias, since a detailed audit file may encourage unwarranted acceptance as well as scrutiny.

The design supports a system-level claim and cannot separate the contributions of guidance, software and additional processing time. No structured-prompt comparator was tested; ARBITER's assessment skill carries RoB 2 guidance. Each arm ran once, so run-to-run variability is unknown. Trial reports or source reviews may have appeared in training; publication dates cannot establish exposure.

The reference and measurement design also limits generalisation. Only two experts contributed, with modest agreement, especially in D3. The experts could consult sources beyond those supplied to the arms. Forced choice offered only three labels and may have anchored responses; the earlier independent ratings avoided that restriction. Overall was derived from domain ratings rather than rated directly. Some published labels were broad, inferred or mismatched to the assessed result, so disagreements may partly reflect outcome mismatch. De Waal's supplied registry record belonged to another trial, illustrating the need to check document identity and applicability. Strictness strata were small. Whether quotations supported the answers was not assessed, and model-written image descriptions were not verified semantically. Structural verification cannot establish clinical validity or performance on other appraisal instruments.

### Conclusion

ARBITER produced auditable RoB 2 assessments that agreed more often with blinded experts across the full domain population than an unassisted model or published labels. Its advantage extended beyond rule arithmetic, supporting prospective evaluation as an assistant with measured human checking and retained human responsibility.
