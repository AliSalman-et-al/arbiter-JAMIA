## Consequential findings

### RoB 2 human disagreement is about interpretation, not just inconsistent form filling

Minozzi et al. (2020) directly studied RoB 2. Four experienced raters independently assessed 70 outcomes. Overall agreement was slight (Fleiss κ 0.16); domain agreement ranged from slight to moderate. Authors identified difficulties in subject-matter knowledge, new terminology, domain-specific changes from the prior tool, and signalling-question wording. This is a small, single-topic study, so it supports the restrained statement that experienced assessors can disagree and report application difficulty, not that all RoB 2 applications are unreliable.

Minozzi et al. (2022) studied a different, specific intervention: four raters assessed outcomes from a Cochrane multiple-sclerosis review, calibrated on five studies, then adopted a condition- and intervention-specific implementation document. Overall agreement was −0.15 before that document, 0.11 for its first five-study phase, and 0.42 for the remaining 11 studies; mean assessment time fell from 168.5 to 41 minutes per study after calibration/document development. This is evidence that added implementation guidance can matter in a particular workflow, not an estimate of the effect of generic RoB 2 prompts or ARBITER.

The 2026 Xiang et al. study adds timely evidence on experience. After training, 10 less-experienced and six experienced raters assessed 94 results from 37 trials. Less-experienced raters had higher agreement on several comparisons but assigned more Low-risk judgments across most domains. The study's own interpretation is that agreement alone may reflect shared interpretation rather than validity. The participants were defined by risk-of-bias training/use, not software expertise; do not describe it as a study of clinicians' software literacy. Its specialty, languages, sample, and small rater groups limit generalization.

The same-trial Cochrane comparison studies cited in the draft, Bertizzolo et al. (2019) and Yao et al. (2024), concern the **earlier Cochrane RoB 1 tool**, not RoB 2. Bertizzolo et al. compared repeated assessments of the same 1,604 trials across reviews and found that disagreement most often reflected different interpretations of the same information (65%–90% across items in the examined disagreements); incomplete or unclear trial reporting commonly contributed. This is useful context for persistent judgment ambiguity, but must be labeled RoB 1. Do not use it as evidence that the RoB 2 algorithms or its inter-rater reliability have a particular property.

### Evidence links help reviewers find material; they do not certify that it supports a judgment

Armijo-Olivo et al. (2020) directly evaluated RobotReviewer against human RoB assessments in 372 trials using RoB 1. They reported high proportions of irrelevant supporting quotations in several domains (e.g., blinding and allocation concealment). Although the abstract's domain-specific findings merit checking against the full article before detailed quotation, this is a much closer task precedent than general generative-search verifiability for the distinction between a quotation being present and its being relevant to the assessment.

Liang et al. (2026) is a newer, directly relevant RoB 2 study. It evaluated three models on 97 trials and used BM25 retrieval plus an LLM verification step to classify generated claims as supported, contradicted, not found, or out of scope. Its abstract reports 60%–65% evidence support even alongside much higher binary label accuracy. This supports evaluating documentary support separately from rating agreement and from source-location integrity. The verifier was itself an LLM-based method, not independent human validation; cite it as an empirical measurement approach, not as ground truth for ARBITER or a cross-study benchmark.

ROBOTO2 (Hevia et al., 2025) is a close RoB 2 precedent: a public interactive interface combines document parsing, retrieval, model suggestions, and reviewer correction; the paper releases an annotated pediatric-trial dataset. Huang et al. (2025) evaluated structured RoB 2 prompts and human comparisons, while later work including Lai et al. and the 2026 RoB2-Evaluator preprint addresses answer derivation/rule-constrained prompts. These precedents rule out any broad claim that ARBITER is the first inspectable, interactive, evidence-linked, structured, or rule-constrained RoB 2 workflow. Position its contribution as this available implementation's retained source-to-answer-to-decision record plus the reported paired, same-model minimal-prompt evaluation.

### Workflow utility and automation bias need human-use studies

Jardim et al. (2022) studied RobotReviewer in two ongoing reviews using the **RoB 1** tool and six evidence-synthesis researchers. Reviewers valued snippets as cues for where to inspect trial text, but checked the original article when snippets were insufficient; some newer reviewers described pedagogical value. They preferred retaining their existing two-reviewer discussion. Crucially, the projects chose between two RobotReviewer workflows, so the study could not compare time with manual-only review. This supports a practical design rationale—make checking easy and preserve reviewer's role—without claiming time savings or substituting for a human workflow evaluation.

Li et al. (2026) examined three human–LLM collaboration modes in RoB 1 with archived Cochrane labels and sampled tasks. It is a human-involved experiment, but it does not evaluate ARBITER or RoB 2 and should not be represented as prospective clinical utility. Cite only to establish that human-in-the-loop design is an active, empirically studied question distinct from offline model-to-reference agreement.

Jabbour et al. (2023, JAMA) randomized clinicians to clinical vignettes with standard or systematically biased diagnostic AI, with or without image-based explanations. It found that biased recommendations harmed clinician performance and explanations did not remove that effect. This is credible primary evidence about clinician interaction with diagnostic AI, but the task, interface, error type, and outcome differ from evidence synthesis and RoB 2. It is not evidence that ARBITER causes automation bias, nor that showing a source quote fails in this particular workflow. Use it only as adjacent rationale for evaluating human checking in future; do not import its effect estimate or assert transfer to ARBITER.

### Fit to the current manuscript

The benchmark does not measure physician usability, reviewer time, checking behavior, adoption, or automation bias. The computation runtime and list-price equivalents must remain clearly separate from human workload and clinical utility. A physician-facing explanation can make the assessed result, source record, answer, algorithmic transition, and override legible; the manuscript should state that this makes inspection possible, not that inspection occurred or was clinically successful in users.

## Limitations and access notes

- Literature was targeted to the requested claims and primary papers; it was not exhaustive or registered as a systematic review.
- Paywalled Minozzi papers and Armijo-Olivo were inspected through abstracts/indexed excerpts and citation trails, not their complete article text. Their limitations are flagged in the ledger.
- Xiang et al.'s publisher-hosted full text was readable in-browser (12-page article-in-press PDF; Abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion/conclusion), but could not be written to `/tmp` because the publisher's download endpoint returned an anti-bot page to the local downloader. The source remains linked in the ledger.
- Evidence-support approaches differ. Liang used an automated retrieval-plus-LLM verification pipeline; RobotReviewer quote relevance was reviewed in a RoB 1 evaluation. Neither demonstrates that ARBITER's specific answers are semantically supported.
- Utility evidence is limited and tool-version-specific. Jardim's real-time study had six reviewers and no manual-only comparison; Li et al. studied RoB 1; Jabbour studied clinical diagnosis. None justifies a claim about ARBITER user benefit or automation bias.
- No cross-study percentage ranking is appropriate: tools, RoB versions, populations, trial sources, reference standards, and human involvement differ.
