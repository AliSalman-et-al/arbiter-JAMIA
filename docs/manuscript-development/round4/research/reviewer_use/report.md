# Human use of AI-assisted evidence-synthesis tools

**Disposition: ISSUES.** The evidence supports a focused future evaluation of reviewer use, correction behavior, semantic evidence support, workload and uptake. It does not support an inference that ARBITER improves reviewers' judgments or saves review time. Direct human-use evidence is concentrated in older RoB 1 RobotReviewer studies; only the system-output and evidence-support studies address current RoB 2/LLM workflows, and those do not establish benefit in actual reviews.

## Evidence synthesis

### What has been tested in actual reviewer use

**Soboczenski et al. (2019), a prospective randomized within-participant task study, is the clearest controlled human-use precedent.** Forty-one volunteers assessed four randomly selected RCT papers using the earlier Cochrane RoB tool: two assessments with RobotReviewer suggestions and two manually, with order randomized. Semi-automation took less time in this task (model-estimated relative time 0.75, 95% CI 0.62–0.92; mean 755 vs 824 seconds). Reviewers changed 27/328 (9%) machine-suggested domain judgments and edited or removed the suggested text more often: 126/328 (38%) underwent partial/full text changes. The authors interpreted the pattern as active checking, but the study did not capture why judgment corrections were made or test semantic correctness of the final reviewer decisions. The final user-edited annotations were the comparator for text precision/recall, not an independent semantic gold standard. The sample was self-selected and the authors cautioned that acceptability may be optimistic. This study is evidence that interaction, time and corrections can be measured prospectively; it cannot estimate ARBITER's effects, given different model generation, instrument, interface and task.

Its important counterevidence to a blanket “reviewers rubber-stamp AI” statement is that 91% of suggested labels were left unchanged while users edited many evidence highlights and spent substantial time. Yet unchanged labels do not prove that reviewers independently verified them; neither unchanged nor changed is an accuracy outcome. The paper also records participant concerns that suggested passages could distract from reading the full report, and that some suggestions were irrelevant or placed under the wrong domain. This is a concrete motivation to measure verification behavior and semantic support directly rather than infer either from acceptance rates.

**Arno et al. (2022) tested integration in ongoing reviews with a parallel randomized noninferiority design.** Among 15 recruited review teams, seven completed the trial (145 studies). Individual reviewers in the intervention arm received prepopulated RobotReviewer assessments; controls received blank forms. The reported overall RoB assessment accuracy was noninferior under the prespecified design (88.8% vs 90.2%; risk difference −0.014, 95% CI −0.093 to 0.065). Person-time was inconclusive (estimated 1.40 minutes saved, CI −5.20 to 2.41). This supplies the closest precedent for assessment within real systematic-review workflows and cautions against claiming time savings from the 2019 task study as a general property. It used the earlier Cochrane tool, a different workflow and an external accuracy reference; it is not a comparator for ARBITER's offline agreement endpoints. The local full article could not be retrieved (ACP publisher request 403); the full PubMed abstract and King's College London institutional record were inspected, and are explicit about design, sample and abstract-level results.

**Jardim et al. (2022) supplies counterevidence on adoption and reviewer preferences, not a manual-control estimate of utility.** In an embedded real-time study of 26 RCTs and six reviewers across two projects, reviewers corrected RobotReviewer assessments and each other's assessments at similar rates under the study's defined comparison (83% of tool assessments and 81% of peer assessments were accepted; RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.92–1.13). The denominator is individual question assessments; “accepted” collapses high/unclear tool outputs into the corresponding human category. There was no manual-only arm, so the authors explicitly could not compare time. Despite seeing the accuracy results, participants generally did not want to replace the usual two-human-reviewer procedure; less experienced reviewers described more flexibility/benefit, while experienced reviewers emphasized human discussion and did not regard the system as a trusted stand-alone assessor. Small sample and local institutional setting limit transfer. The result directly supports measuring acceptability, intended workflow role and perceived value separately from accuracy and time.

These three evaluations do not form a performance ranking. They differ in setting, unit, reference construction, interface, task, instrument, and what “accuracy” or “acceptance” means. Their common contribution is methodological: offline model agreement cannot answer whether reviewers notice, correct, or are influenced by errors, whether review time changes, or whether teams adopt a proposed workflow.

### Extraction, appraisal, support, and conformance are distinct constructs

RobotReviewer (Marshall et al., 2016) evaluated automated RoB 1 judgments and extracted supporting sentences; it reported judgment agreement and supporting-text relevance as distinct tasks. In the human-use literature, Soboczenski's final user-edited highlights do not validate semantic support: a reviewer can keep or delete a passage for reasons not recorded, and “relevance to final edits” is not equivalent to whether the passage justifies the domain answer. The submitted manuscript itself correctly states that its structural checks establish occurrence/location and algorithmic consistency but not semantic evidence support.

ROBOTO2 demonstrates an interactive LLM-assisted RoB 2 tool with user review/editing, but its authors say impact on review quality or efficiency was not explicitly measured. Liang et al. (2026) is a closer recent RoB 2 evidence-support evaluation: generated claims/answers are checked against retrieved source passages, but the support judgments are themselves LLM-assigned. It therefore broadens evaluation beyond agreement while still not supplying human-validated semantic support or an in-review user effect. The current source reports DOI 10.2196/93761; no local file was produced because direct retrieval returned an empty response and publisher text extraction failed. The existing round4 systems ledger records that indexed methods/results/discussion were inspected; I treat claims about this paper as secondary evidence in this slice, and do not make it the lead exemplar.

Thus, four outcome families must remain explicit: (1) extraction/retrieval quality (whether the relevant source content was found); (2) appraisal quality (whether answers/judgments are defensible against an appropriately constructed reference); (3) evidence support (whether each answer is entailed or justified by its cited evidence); and (4) procedural conformance (whether the declared rule mapping was followed). Human-use evaluations add separate observed outcomes: reviewer corrections, time/workload, usability, and adoption. One cannot substitute for another. In particular, source links, citations, high agreement, or low correction frequency do not demonstrate semantic support; rule conformance does not validate inputs; and self-reported usefulness is not an observed workload or accuracy benefit.

### Automation bias: a live question, not a finding about these RoB systems

The Soboczenski report documents concern that highlighted snippets may narrow reviewers' attention, alongside observed text editing and a 9% label-change rate. Jardim documents resistance to changing the established double-reviewer process. Neither establishes automation bias as an observed harmful effect: there was no design isolating a suggestion-induced departure from independent judgment, and a change/acceptance rate alone cannot discriminate justified reliance, anchoring, correction, or oversight. General automation-bias evidence is adjacent and task-dependent, not direct evidence about evidence-synthesis reviewers. The defensible rationale is to prospectively test for over-reliance and missed errors in this workflow, not claim it occurred.

## Recommendation for the manuscript

### Claim-specific placement

- **Introduction:** Keep the brief functional chain already in the baseline: RobotReviewer linked predictions to candidate text; ROBOTO2 made a RoB 2 workflow interactive. If space permits, say that prior human-use studies have tested actual reviewer interaction, including randomized task assessment and use within ongoing reviews. Then identify the bounded gap: the present comparison evaluates system outputs, rule conformance and structural records, whereas it did not observe reviewers using ARBITER. Do not imply that actual use has never been studied.
- **Discussion, current future-work paragraph:** Recommend a prospective, task-based evaluation in real or realistic systematic-review work that compares a clearly specified ARBITER-assisted role with usual human review. Keep endpoints separate: reviewer-level final appraisal against a blinded, independently established reference; answer/evidence corrections and error detection; time/workload; inspection of whether cited passages semantically support answers; and acceptability/intended workflow placement. Measure independent pre-suggestion judgments or otherwise preserve an independent reference before exposure, so label agreement is not confounded with anchoring. Include a way to record whether a reviewer inspected the source beyond the highlighted passage. Describe over-reliance as a hypothesis to assess, not an observed ARBITER risk.
- **Results/Discussion interpretation:** State that ARBITER's runtime is computational runtime, not reviewer time, and that retrospective agreement with two experts is not observed benefit. This transition is the most important guardrail if invoking Soboczenski/Arno.
- **Citation economy:** Soboczenski is the best compact citation for a randomized human-use precedent and observed corrections/time. Jardim is the best counterweight for adoption ambivalence and no manual time comparator. Arno is useful if the manuscript explicitly motivates evaluation in live reviews or cautions that task-study time effects need not transport; it can be omitted if space is tight. Cite Liang for the remaining semantic-support evaluation boundary, with qualification that the support labels were generated by an LLM.

### Strongest evidence for and against future reviewer-use evaluation

**For:** ARBITER's current benchmark does not observe user interpretation, error detection, time, or adoption. The 2019 randomized task study shows those are measurable and can diverge: a system can be accepted frequently yet have text edits, corrections and practical concerns. The 2022 real-world randomized study found time imprecise despite a noninferiority accuracy result. Jardim found that apparent task-level performance did not ensure willingness to change practice. These studies support a designed user evaluation with distinct endpoints.

**Against overclaiming:** The only controlled evidence is for RobotReviewer/RoB 1, not ARBITER/RoB 2; the studies are small or incompletely recruited; time and acceptability vary by context; the 2019 user sample may be favorable; Jardim lacked a human-only comparator; and none validates ARBITER's semantic support. The review supports why an evaluation would be informative, not a prediction that it will be beneficial. The workflow's own single-run offline results and structural audit cannot substitute for it.

### Best Discussion exemplar

**Soboczenski et al. (2019), Discussion, paragraphs beginning “We thus sought to evaluate…” and “One concern expressed by colleagues…” (PDF pp. 7–8; text lines approx. 313–355),** is the best structural exemplar. It explicitly contrasts retrospective algorithm accuracy with prospective reviewer use, reports a measured workflow outcome, surfaces the concern about reviewers focusing only on suggestions, then limits its reassurance to the observed task and recommends more user studies. This gives a useful sequence for ARBITER: state what the current evaluation did not measure; use prior study design to motivate a next study; balance observed corrections with plausible attention risk; bound the claim. Do not copy its conclusion that semi-automation improved efficiency, because that finding is specific to its own tool and task. Jardim's Discussion is the strongest complementary exemplar for presenting user ambivalence as a separate implementation outcome rather than treating agreement as adoption.

### Two exemplar passage descriptions (paraphrased, by locator)

1. **Soboczenski 2019, Discussion, pp. 7–8:** the authors explain that prior work mostly compared model predictions with reference data, then describe their randomized human-in-the-loop evaluation; later they acknowledge concern about superficial acceptance and use observed task engagement/editing only as bounded evidence against that concern. This is a strong balanced Discussion move, although the study did not measure reasons for unchanged judgments.
2. **Jardim 2022, Results and Discussion, pp. 6–9:** the paper reports acceptance and correction counts against both the AI and a second human before describing mixed acceptance of the tool in the review team. It then reports that time could not be compared because there was no manual arm. This is a good model for keeping correction, time and acceptability as separate outcomes and for stating when a design cannot answer a practical question.
