## Three useful paper-level writing exemplars

The first two are the strongest matches for evidence synthesis and methods reporting. The third contributes a recent clinical-AI Discussion pattern, not a style template. The maps describe paragraph functions from the article text; they do not attribute prose to Riaz personally unless author contributions say so. No substantial sentences are reproduced.

### 1. Khan et al., JAMIA 2025: task-centered workflow and explicit fallback

**Why selected.** Peer-reviewed primary evidence-synthesis methods paper, directly about clinical-trial extraction in living systematic reviews; Riaz is last/corresponding author. The full Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion were inspected in the published version.

- **Introduction, paragraphs 1–3:** Opens with clinicians' difficulty keeping pace with expanding literature; introduces living systematic reviews as a response, then names manual screening/extraction and extraction error as the operational bottleneck. The next paragraph moves from older NLP approaches and their annotated-data burden to LLM capabilities. The final paragraph states the remaining workflow need, the two-model/cross-critique approach, and a directional hypothesis. This is a funnel from evidence-use problem → review-process bottleneck → candidate mechanism → study question, rather than an opening with model architecture.
- **Methods:** Starts with corpus and variable set, then development/test split, model roles, concordance definition, cross-critique and accuracy calculation. This is a sequence of reviewer-reproducible operations. “Concordant” and “cross-critique” are locally operationalized; the ordinary systematic-review concept of two independent reviewers is invoked by analogy. The article does not stop to explain familiar data extraction, accuracy or living-review concepts to its specialist audience.
- **Results:** First reports the number of trials/publications and split, then response counts and concordance, then accuracy for concordant/discordant responses and the cross-critique outcome. Denominators and the status of each response group stay adjacent. It separates prompt-development from held-out test data rather than collapsing them into a single performance headline.
- **Discussion:** Opens with the principal workflow interpretation; returns to the living-review bottleneck; then discusses strengths and limitations. The limitation block names single disease area, possible publication-level contamination across split, model-pair choice, and other workflow boundaries. It follows the interpretation and strengths, while a future-work paragraph and short conclusion restore the bounded application. The paper's own “likely to be accurate” wording is an interpretation; it should not be converted into certainty.
- **Rhythm/terms:** Paragraphs are medium-to-long and information dense, typically one argument unit with several linked qualifications. Technical abbreviations such as LSR, NLP and LLM are expanded at first use; familiar systematic-review terms are not tutorialized. The novel process label “cross-critique” is made concrete by defining whose discordant responses are supplied to whom. The analogy to a human two-reviewer process explains the workflow's role, not that a model is equivalent to a reviewer.

**Use for our revision:** Preserve the reader's existing RoB/systematic-review schema; describe only the unfamiliar automation steps; report each process-defined denominator with its result; locate where human judgment remains necessary. **Do not borrow** this article's positive generalization or imply task equivalence: it studies extraction, not RoB 2 judgment.

### 2. Riaz et al., npj Digital Medicine 2025: a design problem before model choice

**Why selected.** Peer-reviewed empirical study; direct full text and contribution statement inspected. It is first-authored and corresponding by Riaz, while original drafting is explicitly shared by three authors.

- **Introduction, paragraph sequence:** Paragraph 1 establishes the scientific use of cancer outcomes, then the missing-data problem in longitudinal EHRs. Paragraph 2 presents AI extraction as a possible route but immediately gives two constraints: variable clinical interpretation and sensitive data/privacy. Paragraph 3 introduces federated learning and distillation, with explanation of the specific limitation each addresses. The final paragraph narrows to unresolved design choices and states what the study evaluates. It proceeds problem → candidate family → competing constraints → exact question.
- **Methods:** Gives study cohorts and outcome definitions before comparing training configurations and evaluation. Named outcomes and operational definitions anchor the computational choices. “Teacher–student” and “distillation” are explained through their roles in transferring labels/model behavior; deeply familiar oncology terms (e.g., RECIST) are expanded or contextualized as appropriate. The article assumes biomedical expertise but explains nonstandard ML operations.
- **Results:** Organized by outcome and training strategy, with estimates and uncertainty reported in the relevant outcome paragraphs. This makes the clinical target visible while comparing configurations; the reader need not reconstruct which endpoint each metric refers to from a distant table.
- **Discussion:** Returns to the three outcomes and privacy/scalability problem, interprets where configurations worked differently, then states limits of cohort/outcome scope and prospective validation. The paper connects limitations to the intended use rather than turning them into a generic AI caveat. The Discussion does not establish that performance transports to other diseases or institutions.
- **Rhythm/terms:** Alternates relatively long explanatory sentences with shorter purpose or result statements. It uses conventional biomedical terms without lengthy definitions and spends its explanation budget on federated learning, teacher–student training and privacy risk. Contribution statements support shared, not individual, prose credit.

**Use for our revision:** Introduce the clinical-review problem before an unfamiliar computational approach; define unfamiliar mechanism at first point of need; keep domain/outcome scope adjacent to interpretation. **Do not use** this oncology task's findings to infer ARBITER performance or to claim privacy/scalability properties for our tool.

### 3. Kenaston et al., npj Digital Medicine 2026: observable errors and consequences

**Why selected.** Recent peer-reviewed early-version clinical oncology study with Riaz as corresponding author. It helps with clinically grounded Results-to-Discussion progression and limitation placement. It is a coauthored paper without passage-level authorship attribution.

- **Introduction:** Starts with high benchmark performance alongside unresolved real-world reliability, then identifies the clinically consequential gap: interpreting authentic oncology notes and making recommendations. It previews an error taxonomy and two-cohort evaluation after the gap. This is a clear benchmark-to-practice contrast, but not a formula to impose when a manuscript's evidence does not establish that gap.
- **Methods/Results:** Methods specify retrospective cohorts, clinical tasks and a hierarchical reasoning-error taxonomy. Results move from prevalence/type of error to association with task and clinician-rated impact. The taxonomy is useful because it names errors visible in the data before interpreting possible clinical consequences.
- **Discussion and limitations:** Discussion relates error patterns to recommendation tasks and lower guideline concordance/clinical-impact ratings, then delimits conclusions to the studied notes, models and cohorts. Limitations should be read alongside the actual design details; do not turn association into causal harm.
- **Terms/rhythm:** “Reasoning error” is made operational by taxonomy and scoring rather than left as a vague label. Clinical recommendation and guideline-concordance concepts are allowed to carry their specialist meaning. Acronyms are expanded on first use. The prose is structured around contrasts and named categories, with tables carrying much of the detail.

**Use for our revision:** When evidence supports it, name an observed failure mode, then state the measured consequence and its uncertainty. **Do not** import its framing to claim clinical harm from ARBITER's judgments or treat clinician ratings as patient outcomes.

## Cross-paper observations, not claims about a personal voice

Across these selected papers, the most transferable practice is **audience-calibrated explanation**: retain established biomedical/evidence-synthesis terminology; define unfamiliar software operations where the reader must understand them; do not define every standard concept for a knowledgeable reviewer. The JAMIA study is especially concrete in explaining the workflow labels; the distillation paper spends its extra exposition on teacher–student transfer, federated learning and the data-privacy rationale. Both tie the system to an evidence or clinical task before method details.

Paragraphs commonly perform one broad move, but sentence rhythm is not a uniform short-sentence style. Multi-clause sentences carry rationale, definitions and qualifications; shorter sentences state the question, transition or principal result. The most useful rhythm lesson is therefore variation by function, not sentence-length imitation. In discussions, limitations tend to follow the initial interpretation/strengths and sit near the claims they bound; future directions do not substitute for study-specific limitation statements.

These are observations of collaborative published writing. The JAMIA author order/correspondence and npj corresponding role do not identify who wrote individual paragraphs. The distillation paper explicitly names several original drafters. The ROBINS-I contribution note explicitly includes Riaz among four first-draft writers, but without body access it cannot support paragraph mapping. Only the sole-author JCO item permits direct attribution of text, and its access and genre limits make it weak evidence for preferred style. No fact about Riaz's personal preferences or likely approval follows from this sample.
