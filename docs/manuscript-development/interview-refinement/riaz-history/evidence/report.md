# Recent evidence-synthesis and clinical-methods writing sample

## Scope and reading basis

This is a targeted deep read of Riaz-linked publications from 2023–2026, with emphasis on living systematic reviews/meta-analyses, risk-of-bias or evidence-extraction methods, and adjacent clinical-methods studies. I read seven complete works: the 2024 Mayo evidence-synthesis framework perspective; the 2025 living prostate-cancer systematic-review preprint; the 2025 European Urology living meta-analysis of PARP inhibitors; the 2025 JAMIA LLM extraction study; the 2025 npj Digital Medicine distillation study; the 2025 *Cancer* care-continuum review; and the 2026 *npj Digital Medicine* oncology-note reasoning study. EviSearch (2026) was also inspected as a full preprint, making eight full-text works total. I inspected the abstract and contributor record, but not the full text, for the 2024 ROBINS-I paper. A 2026 living network meta-analysis and sole-author JCO overview were checked as leads but not counted as full-text reads. The full item-by-item ledger records version, role, access, locators and limits.

Sources are primary article text and official metadata where available. The living PARP-inhibitor meta-analysis full PDF was openly accessible at a non-publisher mirror; I checked article identity and status against PubMed and recorded the mirror separately. PMC and publisher captures were already present in the literature cache or ledgers. There was no access circumvention. Search date: 2026-10-23. Queries included author-plus-topic variants, exact titles/DOIs/PMIDs, and database-limited searches for risk-of-bias, LLM, living review/meta-analysis and evidence synthesis. The search was purposeful and broad within these topics, not a complete bibliography or systematic review.

I read the clinical-research-writing skill and its governing principles: exact gap, audience-calibrated context, problem before method, results before interpretation, calibrated verbs and claim width, and a conclusion bounded to the evidence. I cross-checked with relevant Schimel passages in *Writing Science* (chapters 4 and 9 on OCAR/opening and resolution; chapters 11 and 13 on paragraph units and flow; chapters 16 and 18 on condensation and limitations), Heard, *The Scientist’s Guide to Writing* (chapter 7, finding and planning a story; chapter 12, Results organization and quantitative communication; chapter 13, Introduction/Discussion roles), and Zinsser, *On Writing Well* (chapter 8 unity; chapter 15 sequential scientific explanation). I use these as editorial lenses, not scientific sources or rigid templates. The practical check is whether each paragraph has one job, results stay distinct from interpretation, and the ending returns to the paper’s opening problem without widening the claim.

## What recurs across these papers

These are recurring article-level practices, not a stable personal voice. Riaz is corresponding or senior author on several examples, but that does not establish who wrote a given paragraph. Drafting-role statements narrow the inference: he is one of five drafters of the PARP meta-analysis, one of three original drafters of the distillation study, and one of four credited first-draft writers on the ROBINS-I paper. EviSearch gives no paragraph-level attribution. Only the sole-author JCO review found in the searched corpus could directly establish individual wording, and its full text was not accessible here. Accordingly, no claim about guaranteed approval or personal preference is warranted.

### 1. Open from a concrete evidence-use problem, then state the precise task gap

The 2024 Mayo perspective opens with review burden and rapid obsolescence before asking whether LLMs could conduct an SRMA (Introduction ¶¶1–2). It then walks through the component stages and their differing limitations before proposing a linked, living workflow (¶¶3–17). The 2025 JAMIA article similarly places evidence-extraction burden and the need for recurring updates before its collaborative system and study question (Introduction ¶¶1-3: manual extraction burden; earlier NLP/LLM limits; workflow aim and hypothesis). In the clinical meta-analysis, the opening is more narrowly clinical: the problem is choosing PARP-inhibitor treatment by gene alteration, followed by the stated quantitative subgroup question (Introduction, PDF pp.628–629). The distillation study moves from unmet phenotype availability, through privacy/scalability and the uncertain distillation strategy, to the specific comparison of training-data composition and distillation methods (Introduction ¶¶1–5).

The recurring useful move is a short chain—clinical or evidence-synthesis need → precise uncertainty → what this study evaluates. It is not that every paper uses the same opening. The system-perspective paper's broad “can LLMs conduct an SRMA?” question belongs to its proposal genre; the clinical living review begins with the disease and treatment decision. For ARBITER, use the exact reviewer-facing gap and the study's bounded evaluation question before naming system components. Avoid reproducing a sweeping “AI will transform” rationale from review/perspective genres.

### 2. Explain method mechanics in the order needed to understand the evaluation

Methods-oriented papers often name the workflow unit or process before the model details. The living review describes the LIvE framework, search/update cutoff, eligibility, dual screening and extraction, then prior-treatment/biomarker strata (Methods ¶¶1–9). The JAMIA study defines dataset and preprocessing, then sampling/prompt development, model extraction, postprocessing, reviewer concordance/cross-critique, and evaluation (Methods ¶¶1-9 under “Data sources” through “Error analysis”). EviSearch similarly introduces the extraction/audit tasks and user review before its system and benchmark details (Introduction §§1–2; Methods/system sections). The distillation article explicitly defines teacher and student roles and then separates each training-data condition (Methods subsections for DFCI teacher, MIMIC, Wiki-text, synthetic data and student-only models; paragraphs are independently labeled by design).

This is useful when it clarifies why steps are ordered or what exactly is evaluated. However, it is not evidence of one house style: these system papers must explain their workflow, while a clinical synthesis spends Methods on eligibility, search, outcome definitions and meta-analytic strata. For ARBITER, the transferable principle is to state the evidence unit, sequence, comparator and decision transformation in order. Explain the new mechanism step by step; keep familiar RoB 2 concepts concise for expert readers.

### 3. Keep the result unit and metric adjacent; order evidence by the question

The clinical synthesis and meta-analysis are strong practical examples. In the living mCRPC review, Results begins with studies/patients and risk-of-bias context (Results ¶¶1–4), makes the living website/filter structure explicit (¶5), states which trial tier and prior-therapy strata are reported (¶¶6–7), then organizes treatment findings by those strata (¶¶8 onward). The prose repeatedly ties a treatment to the comparator and endpoint and keeps subgroup context close to the estimate. This is dense, but the tabular and website apparatus is integral to that article’s clinical-navigation purpose.

The PARP living meta-analysis moves from study selection and included-trial context to prespecified genetic strata and pooled effects; its abstract labels methods, findings/limitations and clinical implications separately. The distillation paper reports AUROC, AUPRC and F1 together for a named outcome and validation/test set (Results ¶¶8–17), then adds subgroup results and privacy attacks. This ordering is metric-heavy; it is justified by its predictive-model design, but would be unwieldy as prose in a clinical evidence synthesis. Across all three, the transferable habit is denominator/unit + endpoint + comparator + estimate + uncertainty nearby. Metric order is not universal and should follow the main question, not mimic another discipline’s default.

ARBITER should retain its formal conditional primary endpoint and explain exactly what it conditions on. The author-confirmed label-choice ordering—published-label agreement first, then independent experts, with exact agreement before kappa—reduces the need to make every metric co-equal in the prose. The all-cell expert comparisons can follow as a complementary evaluation. These papers do not justify changing that analysis hierarchy.

### 4. Discussion often returns to intended use, then names a boundary

The mCRPC living review’s Discussion is clinically organized by prior treatment and biomarker status (Discussion ¶¶2–9), then treats the interactive/living design as a strength (¶10) and limitations as a separate paragraph (¶11). It translates observed evidence to possible treatment choices, while explicitly noting gaps in directly applicable populations, heterogeneity, imprecision and evolving standards. The PARP meta-analysis likewise interprets gene-specific patterns and uncertainty before its limitations and conclusion (Discussion §§4–5). The JAMIA article discusses where its collaborative extraction approach fits in living reviews and distinguishes extraction assistance from independent clinical judgment. EviSearch's discussion frames the intended human-in-loop use and its limits; its reported benchmark remains a preprint result.

That proximity between implication and its boundary is worth preserving. Do not overgeneralize clinical decision language to ARBITER: its user is a reviewer assessing RoB 2 domains; the endpoint is agreement/decision behavior in a fixed benchmark. The author's confirmed expansion of the clinical/RoB2/living-evidence motivation and step-by-step system mechanism already fits the problem-first and sequential-explanation strengths observed here.

## Counterexamples and genre effects

The sample does not support “short, spare, direct sentences” as a personal signature. The living prostate review has long, highly nested Results paragraphs and repeated “In terms of…” / “In patients with…” constructions because its job is to navigate treatment, endpoint and patient subgroup combinations. The care-continuum review has broad topic-by-topic coverage and future-facing conclusions; its opening asserts transformative promise and its conclusion revisits workforce, governance, equity and multidisciplinary collaboration. That expansive review register would be a poor template for ARBITER's bounded primary study. The distillation study uses dense metric series, parenthetical definitions and lengthy Methods to expose model-design comparisons. The 2024 framework perspective uses a conceptual stage-by-stage tour rather than IMRaD. These differences are at least as plausibly explained by genre, journal, field, data presentation and coauthor composition as by any individual author's preference.

The 2025 PARP paper is especially instructive against a polished stereotype: it combines a compact problem-to-subgroup rationale with dense technical Methods, stratified estimates, a clinical “Advancing Practice” box and formal contribution/disclosure sections. Its contribution statement explicitly credits several coauthors with drafting. We can observe its organization; we cannot attribute that organization to Riaz alone.

## Transfer to ARBITER

**Worth carrying forward**

- State the concrete evidence-use need, exact gap and study question before describing the system.
- Explain ARBITER's evidence-to-answer-to-rating sequence in the order a reviewer would inspect it, and distinguish independent expert ratings from subsequent label choice.
- Keep unit, denominator, reference, endpoint, estimate and uncertainty together; use tables for full metric inventories.
- Organize the Discussion around what ARBITER exposes, what benchmark agreement indicates, and what it does not establish. Place limitations close to the claims they bound.
- Use field-standard terms for RoB 2 and systematic reviews; define the software mechanism and any nonstandard unit rather than re-teaching familiar appraisal concepts.

**Not worth copying**

- A comprehensive task-by-task survey of all review stages in an original-study Introduction.
- The repeated treatment-by-subgroup paragraph template from the living clinical review, where exhaustive clinical navigation is its purpose.
- A long list of model metrics or every sensitivity analysis in the Results narrative.
- The expansive, optimistic future register of a narrative review or framework perspective.
- Any implication that an extraction or retrieval trace demonstrates semantic support, correct RoB judgment, reviewer utility or clinical benefit.

## Conclusions for the ongoing author interview

The sample offers a few reliable questions for the interview, not a recipe: Which problem does the reader need to recognize first? Which mechanism needs a sequential explanation? Which result is primary, and which comparison answers a different question? Where should the limitation sit so it qualifies the claim it belongs to? These align with the author's confirmed choices: expanded clinical/RoB2/living-evidence rationale; full ARBITER name and evidence-linked title; stepwise system mechanism; published-label agreement before the independently collected expert comparison; exact agreement before kappa; and preservation of the conditional formal primary endpoint. The report captures congruence between those choices and selected published article structures, not evidence that Riaz personally prefers or will approve them.

## Source and access limitations

The ROBINS-I paper remains abstract/contributor-note level in this review; no style conclusions rely on it. EviSearch and the 2025 mCRPC systematic review are preprints, so their article structures are observable but their claims are not peer-reviewed evidence. The 2026 living network meta-analysis record was relevant, and its contributor note reports joint drafting by Riaz and colleagues, but the fulltext page returned an anti-bot challenge; it was not used for paragraph-level conclusions. The JCO sole-author overview was not locally accessible and is not used to infer sentence-level habits. This is an inspected sample, not an exhaustive publication-history audit.
