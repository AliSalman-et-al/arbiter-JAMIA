# Clinical AI paper history: rhetorical patterns and counterexamples

**Purpose and limits.** This is an article-level rhetorical analysis to support an author interview about the ARBITER JAMIA manuscript. It focuses on relevant clinical-AI empirical and system papers from 2023–2026, beyond the living-review extraction paper. The local corpus supports close reading of four complete full texts and abstract-level inspection of a fifth. It is a targeted sample, not an exhaustive bibliography. Publication patterns below describe collaborative texts; they do not establish Riaz’s private preferences, predict editorial acceptance, or justify courtesy citations.

## Works examined

### 1. Distillation for cancer outcomes from EHRs (2025; peer reviewed)

Riaz IB et al., “Empirical evaluation of artificial intelligence distillation techniques for ascertaining cancer outcomes from electronic health records,” *npj Digital Medicine* 8, 347 (2025), DOI [10.1038/s41746-025-01646-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-025-01646-7). First and corresponding author. The contribution statement assigns original drafting jointly to Riaz, Naqvi, and Kehl, so the prose cannot be assigned to Riaz alone. Full publisher/PMC text inspected.

- **Rationale and design choice:** Introduction ¶¶1–4: clinical outcome ascertainment in longitudinal EHRs leads into missingness, then candidate AI extraction and its interpretive/privacy constraints; federated learning and distillation are introduced as responses to different constraints before the unresolved design choices and study objective. The design rationale is local to the problem rather than presented as a generic model leaderboard.
- **Mechanism progression:** Methods, “Study design,” “Model development,” and the distillation/federated-learning subsections: cohort/outcome definitions precede the explanation of teacher–student transfer, where labels/behavior pass from a teacher to a deployable student; federated training is described as sites retaining data while exchanging model updates. The sequence is problem unit → data/cohort → training operation → evaluation. The paper explains the less familiar computational steps while leaving standard oncology concepts in specialist register.
- **Model role and uncertainty:** The methods distinguish teacher and student roles and discuss privacy as motivation, while the Discussion does not treat the design itself as proof that every privacy or transportability concern is resolved. Result interpretation remains tied to the studied cohorts/outcomes.
- **Results and metrics:** Results are organized by outcome and training configuration; the named target stays close to its reported performance (Results subsections and corresponding tables). This makes the clinical endpoint legible while models/configurations are compared. The Discussion returns to outcome-specific differences and study-scope constraints rather than converting them into universal model claims.
- **Counterweight:** The exposition is dense and architecture-heavy in Methods. Several claims and configurations require tables for comparison; it is not a uniformly terse or prose-only style. Shared drafting further weakens any personal-style inference.

### 2. Structured reasoning failures in oncology notes (2026; peer-reviewed early version)

Kenaston MW et al., “Structured reasoning failures compromise LLM interpretation of clinical oncology notes,” *npj Digital Medicine* 9 (2026), DOI [10.1038/s41746-026-02951-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-026-02951-5). Riaz is last/corresponding author; the inspected record provides no paragraph-level drafting attribution. Publisher HTML full text inspected. This is an early peer-reviewed version and may be edited before version of record.

- **Question and method:** Introduction ¶¶1–4 moves from benchmark capability to unresolved reliability on authentic notes, identifies clinical interpretation/recommendation as the gap, then previews a two-cohort evaluation and structured error categories. Methods defines the task, cohorts, and reasoning-error taxonomy before the Results use those categories.
- **Failure explanation:** Results subsections and taxonomy tables name observable error types, then report their distribution and associations with tasks/ratings. The explanation moves from what was counted, to where it occurred, to measured ratings. It does not by itself demonstrate patient harm or causal effects.
- **Role and uncertainty:** Model outputs are treated as objects of evaluation; clinician assessment is a separate measurement layer. The Discussion and limitations restrict interpretation to the evaluated notes, models, and cohorts. “Reasoning failure” is operationalized with a scoring scheme rather than used as an unmeasured psychological label.
- **Counterweight:** The paper’s framing is explicitly reliability-oriented and begins with a benchmark-to-practice contrast. That rhetorical move fits its error-focused question and should not be imported into ARBITER as a clinical-harm frame. Tables carry considerable detail; prose does not narrate every taxonomy cell.

### 3. EviSearch (2026; arXiv preprint)

Ahuja N et al., “EviSearch: A Human in the Loop System for Extracting and Auditing Clinical Evidence for Systematic Reviews,” arXiv:2604.14165 (2026), [record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14165). The checked version is an arXiv preprint, not a peer-reviewed publication. Riaz is one of two corresponding contacts; no individual prose contribution is established. Full PDF and text inspected.

- **Task-to-mechanism chain:** Introduction ¶¶1–4 frames the evidence-extraction burden and auditability need, then positions the system. System section (PDF pp. 4–6; text lines 206–369) describes extraction into a schema, parallel agent outputs, a triage/disagreement signal, source attribution, and human review. The stages are introduced in operational order: identify a value → locate source evidence → compare outputs → route uncertain/disputed cells for checking → retain provenance.
- **Why this design:** The system rationale is tied to traceability and reviewer effort rather than accuracy alone (Introduction and evaluation setup). Results separate autonomous accuracy, attribution/traceability, reviewer effort, and conditions of review (Results, PDF pp. 6–9; text lines 373–553). The article explicitly notes that autonomous accuracy is not the principal differentiator in the reported comparison (Results, text lines 492–511), an example of tying the claimed contribution to the metric that represents it.
- **Model role and uncertainty:** Agents are defined by access/role, and human review is an explicit workflow step rather than a claim that agents are clinicians. Discussion/limitations define intended assistance and remaining validation needs (Discussion, text lines 631 onward and 716 onward). Because it is a preprint, numerical and comparative claims remain unreviewed claims at this version.
- **Counterweight:** Its multi-agent architecture and several evaluation axes are more elaborate than a single-system empirical paper needs. The detailed mechanism should not be copied wholesale into ARBITER; the transferable move is to narrate the actual sequence and explain what each check can establish.

### 4. FD-SCoPE (2026; arXiv preprint)

Roy Choudhury M et al., “Answering clinicians’ questions over trial evidence tables with verifiable, feedback-driven language models,” arXiv:2610.02576v1 (submitted 1 October 2026), [record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02576). Corresponding author is Riaz; no paragraph-level contribution statement was found. Full PDF inspected. The source ledger identifies FD-NL2SQL (arXiv:2604.15646) and SCOPE (arXiv:2604.25120) only through citations/metadata; their full texts were not inspected and they are not counted as close-reading exemplars.

- **Rationale and task definition:** Introduction ¶¶1–3 (PDF pp. 2–3) distinguishes questions answered by recorded trial fields from questions requiring derived clinical attributes, identifies the gap, then states the study contributions. This taxonomy makes clear which task the system handles before naming architecture.
- **Mechanism progression:** Figure 1 and Methods §4.1 (PDF p. 11) describe a safety screen, routing to a plain NL2SQL or derivation path, record retrieval, field-level computation/verification, and response assembly. The methods give operational detail for the path; the figure carries the high-level route. Results §2.4 (PDF pp. 6–7) reports clinician-style task completion separately from returned-record agreement, derived-value performance, and held-out feedback outcomes. Units and denominators are kept near the relevant result.
- **Model role and limits:** Discussion §3 (PDF p. 9) positions FD-SCoPE as an interface over curated evidence and distinguishes its computational evaluation from clinical decision-making. This is a boundary statement, not evidence of clinician utility. The preprint’s results should not be represented as peer-reviewed.
- **Counterweight:** The tasks, database, and query/derivation pipeline differ fundamentally from RoB 2 appraisal. The detailed task taxonomy is useful as an explanation pattern only; it does not support ARBITER performance, semantic evidence support, or clinical effectiveness.

### 5. PICOS-assisted systematic-review screening (2025; abstract-level only)

Vallamchetla SK et al., “Do it faster with PICOS: Generative AI-Assisted systematic review screening,” *Journal of Biomedical Informatics* 168 (2025), 104860, DOI [10.1016/j.jbi.2025.104860](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbi.2025.104860). Riaz is a coauthor. Only title, abstract, author/bibliographic record were inspected from the prior PubMed trail; no full text or contribution statement was recovered. This is included to mark a relevant adjacent work, not to infer paragraph-level rhetoric or authorial habit. Any observations are restricted to the abstract and are omitted from the recurring-pattern claims below.

## Cross-paper observations and contrary evidence

The clearest recurring article-level feature in the four full texts is **mechanism before implication**: the reader is told what the system receives or what is counted, then what operation happens, then what output/result follows. Distillation uses teacher/student and federated-update steps; oncology reasoning defines errors before associating them with task/rating patterns; EviSearch routes extracted cells through provenance and review; FD-SCoPE separates retrieval from derivation before reporting outcomes. This is a feature of these articles and their technical subjects, not a confirmed personal preference.

The papers also show **purpose-linked metric organization**. Named clinical outcomes or units are kept near the associated result, and distinct evaluation levels are not collapsed into one broad accuracy claim. EviSearch distinguishes autonomous accuracy from traceability/reviewer effort; FD-SCoPE separates task completion, record agreement, and derived values; the oncology reasoning paper moves from a defined error category to observed frequency/association. Distinct metrics answer distinct questions.

**Model roles are operational rather than personified.** Teacher/student, extraction agents, routing/derivation components, and evaluated LLMs are described by inputs, access, and functions. Where human expertise appears, it is a separate review or rating step. The papers do not establish that model output is equivalent to a clinician’s judgment. For ARBITER, this supports stating which operations the model performs and where rules, evidence records, or experts enter, without calling the system a reviewer.

There is **no single sentence-length or density template**. The distillation and EviSearch methods are technically dense; FD-SCoPE alternates a concise task taxonomy with detailed architecture; the reasoning paper relies on categories and tables. Explanation is spent on unfamiliar operations, while domain-specific terms are often left for the specialist audience. These are genre/task effects as much as any possible author influence.

Counterexamples bound these patterns: some system papers have multi-stage architectures and extensive evaluation inventories; tables/figures carry detail that prose does not repeat; some discussions use a strong benchmark-to-practice contrast specific to their study question; and EviSearch/FD-SCoPE are preprints. None yields a stable “Riaz voice.” The distillation paper’s original drafting is shared by three authors, and the other multi-author works offer no passage-level attribution. Corresponding-author status does not establish who wrote a paragraph. The screening paper is too limited to add stylistic evidence.

## Interview-stage implications for the ARBITER discussion

These are questions/working hypotheses for discussion with the author, not inferred preferences or revision orders. They are consistent with the author-confirmed decisions recorded for this project: retain expanded clinical/RoB 2/living-evidence motivation, use the full ARBITER name, keep the evidence-linked title, explain system mechanism step by step, report published-label agreement before independent experts and exact agreement before kappa, and preserve the formal conditional primary endpoint.

1. **Mechanism:** Does the current system description let a reader follow an input trial passage through answer capture, executable rule application, and domain rating in that order? The close-reading examples suggest the mechanism is most legible when each transition is explicit. Retain the actual ARBITER mechanism; analogy to these systems is not evidence for it.
2. **Role wording:** Are model, rule engine, source record, and human expert roles distinct at each step? The comparator and expert sequence should remain consistent with the confirmed design. Avoid language suggesting a model has independent clinical authority.
3. **Metrics:** Do published-label agreement, independent-expert exact agreement, and kappa each have their own named question, unit, and denominator adjacent to the result? Keep exact agreement before chance-corrected agreement, and preserve the conditional primary as formal primary while explaining its conditional interpretation.
4. **Uncertainty and limits:** Does every result state what it measures, followed by the relevant boundary (benchmark-specific agreement, process conformance, source-link integrity, or semantic support)? The papers reviewed do not make provenance alone equivalent to semantic correctness; ARBITER’s wording should retain that distinction.
5. **Results sequence:** The title/order of subsections should help the reader distinguish label concordance from independent expert comparison without implying that one outcome supersedes the formally designated primary. A short transition can identify the different inferential target.

No citation is recommended merely to echo the author’s publication history. Cite any of these works only if a specific substantive claim in the manuscript requires it and the design/status is accurately described. Neither rhetorical resemblance nor corresponding authorship demonstrates approval or preference.
