# Riaz publication-history voice review (2023–2026)

**Purpose.** Identify useful writing preferences for the ARBITER JAMIA author interview from recent work by Irbaz Bin Riaz (Mayo Clinic/BEACON), with emphasis on sole-, first-, corresponding-authored papers and declared drafting contributions. This is a targeted review of six works across overview, clinical review, perspective, and empirical-methods genres. It is not a complete bibliography, stylometric authorship analysis, or prediction of editorial approval.

**Review date:** 23 October 2026. **Disposition:** Useful, qualified profile. Full text was inspected for five works in the local corpus and primary-publisher indexed full text for the sole-author JCO overview. Search terms, source routes, version/role basis, access, and exact text locators are in `source-ledger.tsv`.

## Finding in brief

The stable advice is about *audience and explanation*, not a signature prose style: establish a clinical or evidence-workflow problem; state the task or question; explain unfamiliar AI/system operations at the point they matter; keep clinical meaning and workflow consequences visible; and say where human expertise, uncertainty, or validation still matters. These are practices that recur in some but not all sampled articles.

The best evidence of Riaz's own sentence-level choices comes from one sole-author invited JCO overview (2026), and one *Cancer* review whose author-contribution note credits him with original drafting alongside two coauthors. The JCO piece has an editorial-series brief and the *Cancer* piece is a sweeping clinical review. Those genres reward broad framing and future-facing synthesis. Most empirical work is coauthored, so its conventional reporting architecture cannot safely be assigned to him personally. The paper sample does **not** support “Riaz always writes X,” a dependable first-person preference, a universal paragraph length, or an inference that adopting a style will guarantee manuscript approval.

For ARBITER, keep the author-confirmed choices already established in the interview: broaden the clinical and risk-of-bias motivation (including living evidence), spell out the full ARBITER name, use an evidence-linked title, explain the system mechanism step by step, report agreement with published labels before independent experts and exact agreement before kappa, and retain the conditional primary endpoint as the formal primary. The profile below is compatible with those decisions; it is not a rationale to change them.

## Specific observations, with counterexamples

The counts below refer to the six sampled works, not the author's total output. They are descriptive checks rather than statistical tests. Paragraph lengths are counted from paragraph segmentation in publisher/PMC text and are not comparable across journal layouts.

### Opening scale

- **Broad clinical/stakes opening is visible in 3/6** (the sole-author JCO overview, the *Cancer* review, and the 2024 Mayo perspective). Examples: JCO begins with LLMs as a major technological shift and then pivots to oncology's data and vulnerability context (opening paragraph; publisher PDF pp. 1–2); *Cancer* begins with the potential of AI across cancer care (Introduction ¶1); Mayo opens on the time and resource burden of systematic reviews and their rapid obsolescence (Introduction ¶1).
- **A bounded operational gap also appears in the empirical/system papers (3/6):** JAMIA starts with the evidence-update burden then lands on manual screening/extraction and error as the workflow bottleneck (Introduction ¶¶1–3); npj starts with limited longitudinal clinical-outcome data then defines the privacy/scalability problem (Introduction ¶¶1–4). EviSearch starts from clinician questions over trial evidence tables and differentiates queryable versus derived information (Introduction ¶¶1–3; PDF pp. 2–3).
- **Counterexample:** the *Cancer* review stays expansive across the prevention-to-end-of-life continuum and uses many speculative application claims. The short Mayo perspective shifts from stage-by-stage review of automation to a proposed framework. A broad opening is a genre option, not a personal rule. For ARBITER, use the clinical stakes already chosen, then narrow promptly to the actual risk-of-bias workflow gap; do not imitate a broad oncology horizon.

### Transitions and paragraph jobs

Across the six, transitions often mark a move from established practice or task to constraint, then to a possible system/framework and its needed safeguard. Mayo 2024 uses explicit turns (“Although…”, “However…”, “Therefore…”) from evidence-synthesis steps to the integrated human–AI framework (Introduction ¶¶2, 7, 13–16). npj 2025 uses a more empirical funnel: clinical outcome need → NLP/longitudinal context → privacy constraint → unresolved distillation choices (Introduction ¶¶1–4). JCO's invited overview uses section headings to organize context, a survey of the eight papers, then agenda and conclusion; that high-level transition pattern depends on the special-series assignment.

This is not a consistent “however-therefore” personal tic. It is a recognizable but conventional logic scaffold. Avoid repeated contrastive pivots when a direct statement of the remaining gap would be clearer. For ARBITER, a transition should explain why the preceding review workflow creates the next methodological need, not merely announce a new topic.

### First person

First person is **variable across the sample**, not a stable preference: visible in the JCO overview (editorial “we” in the series discussion), Mayo 2024 (first-person proposal language, e.g., Introduction ¶¶2, 15–19), *Cancer* (aim and conclusion, Introduction ¶¶1–4; Conclusion ¶¶4, 6, 8), JAMIA (methods/action and interpretation), and npj (e.g., Discussion ¶¶1–4), and EviSearch (Introduction and Methods). The *Cancer* first-person plural is present even though Riaz is a credited original drafter; it remains coauthored language. **Negative case:** the opening paragraphs of the npj paper and several descriptive review sections are impersonal. Use first person when it clarifies who performed an action or is proposing a framework; do not force it into every claim.

### Paragraph size and rhythm

No single length dominates. In the sampled introductions, npj paragraph sizes are about 73–179 words (4 paragraphs); *Cancer* has a broader range of about 74–358 words (5 opening paragraphs, with the article then organized by care stage); Mayo's numbered BioC introduction paragraphs range from very short section-label items to roughly 228 words; JAMIA and JCO also use both short framing/transition paragraphs and denser explanatory blocks. The *Cancer* opening paragraph and several conclusion paragraphs run long, while the JCO overview uses compact sections and the empirical papers often place one interpretive move per paragraph. This is a genre/layout counterexample to any short-paragraph stereotype.

For ARBITER, give each paragraph one step of the argument and let the mechanism section carry necessary detail. Split where a paragraph moves from system operation to validation or interpretation, not to enforce an arbitrary word ceiling.

### Technical explanation and intended reader

A useful recurring feature is **selective explanation**. Mayo 2024 explains conventional evidence-synthesis steps sufficiently to motivate automation, then explains the novel integrated framework and assigns functions to modules (Introduction ¶¶4–19). JAMIA operationalizes its new “cross-critique” workflow and ties it to human review, while leaving familiar extraction/accuracy concepts largely in specialist register (Methods and Discussion). npj explains federated learning and teacher–student distillation at the point they address data privacy and sharing (Introduction ¶3; Methods). EviSearch separates trial-table lookup from deriving unrecorded clinical attributes before detailing components (Introduction ¶¶1–3; Methods §4.1). The sole-author JCO overview uses a more general oncology-reader register and contains broad contextual explanation.

**Negative case / caution:** the *Cancer* review's very wide tour (risk, prevention, diagnosis, treatment, survivorship, end-of-life, and care delivery) devotes paragraphs to many applications, sometimes with claims that reach beyond a specialist system paper's evidence. That is useful evidence of how review genre differs, not a model for ARBITER's detail. For the target, retain expert RoB 2 vocabulary and explain the actual system mechanism sequentially, as the author already requested; do not add a RoB 2 tutorial or use “human-like” glosses where the mechanism can be named more exactly.

### Claim verbs and calibration

The claim register changes with genre and study design. Empirical JAMIA and npj passages usually use task-matched verbs such as *evaluated*, *assessed*, *explored*, *found*, and *suggests*; their interpretation is linked to studied datasets/outcomes and ends with named limits. Mayo 2024 and JCO use proposal/agenda verbs (*propose*, *could*, *may*, *will*) and, at points, stronger promotional language because they are perspectives/overviews. The *Cancer* review includes both cautious “potential/may” wording and confident predictions about future AI use; its conclusion is notably expansive. This is an important negative case against reading every Riaz-linked work as uniformly restrained.

For ARBITER, verbs should follow the actual design and estimand: *agreed*, *matched*, *classified*, or *was associated with* where warranted. Keep agreement distinct from validity, clinical utility, repeatability, and benefit. Preserve the existing conditional primary estimand as formal primary, as already author-confirmed. Do not import future-facing certainty from review articles.

### Limitations and endings

In the empirical JAMIA and npj papers, limitations are explicit and concrete, usually after the main interpretation/strengths and before future work or the conclusion (npj Discussion ¶4; JAMIA Discussion limitations section). The limitations name dataset, model, outcome, or validation boundaries rather than relying only on a general AI caution. In a perspective, Mayo 2024 instead foregrounds the promise of an integrated approach while acknowledging hallucination, transparency, and human-role concerns in its discussion (Introduction ¶¶16–19); it is not an empirical limitations section. The JCO overview closes by listing research gaps and arguing for collaboration, then says the series offers principles rather than definitive answers (Conclusion, publisher-indexed final pages). *Cancer* closes with a broad optimistic call to responsible human–AI collaboration and improved outcomes (Conclusion ¶¶4–8) after a very future-oriented article. These are distinct genre-specific endings.

**Negative case:** there is no single shared “limitations always first” or “always end with a modest caveat” pattern. Preserve ARBITER's design-specific limitations near the claims they bound, then end on the bounded contribution supported by results. Do not substitute a generic “more research is needed” sentence or an expansive promise about review transformation.

## Nuanced voice profile

**Higher-confidence, interview-safe preferences**

- Keep the clinical/evidence task in view while explaining AI; the method is valuable only in relation to a real workflow or decision.
- Define new workflow mechanics, role labels, or system components at first use, especially when they affect how evidence is reviewed or a judgment is produced.
- Retain clinical expertise and human oversight as substantive parts of the workflow, not ceremonial caveats.
- Use section structure and explicit transition logic to orient the reader through complex, multi-step systems.
- State validation, implementation, and evidence gaps explicitly when the article makes forward-looking claims.

**Lower-confidence tendencies, not rules**

- Broad opening stakes and broad future-facing conclusions recur in review/perspective genres but not in all empirical papers.
- First-person plural appears in several kinds of paper, but is not obligatory or constant.
- Longer, information-dense paragraphs occur, particularly in reviews, but short transitions and bounded empirical paragraphs are counterexamples.
- Contrast words and named frameworks recur as ordinary scientific rhetoric; avoid overreading them as signature habits.

No inference here supports a claim about what Dr. Riaz will prefer or approve in this specific manuscript. His direct interview decisions are better evidence than stylistic generalization from this corpus.

## ARBITER-specific do / don't guidance

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Lead from clinical decision-making and the limitations of manual/fragmented risk-of-bias work into the exact gap ARBITER addresses. | Start with a generic AI revolution, cancer-care panorama, or unsupported claim that LLMs replace reviewers. |
| Use the full ARBITER name and evidence-linked title already confirmed by the author. | Treat the acronym as self-explanatory or select a catchy title that obscures the study's evidence base. |
| Walk the reader through input evidence, specialist/model roles, independent judgments, disagreement handling, and outputs in the actual order of operations. | Skip system mechanics or imply source retrieval alone proves that an excerpt semantically supports a judgment. |
| Keep published-label comparison first, then comparison with independent experts; report exact agreement before kappa, as confirmed. | Collapse different reference standards or agreement statistics into a single “accuracy” headline. |
| Keep the conditional primary endpoint formally primary and explain what the conditioning means. | Recast it as an unconditional performance claim or quietly demote it to secondary. |
| Use risk-of-bias and evidence-synthesis terms as expert vocabulary; define only ARBITER-specific roles, measures, and mechanics. | Over-explain RoB 2 basics or import the sweeping explanatory register of oncology reviews. |
| Match verbs and implications to the observed agreement design; keep validity, utility, repeatability, and reviewer benefit separate. | Say “validated,” “improves,” “replaces,” or “clinical utility” unless the specific design and evidence establish that claim. |
| Place concrete limits close to claims about domain, reference experts, published labels, and system scope; finish with the bounded contribution. | Add a promotional future promise or a generic future-work line as the last word. |

These recommendations are informed by the clinical-research-writing skill and its evidence/claim calibration, audience fit, IMRaD placement, and limitation guidance. The cross-check is consistent with Schimel, *Writing Science* (chs. 2–5, 9–11, 18, 21): distill the story from the evidence, give each paragraph a clear job, and keep the resolution within the result. Heard, *The Scientist's Guide to Writing*, and Zinsser, *On Writing Well*, were consulted as secondary checks on reader orientation and clutter. These are editorial principles, not evidence about Riaz's preferences.
