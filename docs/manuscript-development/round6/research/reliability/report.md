# Reliability and reviewer-use evidence

## Verification and the Minozzi 2022 claim

The previously cached minozzi2022.txt was not used. The cited target is Minozzi et al., Journal of Clinical Epidemiology 2022;141:99–105, DOI 10.1016/j.jclinepi.2021.09.021. ScienceDirect’s publisher-indexed full-text view confirms the exact title, DOI, study and results; this is the RoB 2 multiple-sclerosis review, not the unrelated immunization text. The direct publisher download returned HTML rather than a PDF. Open full text for Soboczenski and Jardim was retrieved through Europe PMC and saved at /tmp/arbiter-r6-fulltexts/reliability. The Bertizzolo publisher PDF endpoint did not yield a local PDF; its publisher-indexed full-text PDF text and abstract were inspected online. Minozzi 2020 was checked against the ScienceDirect indexed abstract and PubMed record; I did not inspect its full article body.

The current statement that review-specific instructions and calibration “were followed by improved agreement in one setting” is directionally correct but hides the magnitude, sequence, and limitations. Four raters assessed 80 outcome results from 16 RCTs in one Cochrane review of cannabis/cannabinoids for multiple sclerosis. They calibrated on five studies, resolved disagreements by discussion, and developed a review-specific implementation document with detailed instructions for answering RoB 2 signalling questions in that condition/intervention. Overall Fleiss κ was −0.15 during calibration, 0.11 (slight) for the first five studies assessed after adopting the document, and 0.42 (moderate) for the remaining 11. The abstract also reports mean time per study of 168.5 minutes during calibration and 41 minutes after adoption, with about 40 hours spent on calibration and document development.

This was a single-team sequential evaluation, not a randomized comparison of instruction against no instruction. Nor is it a straightforward identical-set before/after comparison: the first five studies comprise calibration, agreement is separately reported for the first five after document adoption, and 0.42 applies to the remaining 11. The pattern is compatible with benefit from tailored instruction, but also with discussion, practice, learning, or study sequence. Do not attribute the change to the document alone or generalize it to other review teams.

**Recommended replacement (complete):**

> In one Cochrane review, four raters assessed 80 results from 16 trials; after calibrating on five studies and developing review-specific guidance for answering RoB 2 signalling questions, overall agreement rose from no agreement during calibration (Fleiss κ=−0.15) to slight agreement for the first five studies assessed with the guidance (κ=0.11) and moderate agreement for the remaining 11 (κ=0.42). Because this was a single-team, sequential evaluation, it does not isolate the effect of the guidance from discussion, practice, or study order.[3]

**Shorter option:**

> In one multiple-sclerosis review, calibration and review-specific instructions for answering RoB 2 signalling questions preceded an increase in overall Fleiss κ from −0.15 during calibration to 0.11 for the first five studies assessed with the instructions and 0.42 for the remaining 11; the sequential single-team design cannot attribute this change to the instructions alone.[3]

“Followed by” is temporally accurate, but “improved” can read causally without the design qualifier. Keep “agreement” or define Fleiss κ; specify that the guidance concerned signalling-question answers. This was not generic training.

## Contextualized fact cards

### Minozzi 2020 (manuscript reference 2)

**Design and sample.** Cross-sectional reliability/usability exercise: four raters independently applied RoB 2 to primary outcomes of 70 individually randomized parallel-group RCTs (70 outcomes).

**Verified facts.** Overall Fleiss κ 0.16 (95% CI 0.08–0.24); randomization-process κ 0.45 (0.37–0.53); deviations-from-intervention κ 0.04 (−0.06 to 0.14) for assignment effect and 0.21 (0.11–0.31) for adherence effect; other domains 0.22–0.30. Mean assessment time was 28 minutes (SD 13.4) per outcome.

**Locator and source.** ScienceDirect indexed abstract, p. 37 of J Clin Epidemiol 2020;126:37–44; DOI 10.1016/j.jclinepi.2020.06.015; PMID 32562833. Abstract only inspected.

**Supports / cannot support.** Supports that this sampled exercise found slight overall agreement and nontrivial assessment time. It does not estimate reliability in review-specific expert teams, establish a universal rate, or show that training fixes the problem.

**Prose option.** “In a cross-sectional exercise in which four raters assessed 70 trial outcomes, overall Fleiss κ was 0.16 (95% CI, 0.08–0.24), and mean assessment time was 28 (SD 13.4) minutes per outcome.[2]”

### Minozzi 2022 (manuscript reference 3)

**Design and sample.** Four raters, one Cochrane multiple-sclerosis cannabis/cannabinoid review; 80 results across seven outcomes from 16 individually randomized parallel-group trials.

**Verified facts.** Calibration on the first five studies yielded κ=−0.15 overall. After the implementation document, overall κ=0.11 for the first five studies and 0.42 for the remaining 11. Domain-level IRR ranged from no agreement to almost perfect. Mean time was 168.5 minutes per study during calibration and 41 minutes per study after adoption; calibration and ID development took about 40 hours.

**What the instruction involved.** A review-specific document, tailored to the clinical condition and intervention, with detailed instructions for answering RoB 2 signalling questions. The five-study calibration included discussion/resolution of disagreements.

**Locator and source.** Abstract p. 99; publisher-indexed full-text sections “Risk of bias assessment with RoB2,” “Inter-rater reliability of the calibration exercise and time needed,” and “Main findings”; journal pages 99–105; DOI 10.1016/j.jclinepi.2021.09.021. Full-text publisher-indexed view inspected, direct download unavailable.

**Supports / cannot support.** Supports a concrete sequence in which tailored implementation instructions followed by higher observed agreement and lower observed time in this team. It does not isolate an instruction effect, prove generalizability, or provide a clean same-sample before/after. Keep the source unit “per study,” not “per result.”

### Bertizzolo 2019 (manuscript reference 4)

**Design and sample.** Cross-sectional “research on research” of the earlier Cochrane tool: 2,796 Cochrane reviews screened; 1,604 RCTs included in multiple reviews. Five items were compared. For the small subset appearing in at least three reviews, two reviews were randomly selected for direct comparison.

**Verified facts.** Item-level agreement ranged from 57% (770/1,348) for incomplete outcome data to 81% (1,193/1,466) for sequence generation. Among classified disagreements, different interpretation of the same information accounted for 65% (88/136) in sequence generation to 90% (56/62) in participant/personnel blinding. In the reason table, incomplete/unclear description accounted for 73 (83%) sequence-generation disagreements attributed to interpretation.

**Locator and source.** BMJ Open 2019;9:e028382, DOI 10.1136/bmjopen-2018-028382; abstract and Results/Table 2; Table 3 continuation (printed p. 7) for reasons. Publisher-indexed full-text PDF text inspected; BMJ endpoint did not download locally.

**Supports / cannot support.** Supports that ratings for the same trials differed across reviews and that interpretation of incomplete reporting often explained differences. It is about the earlier instrument and cross-review reproducibility, not RoB 2 within-team reliability or instruction effects. Different information availability may also contribute.

**Prose option.** “With the earlier Cochrane tool, ratings of the same trials across reviews agreed in 57%–81% of item-specific comparisons; most classified disagreements reflected different interpretations of the same information, often incomplete or unclear reporting.[4]”

### Soboczenski 2019 (reference 22)

**Design and sample.** Prospective randomized within-subject user study. Forty-one participants each assessed four articles: two with RobotReviewer suggestions and two manually. The task covered four domains of the earlier Cochrane tool (RoB1), dichotomized low versus high/unclear.

**Verified facts.** 82 assisted and 82 manual article assessments. Mean time was 755 seconds (SD 868) per assisted article and 824 seconds (SD 727 in Table 3) per manual article; a participant-random-effect model estimated relative time 0.75 (95% CI 0.62–0.92), described as 25% faster. The Results prose gives manual SD=717, whereas Table 3 gives 727; use Table 3 or omit SD. Users changed 27/328 (9%) assisted domain judgments; edits are not a correctness measure.

**Locator and source.** Primary full text, PMC6505190, DOI 10.1186/s12911-019-0814-z; Methods “Study design” and “Outcomes”; Results and Table 3, printed pp. 6–7; Table 4 for judgment changes. XML and extracted text saved at /tmp/arbiter-r6-fulltexts/reliability/soboczenski2019.xml and .txt.

**Supports / cannot support.** Supports direct measurement of reviewer time and behavior; in this RoB1 task setting, assistance was faster. It does not establish ARBITER or RoB 2 time savings; label acceptance/edit behavior does not prove evidence support.

**Prose option.** “In a randomized within-reviewer study of four RoB1 domains, 41 participants completed 82 assisted and 82 manual article assessments; assistance reduced modeled assessment time by 25% (relative time, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.62–0.92).[22]”

### Jardim 2022 (reference 23)

**Design and sample.** Real-time mixed-methods implementation in two ongoing reviews at a national public health institute; six reviewers and 26 RCTs. Project leaders declined a manual arm, so the randomized comparison was between two RobotReviewer platforms, not automation versus manual review. The tool used RoB1.

**Verified facts.** Acceptance of RobotReviewer versus colleague judgments during consensus was similar when dichotomized (RR 1.02; 95% CI 0.92–1.13; p=0.33). Acceptability was mixed, with less experienced reviewers generally more positive. Although reviewers tracked time, authors could not compare use against manual practice.

**Locator and source.** Primary full text, PMC9174024, DOI 10.1186/s12874-022-01649-y; abstract; Methods “Participants and included systematic reviews” and “Data collection and analysis”; Results and Discussion/Methodological considerations. XML and extracted text saved at /tmp/arbiter-r6-fulltexts/reliability/jardim2022.xml and .txt.

**Supports / cannot support.** Supports treating adoption/acceptability as separate from output agreement and measuring actual workflow. It does not support time savings; RR is acceptance behavior, not independent accuracy; sample is six reviewers in two projects using RoB1.

**Prose option.** “In a real-time study of six reviewers across 26 trials, acceptance of RobotReviewer judgments was similar to acceptance of a colleague’s (RR 1.02; 95% CI, 0.92–1.13), but acceptability was mixed and no manual arm was available to estimate time savings.[23]”

## Synthesis and manuscript use

Use Minozzi 2020 and 2022 to frame a bounded interpretation/application problem, not a universal assertion that RoB 2 is unreliable. Keep random-trial sampling (2020) distinct from a single clinically focused review team (2022). Use Bertizzolo as evidence of cross-review disagreement under the older tool and a plausible mechanism: interpretation of incomplete reporting. Use Soboczenski and Jardim to explain why time, user behavior, and acceptability need separate evaluation. Neither establishes ARBITER reviewer benefit.

Do not compare the Bertizzolo 57%–81% item agreement, Minozzi Fleiss κ, ARBITER exact label agreement, Soboczenski task-time estimate, and Jardim acceptance RR as if they had a common denominator or estimand. Include each metric only with its design, unit, instrument version, and comparator. ARBITER’s 7.6-minute runtime per trial is computational runtime; it is not human verification time and cannot be compared as workload benefit to the cited human-task studies.

## Narrative/style note

The clinical-writing skill applies Schimel’s reader-first story structure: open with the reviewer/evidence-synthesis problem, synthesize the exact known boundary (interpretation varies; one tailored calibration sequence was followed by higher agreement), then state the unresolved question and action. Do not lead with ARBITER before establishing the problem. Resolve only what the benchmark measures; its runtime and label agreement do not demonstrate reviewer benefit.

The requested writing examples were checked as structural examples, not evidence sources. For Ali Salman, the relevant article is Usman et al., “The effect of sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors on left cardiac remodelling in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction,” European Journal of Heart Failure 2024 (DOI 10.1002/ejhf.3129); PubMed lists Salman among the authors and its affiliations include Dow University of Health Sciences. Its Introduction moves from established clinical benefit to a disputed remodeling explanation, identifies inconsistent/small prior studies and prior-review population mixing, then states the review question. For Irbaz Bin Riaz, Sonbol et al., “Systemic Therapy and Sequencing Options in Advanced Hepatocellular Carcinoma,” JAMA Oncology 2020, is a clinically framed review; its opening moves from a concrete treatment-comparison problem to the role of the review. Riaz et al., “Disorganized Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses,” Am J Med 2016, begins with a field-level publication problem and a focused methods response. The bibliographic search did not identify a paper coauthored by Ali Salman and Irbaz Bin Riaz. These are editorial observations about selected texts, not proof of either author’s personal style preferences; no wording has been copied and no clinical number from these examples is used in the manuscript argument.
