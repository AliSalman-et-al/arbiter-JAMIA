## What the paper can say with concrete evidence

The most useful literature-backed Introduction claim already cited by the manuscript is that systematic reviews are slow to produce and can become outdated as evidence accumulates. Riaz et al.'s 2024 *Mayo Clinic Proceedings: Digital Health* perspective states that reviews require 6–18 months and need frequent updates (Introduction ¶1; based on its cited literature). It supports motivation for automation and living synthesis; it does not demonstrate that ARBITER accelerates reviews or makes living synthesis effective. Preserve the citation at that claim and do not turn this broad review-cycle estimate into a measured RoB 2 task-duration claim.

The manuscript's motivation can then narrow to the actual assessment work: result-specific RoB 2 judgments require selecting the relevant outcome, comparison and analysis, extracting and interpreting evidence across reports and supporting documents, and applying domain rules. The Riaz perspective is a source for the broad evidence-synthesis context; RoB 2 task-specific claims should remain grounded in the instrument and existing risk-of-bias literature already cited in references [1–4], rather than attributing detailed RoB 2 workflow facts to that perspective.

For prior automation, distinguish what a system makes inspectable from whether its evidence is relevant or interpreted correctly. The current manuscript's discussion of RobotReviewer, ROBOTO2 and RoBGuard already provides that bridge. Keep the manuscript's concrete ARBITER mechanism close to the claim: text selections are matched against captured source text and source slices are stored; this limits arbitrary fabricated quotation wording in that pathway, but does not establish relevance, completeness, extraction fidelity or interpretation. Image descriptions are model-written. Software applies decision rules to recorded answers but cannot correct a wrong answer. These mechanisms and boundaries come from the project implementation record/interview, not from Riaz's papers.

For results narrative, the already verified ARBITER values should stay tied to their comparator and denominator: in the 102-trial, 510 trial–domain-cell benchmark, Expert 1 comparison is 371/510 (72.7%) versus 332/510 (65.1%) with paired gain 7.6 percentage points (95% CI 5.1–10.2); Expert 2 comparison is 433/510 (84.9%) versus 400/510 (78.4%) with paired gain 6.5 points (95% CI 3.5–9.4). These are full-cohort exact-agreement results (manuscript Results, “Independent expert ratings,” paragraph 1; Figure 4). The conditional disagreement set contains 80 cells; experts agreed on 60 and split on 20; among the 60 agreed choices, 53/60 (88%; 95% CI 80–96%) matched ARBITER (Results, “Blinded label choices,” paragraph 1; Table 2). The 53/60 figure addresses only the conditional set where experts agreed. It cannot support a general accuracy or superiority claim. The Published-label Results paragraph reports another 510-cell full-cohort comparison and should precede these expert comparisons as the author directed (Results, “Published-label comparison,” paragraph 1). These results are from the single-run paired benchmark, not independent repeated runs. Denominator discipline is consonant with the examples below; it is not a reason to cite those articles for ARBITER's own results.

## Paragraph maps: Ali Salman, MBBS / Dow match

**Mahmood et al., Saudi Medical Journal 2024**, “The effect of having a physician in the triage area on the rate of patients leaving without being seen.” *Saudi Med J* 45(1):74–78. DOI 10.15537/smj.2024.45.1.20230674. Publisher full text, PDF pages 74–78; downloaded PDF and layout extraction retained outside the repository.

1. **Introduction, p. 74, paragraphs 1–3:** starts with a defined operational problem (ED crowding), links it to care processes and patient departure, then moves from prior definitions/previous findings to the local question about a triage physician. The useful move is not the broad crowding claim itself; it is the progression from clinical operation to a specific measurable outcome. The paper relies on citations for general stakes, then states its local study question.
2. **Results, p. 75, “Results,” paragraphs 1–3; Table 2, p. 77:** establishes the analysis population (7,826 patients) and event count (140 LWBS; 1.8% overall), then compares the three months: 2.2% (64/2,854), 2.3% (53/2,321), and 0.9% (23/2,651), with table p<0.001. It separately reports time to physician (median 30 minutes, IQR 16–52 overall; monthly values 31, 31 and 27 minutes) and non-significant differences in disposition time and length of stay (Table 2, p. 77). The paragraph structure gives the outcome and comparison before drawing attention to statistical significance; the table carries precision and units. These are descriptive pre/post-period results in one Saudi hospital, not a randomized causal estimate.
3. **Discussion, p. 75–76, first and limitations paragraphs; conclusion p. 78:** interprets the local pattern, compares it with cited studies, then explicitly names null outcomes and plausible contextual factors. The limitations paragraph returns to short duration, one hospital and residual confounding. This is a practical model for keeping a favorable result alongside outcomes that did not change, and for bounding transfer. The concluding causal-sounding phrase “reduces the likelihood” is stronger than the observational before/after design alone can establish; do not copy that claim width.

**Writing pattern that may transfer:** state the clinical process, identify the outcome that makes the problem measurable, keep event count and denominator near the percentage, then let the Discussion explain both positive and null findings. **Counterexample/boundary:** one paper cannot establish a stable personal voice; it is an emergency-department quality-improvement study unlike an AI-methods benchmark. Its title and conclusion use causal-sounding “effect/reduces” language for before/after evidence, so ARBITER should not imitate that calibration. The article's front matter thanks an English-language editor, another reason not to attribute every sentence-level choice to Ali.

## Paragraph maps: Irbaz Bin Riaz corpus

These are grounded in the prior round's source inspection and synthesis, not new claims about private preference. Three works offer especially transferable structure; reported paragraph locators refer to body text, excluding abstract/captions, in the cited version.

1. **Riaz et al., “Future of Evidence Synthesis: Automated, Living, and Interactive Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses,” 2024, Introduction ¶¶1–3 and task-specific subsections.** The opening supplies the review-cycle problem and outdatedness, then moves through what automation has and has not covered. The paper next describes literature search, study selection, extraction, risk of bias, meta-analysis, certainty and narration step by step, before proposing a living interactive framework. This operational sequence can help the ARBITER Introduction go from evidence-synthesis need to the particular appraisal step. It is a methodological perspective and proposed framework, not empirical evidence of automation benefit. Its 6–18 month estimate concerns systematic reviews broadly, not risk-of-bias assessment specifically.
2. **Khan et al., “Collaborative large language models for automated data extraction in living systematic reviews,” JAMIA 2025, Introduction ¶¶1–3; Methods workflow/cross-critique; Results concordance; Discussion limitations.** The article moves from the upkeep burden to a specific extraction bottleneck, explains the multistep workflow and human review, and organizes results by the kind of comparison and evidence being checked. It distinguishes extraction performance and disagreement handling instead of compressing the system to one “accuracy” label. The detailed full-text inspection and mapped locators are documented in the project census/synthesis; the full-text XML retrieval attempted in this round was unavailable. Riaz is last/corresponding author, but no paragraph-level drafting credit was found. Use as team-article structure only.
3. **Riaz et al., “Empirical evaluation of artificial intelligence distillation techniques for ascertaining cancer outcomes from electronic health records,” npj Digital Medicine 2025, Introduction ¶¶1–4; Results “Error analysis of student-only models trained with synthetic data” (body paragraph containing AUROC/AUPRC/F1 and generated-sequence examples); Discussion limitations paragraph; conclusion.** The Introduction moves from missing longitudinal outcome information to the privacy/scalability issue and then to the precise unresolved question about training-data composition and distillation strategy. Methods define the cohort/outcomes before teacher/student operations. Results retain outcome and configuration labels; the Discussion names dataset and subgroup constraints. The paper reports 99,318 radiology reports in the Discussion limitations paragraph while explaining why direct de-identification would still have substantial resource demands. The Results error analysis says synthetic-data reports sometimes contained unrealistic sequences (e.g., complete response after progression), and reports validation/test performance separately. This supports a structure that preserves counterexamples and dataset constraints; the article does not treat a proposed safeguard as proof of complete privacy or transportability. Exact detail is in the PMC full-text body, Results and Discussion (HTML has no stable printed page locator); XML is retained under `/tmp`. Riaz is first/corresponding, but original drafting is shared with other authors.

**Recurring article-level practice, not “Riaz’s style”:** define the problem first; explain unfamiliar operations in the order they happen; keep reference, outcome and metric close; distinguish one evaluation property from another; state the limits beside the conclusion. Counterexamples matter: the 2024 perspective uses broad advocacy/future framing; npj methods are dense; multi-author contributions weaken individual attribution. The sole-author 2026 JCO overview also uses expansive, future-oriented language and was available only through publisher-indexed passages in the prior synthesis, so it counters any stereotype of uniformly restrained prose but is not a full-text paragraph model.

The prior publication-history synthesis says the JAMIA work's full published PMC text was inspected during prior project research, while this turn's direct full-text retrieval failed. Riaz BMJ ROBINS-I paper (2024; DOI 10.1136/bmjebm-2023-112597) has a publisher contributor note saying Riaz co-wrote the first draft with three others, but the article body was inaccessible (403); it is not used for paragraph-level analysis. These access levels must not be conflated.

## Schimel application

The clinical-research-writing skill and its glossary operationalize Schimel's OCAR (Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution), “develop bottom-up, tell top-down,” and the instruction to stop at the highest evidence rung warranted. Applied here: open with the evidence-review decision context; state the exact challenge in appraising result-specific bias; describe the ARBITER pathway as the action; resolve with reference-specific agreement and the distinction between inspectability, rule conformance and correctness. The existing manuscript already follows this general arc. Use Schimel as an editorial framework, not a source for clinical facts or a substitute for the manuscript's verified study record. The task's requested “deeper citation-driven argument” should be made with the cited studies' exact populations, designs and endpoints, not by stacking headline percentages from incomparable work.

## Recommendation to the parent

The highest-value change is not a new headline statistic: it is a citation-backed funnel that distinguishes the general 6–18 month review-cycle context from the work of result-specific RoB 2 appraisal, then states the unsolved evaluation issue precisely—retrieved/source-linked evidence and a plausible label do not establish that the evidence was appropriately interpreted. Keep the system mechanism and its limits adjacent. In Results, preserve the author-confirmed order and the 510-cell vs conditional 80/60/20 denominators. Avoid comparing ARBITER's agreement percentages with other studies' headline rates: populations, tasks, reference standards and units differ.
