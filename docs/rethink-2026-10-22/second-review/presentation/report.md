## Judgment

The settled direction is sound, but the proposed Figure 4 is carrying too many unlike tasks: rate levels, paired contrasts, two expert references, between-expert variability, published-reference comparisons, and derived Overall. This is not a cosmetic crowding problem. A reader cannot infer “closer agreement” from a difference plot alone, and cannot see its scale or expert-to-expert context without absolute rates. Yet the full-cohort Published comparison should not receive equal visual weight because it answers a different reference question. I recommend reserving Figure 4 for the central expert comparison and using Figure 5 for diagnostic explanation; give the Published contrast a compact aligned row in Figure 4 or a separate sixth figure if at journal column size the row becomes illegible. Do not retain the current Figure 5B directional stacked bars in the main paper: they depend on post hoc strictness strata and risk inviting the rejected target-match stratification logic.

The verified headline numbers support this hierarchy. Across 510 domain cells, exact agreement was 371/510 (72.7%) versus 332/510 (65.1%) against Expert 1, and 433/510 (84.9%) versus 400/510 (78.4%) against Expert 2. Paired differences were +7.6 points (95% CI 5.1–10.2) and +6.5 (3.5–9.4). Experts agreed on 388/510 (76.1%; weighted κ .59). Domain levels expose material variation: for Expert 1, D3 exact agreement was 42.2% vs 36.3%, while D5 was 73.5% vs 53.9%; against Expert 2, D3 was 78.4% vs 72.5% and D5 80.4% vs 69.6%. These are descriptive domain patterns, not causal localization or external validation. The D3 cross-expert level is particularly different (52.9% agreement, κ .43 in the current display export), which should caution against one pooled performance adjective.

The formally primary result remains a separate conditional proposition. In 80 cells where arms differed, experts agreed on a forced choice in 60; ARBITER matched 53/60 (88.3%; 95% CI 79.7–96.3), with 20 split choices reported as such. The result is not population accuracy. Do not put this rate on the same visual scale as 510-cell exact agreement without an unmistakable population label and separate panel. Its prominence belongs in Table 2 and a concise abstract clause; Figure 3 should show the selection path.

Published labels need a clear but subordinate disclosure. Their paired exact-agreement contrast was −1.8 points (95% CI −5.1 to 1.6), while agreement with the two independent experts favored ARBITER. Published labels were heavily concentrated in Low (427/510, 83.7%). This comparison teaches that conclusions depend on the reference; it cannot show published labels are wrong or that one significant contrast differs from another nonsignificant one. Preserve the full cohort and the qualitative target uncertainty. Do not create matching strata. Current Figure 5’s directional panel reports only 88 stricter and 36 more-lenient ARBITER cells against Published and is post hoc; its partial denominators must not become an informal proxy for the rejected matching analysis.

## What the books actually support

These are applications of the passages inspected, not empirical rules from the books. Schimel’s *Writing Science*, chapters 2 and 4 on story structure and chapter 9 on resolution, treats the result as needing to resolve the opening question and give the reader a changed understanding. The manuscript should therefore open with the need for inspectable and defensible evidence appraisal, then resolve with the bounded point that an auditable workflow can preserve a checkable record and show closer expert agreement in this benchmark, while uncertainty across domains and reference standards remains. “We ran 102 trials and found significant coefficients” is action/result, not resolution. The current working ending still risks making the Published-reference issue sound like a second paper; keep it as an interpretive qualification.

Heard’s *The Scientist’s Guide to Writing*, chapter 7 (“Finding and Telling Your Story”), argues for choosing the question and answer before selecting supporting material; his clarity discussion frames writing as transfer of an argument to a reader. Applied here, first define the reader’s sequence, then assign exactly one job to each display. Hindsight can reorder exposition, but it cannot relabel analyses as prespecified; preserve the frozen plan chronology and use the correct scope wording for the all-cell comparisons.

Zinsser’s *On Writing Well*, “Unity” and “Clutter,” supports choosing one organizing idea and removing material that does not serve it. The choice to report only the Sonnet workflow is therefore more than a word-count decision: it protects the reader’s mental model of the intervention. The current literature matrix does not earn a supplementary table in its present form. Its heterogeneous “Yes/Partly/NR” definitions and unresolved `[ref: ...]` placeholders make ARBITER’s all-Yes row look like a sales comparison. Replace it with a short sourced narrative on prior approaches, or rebuild it only if every feature has an operational definition, primary citation, and reproducible evidence trail. A supplement is not a safe place for a weak display.

## Transfer from JAMIA and medical-informatics papers

Three useful, limited precedents are: the clinician-in-the-loop reproducible evaluation framework in *JAMIA Open* (2025), which explicitly connects a multi-expert rating workflow to consensus handling and keeps response-level evidence in a supplement; a *JAMIA Open* patient-safety-event evaluation strategy paper (2024), whose title and evaluation design foreground that evaluator strategy is itself a methodological contribution; and a recent JAMIA clinical decision-support evaluation paper that presents task-stage performance and uncertainty in separate displays rather than one composite score. See [the clinician-in-the-loop framework](https://academic.oup.com/jamiaopen/article/8/3/ooaf054/8163901), [the patient safety event evaluation strategy](https://academic.oup.com/jamiaopen/article/7/4/ooae128/7888965), and [the JAMIA clinical decision-support evaluation](https://academic.oup.com/jamia/advance-article/doi/10.1093/jamia/ocag171/8858988?searchresult=1). The first supports making the independent-first / conditional consensus paths explicit; its consensus procedure is different and should not be copied. The second supports stating the evaluation design as part of the contribution; it does not validate ARBITER’s checks. The third supports pairing estimates with intervals and clearly naming task configurations; it evaluates extraction, not ordinal RoB judgments. None is a template for colors, fonts, or panel shape. These papers support explicit populations and uncertainty, not stylistic imitation.

## Proposed paper architecture

**Title.** Keep “ARBITER: an auditable workflow for AI-assisted risk-of-bias assessment.” It names system and task without implying clinical validation. Avoid “accurate,” “automated review,” or “saves time.”

**Abstract.** Background: appraisal outputs need inspectable evidence and rule application. Objective: describe ARBITER and evaluate its complete Sonnet workflow against the same model with a minimal prompt and the same files. Methods: 102 trials, 510 domain cells, two blinded independent-first experts, then separately identify the 80 discordant cells and 60 agreed forced choices. Results: give absolute and paired all-cell exact agreement against each expert, followed by the formal primary 53/60 (88.3%) conditional result, then state domain variation and the Published-reference contrast. Conclusion: auditable record plus benchmark-specific improvement in expert agreement; difficult ratings remain; no human workflow effect or clinical correctness tested. Do not call exact agreement “accuracy.”

**Introduction.** Paragraph 1 explains why risk-of-bias appraisal matters to interpretation of evidence. Paragraph 2 states the inspectability problem: a final rating hides source selection, interpretation, and rule translation. Paragraph 3 introduces ARBITER’s model/software division, ending with the distinction between procedural checks and judgment quality. Paragraph 4 gives the question and complete workflow contrast, identifying the independent expert evidence and formal conditional endpoint without claiming confirmatory status beyond the dated record.

**Methods.** Order: system and evaluated version; benchmark and same-model comparator; independent-first expert sequence and blinding; outcomes with explicit population definitions; statistical methods and clustered bootstrap; then process, structural verification, and computational resource measures. Explain why Published labels are a separate reference and their uncertain target correspondence. Do not bury the forced-choice eligibility rule. State that no human-time benefit or clinical use was measured.

**Results.** First sample and design (Figure 3, Table 1). Second the primary forced-choice result and sensitivities (Table 2). Third all-cell absolute agreement and paired contrasts against each expert, with reliability context (Figure 4). Fourth domain/question interpretation, focusing D5 gain and D3 sensitivity to the expert reference (Figure 5). Fifth the Published-reference contrast in a subordinate row or sixth figure. Last separate procedural checks from computation in Table 3. Report estimates in prose only when they move the story; point to displays for the full pattern.

**Discussion.** Open with the answer in one sentence. Then interpret the complete workflow contrast, discuss heterogeneity and expert variability, explain why Published-reference comparison is different, and distinguish inspectability from semantic validity. Close with actual contribution and limits: one model workflow, benchmark sample, no human-use evaluation, no clinical correctness claim.

## Figure 4 stress test and choice

**Alternative A, recommended: two aligned panels with a compact reference strip.** Panel A is a rate plot, x-axis exact agreement 0–100%, rows ordered pooled D1–D5 then D1–D5, split Expert 1 and Expert 2 into two adjacent columns. Plot ARBITER and Unassisted as distinct shapes and colors on each row; add thin 95% CIs and print numerator/denominator at right for pooled rows only. In each expert facet, a short neutral vertical or interval marker on the pooled row indicates expert-to-expert agreement (76.1%); label it “expert pair” rather than target or ceiling. Panel B is the paired percentage-point difference ARBITER minus Unassisted for the same five domains and pooled row, with a zero reference. Reuse the exact domain order and reference facets, allowing direct reading from level to contrast. Below these panels put one thin, clearly separated Published row for the pooled paired contrast (−1.8 points, 95% CI −5.1 to 1.6) and caption it “secondary reference comparison; all 510 domain cells.” Overall is omitted from Figure 4 and identified as derived/secondary in Table 2 or supplement. This uses common 0–100% and −20 to +25 point scales with zero centered; do not truncate the rate axis. Add 95% intervals; caption identifies trial-resampled-within-meta-analysis percentile bootstrap and exact n. Colors are colorblind-safe blue/orange, with circle/square redundancy; expert identity is facet headings, not color. Keep gridlines faint, no shaded significance regions, direct labels, minimum 8–9 pt at final size. White space separates the reference strip. The two panels teach “how much agreement?” and “how much paired gain?”; the strip teaches that the Published result differs without elevating it to a co-primary question.

**Alternative B: three vertical panels, each expert separately.** Show two expert facets with paired rates and difference inside each, plus third compact Published contrast. It is more immediately comparable to experts, but labels and CI bars become cramped at single-column width and a third axis makes scanning harder. It is acceptable only at full-width landscape size.

**Alternative C: main dot-and-interval plot of paired differences plus a small table of absolute pooled rates.** This is compact and suitable if the journal limits figure height, but it leaves domain absolute agreement in the supplement and makes the main visual support a contrast detached from baseline levels. I do not recommend it because domain-level absolute levels are central, especially the lower D3 agreement.

At final publication size, use a single sans-serif family, consistent with Figure 1/2’s strong dark text and restrained categorical palette, but do not reproduce Figure 2’s saturated evidence yellow as statistical encoding. Figure 1’s separate colored lanes and Figure 2’s explicit distinction between quoted evidence, context, inference, model interpretation and software checks provide a useful semantic grammar. The new statistical figures should preserve that explicit labeling discipline, not their exact illustration style. Tables and captions carry denominators, metric definitions and bootstrap detail; panels carry direction, scale, reference, estimates and uncertainty. Use redundant shape encoding and alt text that states the pattern without claiming correctness.

## Reviewer objections to preempt

1. “The 88.3% is accuracy.” Answer in title/abstract/table/caption that this is conditional agreement among 60 agreed choices selected from 80 discordant cells.
2. “Two expert assessments are ground truth.” Call them independent expert references; show them separately and show their 76.1% agreement, without treating it as a ceiling.
3. “The workflow itself caused the gain.” Attribute only to the complete Sonnet workflow contrast; no component effect follows.
4. “Published reviewers were wrong.” State only that exact agreement did not show the same advantage against Published labels; target match is uncertain and no matching strata were used.
5. “The process audit proves evidence correctness.” Distinguish structural source/quote checks from whether evidence supports the model’s interpretation.
6. “ARBITER saves reviewers time.” The current resource table reports API cost and system runtime. Human effort was not measured.
7. “The results generalize to models or clinical use.” This study reports one Sonnet workflow on this benchmark; no alternative-model manuscript claim or clinical use effect.

**Do not claim:** accuracy, expert equivalence, consensus ground truth, universal domain gains, component-specific benefit, published-label error, human time saved, clinical validity, model generalization, or confirmatory prespecification where the dated record does not establish it.
