# Reference validity and the OCAR story

Status: **ISSUES**. The core system-level result is defensible with careful wording, and the manuscript already reports several safeguards and limitations. The current design does not establish population accuracy or clinical validity. The most consequential methodological issue is whether the 102-trial benchmark specifies the individual outcome result each RoB 2 assessment is meant to judge. That is a construct-validity limitation if the benchmark labels pool, infer, or mismatch outcomes, and it cannot be cured by better agreement statistics.

This is a discussion-stage review. No data, analysis, manuscript, glossary, or artwork was changed. Figures 1 and 2 need no change for the reference-validity argument; keep them intact while the story is reconsidered.

## What RoB 2 means by a valid target

RoB 2 assesses the risk of bias of a **specific result** from a randomized trial: a particular intervention comparison, outcome, measurement method, time point, and numerical result, interpreted for the effect of assignment or adherence. It is not a trial-level quality score. Domain 5 in particular can change according to the outcome and result selected. The official Cochrane version remains the 22 August 2019 parallel-trial tool and guidance. See the [official current RoB 2 page](https://www.riskofbias.info/welcome/rob-2-0-tool/current-version-of-rob-2), the full-text development paper, [Sterne et al., BMJ 2019](https://www.bmj.com/content/366/bmj.l4898), and the [Cochrane Handbook chapter](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-08). The BMJ article and handbook are full text; the official page links the full guidance and cribsheet.

The benchmark describes judgments by trial × domain and uses labels from source meta-analyses, while the manuscript acknowledges that some published labels are broad, inferred, or may concern another result. This matters more than any metric choice: a published label and a new assessment may both be internally consistent but target different outcomes, analyses, or time points. The De Waal registry mismatch is an observed document-applicability defect; the broader result-mismatch concern is documented but its frequency and effect on the current results have not been quantified in the material reviewed. Do not describe all published disagreement as evidence that the meta-review is wrong.

**Practical implication:** Before interpreting disagreement as model error or model correction, show, for every trial, the target result available to the two arms, experts, and published label: outcome, time point, measure, contrast, estimate or unique result locator, and effect-of-interest choice. A result-matching table can be generated from existing benchmark files and documents if those fields are present. Mark “not identifiable” where they are absent, rather than guessing. Recalculate key comparisons in the subset with defensibly matched targets if existing metadata allow it. This does not require new experiments, but may expose a benchmark that supports system workflow comparison more strongly than RoB 2 accuracy claims.

## Reference reliability and what the present design supports

The published labels are a useful comparator, not a gold standard. The local analysis plan already supports this distinction: it reports agreement against both experts and published labels and describes all three as comparison sources. Literature on imperfect references supports reporting the reference construction and dependence rather than calling a composite or published review “truth.” Naaktgeboren et al. discuss composite reference-standard limitations in the [full-text BMJ paper](https://www.bmj.com/content/347/bmj.f5605); Hadgu's 1996 Lancet note on discrepant analysis is available here by [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8774575/) and was checked as an abstract, not full text. These are diagnostic-method analogies, not direct validation studies of ordinal RoB judgments.

The stronger evidence is the independent-first expert procedure: both experts rated all 510 D1–D5 cells before seeing labels, labels were blinded and order randomized per cell, and experts could revise their ratings later. The manuscript reports 388/510 independent domain agreements (76.1%; weighted κ 0.59), only 54/102 agreement in D3, and 42/81 agreement for derived Overall. These are direct reliability observations, not proof that the agreed label is correct. Two experts provide a thin reference panel; κ varies with category prevalence and the sampled cases, and its 0.59 value is not a ceiling or a reliability correction factor.

Forced choice is a separate endpoint. It asks which of three available labels an expert finds most defensible, after the independent rating has been frozen and without rationales. This is a useful blinded preference task, but it restricts choice to the offered labels, which may omit a valid RoB 2 category when sources duplicate one another, and can anchor choice to offered options, and label-level credit can award both sources if their labels match the selected option. The observed primary result, 53/60 consensus choices matching ARBITER (88.3%), estimates preference **within selected arm-discordant, adjudicated cells**. It is not accuracy across the 510 cells. The 60-cell denominator and selection rule are essential to its interpretation. Independent expert rating agreement across all cells is a valuable parallel result because it is not restricted to the forced-choice consensus subset, though an individual expert is still an imperfect reference.

The manuscript's expert mean is descriptive, not a pooled panel estimate. The analysis plan's expert-to-expert line is appropriately a reliability yardstick, not an upper bound. Report each expert separately alongside the mean, and keep the consensus forced-choice result distinct from independent ratings and revised ratings. A reviewer could reasonably ask whether the large forced-choice share reflects a real preference, a constrained label set, or both. Existing independent ratings and each-expert forced-choice results are the available checks; revised ratings should remain descriptive because experts saw the offered labels first.

## Demonstrated defects versus design limits

| Finding | Classification | Evidence and implication |
|---|---|---|
| Supplied De Waal registry record belonged to another trial | Demonstrated source-applicability defect | The manuscript says this occurred. Preserve it as a concrete example; state whether the record entered any final assessment and what correction was made. Do not generalize from one incident to a corpus-wide rate. |
| Published labels may be broad, inferred, or refer to another result | Benchmark/construct limitation; extent not yet measured | The manuscript acknowledges this. Audit label provenance and target matching before asserting that a published disagreement exposes error. |
| Two experts; domain κ 0.59, D3 54/102; Overall 42/81 agreement | Demonstrated limited observed agreement | Report estimates with denominators and intervals if available. Do not imply that consensus is a verified truth label. |
| Three-option forced choice | Design limitation, not a data-processing defect | Preference is conditional on the offered options. Independent ratings provide an unrestricted-label sensitivity. |
| Two Sonnet arms, one run each | Design limitation | No estimate of stochastic run-to-run variability. Do not imply repeatability across calls, seeds, days, or model versions. |
| Unassisted used a minimal task/format prompt; ARBITER received an assessment skill with RoB 2 guidance | Comparator/design limitation | The contrast supports a complete-workflow claim only. It cannot assign effect to software, guidance, tool access, or processing time. This is stated in the manuscript. |
| No semantic verification that cited passages justify answers; generated image descriptions unverified | Demonstrated scope of audit, not proven answer error | Occurrence in stored source and structural bundle verification do not establish evidential support or correct interpretation. Keep auditability separate from clinical correctness. |
| Experts could consult sources beyond arm inputs | Information-access asymmetry | It may explain some differences; it is not evidence of expert error. Report source access and frame it as an evaluation condition. |

Single runs, minimal prompt, small expert panel, forced choices, outcome mismatch risk, and shared-source/shared-model errors are design limits, not defects to “fix” by reanalysis. The documented wrong-trial registry is a defect. The result-matching question is a material benchmark validity issue, but the files reviewed do not establish how many cases are mismatched.

## Endpoint hierarchy and manuscript framing

The analysis plan makes only forced-choice consensus on discordant cells confirmatory. It labels the rest secondary or exploratory, with no p-values or multiplicity correction. Keep that hierarchy visible. A reviewer may challenge a confirmatory endpoint that is a small selected subset and chosen from three offered labels; the appropriate response is precise estimand language and transparent secondary evidence, not calling the endpoint accuracy or expanding its claim.

The strongest available story is system evaluation under two references with explicit reliability limitations:

1. ARBITER, as a complete system, was preferred over the minimal-prompt unassisted arm on the prespecified selected discordant cells.
2. It also had higher independent-rating agreement than that arm against each expert across all domain cells, while absolute agreement differed between experts.
3. Agreement with published labels did not clearly separate the arms, and the direction of apparent strictness changes with the reference.
4. The study supports a workflow-level comparison and an argument for measuring reference reliability. It does not prove ARBITER's true RoB 2 accuracy, isolate a component effect, or establish that published reviews generally underrate bias.

An acceptable concise conclusion would say that “ARBITER showed higher agreement with two blinded experts than a minimal-prompt unassisted model on this benchmark, while agreement with published labels did not distinguish the systems.” The discussion should add the narrow forced-choice estimand and result-specific target caveat. Avoid “more accurate,” “outperformed human reviewers,” and claims that the published labels are wrong in general.

Existing expert label-choice data can only describe this adjudication task. | DECIDE-AI applies to early-stage live clinical use; its absence is not a reporting failure for this offline benchmark. Human-AI use-effect claims require a different study. |

Feasible sensitivity outputs from the existing workbook and analysis plan: (a) expert-specific unrestricted independent ratings over all 510 cells; (b) the same comparisons limited to the forced-choice eligible subset, to reveal the effect of choice constraint; (c) agreement and paired contrasts by domain, with D3 prominent; (d) retain split expert choices as a separate category and report pessimistic treatment; (e) primary preference with numerator/denominator, neither credit, and trial-cluster interval; (f) matched-result subset if the metadata supports it; (g) exclude any judgments informed by the misassigned De Waal registry document and report the impact; and (h) reframe all agreement outcomes as agreement with a named reference, never truth accuracy.

Do not add model run replicates, new experts, factorial prompting, or a prospective human-AI trial to this paper's analysis plan. Those are follow-on designs, not feasible analyses of existing records.

## Fit with JAMIA and reporting guidance

The current [JAMIA author instructions](https://academic.oup.com/jamia/pages/General_Instructions) classify system formulation, implementation, and evaluation as “Research and Applications,” which fits this paper. The journal asks for a structured abstract with Objective, Materials and Methods, Results, Discussion, and Conclusion, a Background and Significance section, and currently lists a 4,000-word main-text limit, 250-word abstract, and up to six figures. That supports a system-and-evaluation manuscript. Keep the main result tightly framed, use supplement material for audit detail, and retain Figures 1 and 2 as requested. JAMIA requires a data availability statement and repeatable analyses using archived data; the manuscript currently marks repository and DOI details as pending, so that is a submission blocker rather than a scientific conclusion.

The manuscript cites GRRAS and TRIPOD-LLM. This is reasonable as reporting support but should not be presented as methodological validation. Original [GRRAS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2010.03.002) is the reliability/agreement reporting guideline; its checklist asks readers to understand the design, sample, raters, measurement procedure, and statistical methods. The currently published guideline is undergoing a [GRRAS-COSMIN update](https://www.grras-cosmin.org/), so describe the 2011 guideline as the version used, not as current best-practice consensus without qualification. Full text of the original article was not verified in this review; the checklist and abstract were located.

[TRIPOD-LLM](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-03425-5) explicitly covers evaluation studies of LLMs and provides a reporting checklist. The full-text article is available; it states that the checklist is for transparent reporting, not a recipe or standard for evaluation quality. It fits reporting of model identity, prompts, data, evaluation, human involvement, and limitations, but RoB 2 result-level definition, reference selection, and paired inference need their substantive methods sources too.

The [DECIDE-AI consensus guideline](https://www.bmj.com/content/377/bmj-2022-070904) addresses early-stage live evaluation of AI decision-support in actual clinical practice, including human factors. It is not a direct reporting checklist for this offline retrospective benchmark; its human factors concepts support the manuscript's explicit statement that staff review time, corrections, and automation bias were not measured. Do not claim DECIDE-AI compliance as a badge of quality here.

## Actionable revision order

1. Verify result-specific target identity before strengthening any RoB 2 validity claim. Add a transparent matchability table or state that the benchmark operates at a trial-label rather than fully result-specific level.
2. Use the term “published label” consistently and “imperfect comparator” in interpretation. Avoid “ground truth,” “accuracy,” and generic claims of published-review underestimation.
3. Keep the primary forced-choice result but label it as conditional preference on the eligible discordant cells. Pair it with the independent expert ratings, expert-specific estimates, and observed expert agreement.
4. State in the abstract and conclusion that the evaluated contrast is ARBITER workflow versus a minimal-prompt model, based on one run per trial. Do not attribute improvement to the server or the model alone.
5. Preserve the current manuscript's careful distinctions among quote occurrence, structural verification, answer support, and clinical correctness. The audit verifies traceability properties, not semantic justification.
6. Complete the expertise/training description, assessment form/procedure supplement, data availability archive and DOI, and reporting checklist before submission. Current manuscript placeholders make those items unresolved.
