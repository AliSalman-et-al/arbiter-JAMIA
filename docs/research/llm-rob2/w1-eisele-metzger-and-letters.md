## Eisele-Metzger et al. 2025, Research Synthesis Methods: Claude 2 and RoB 2

- **DOI / IDs**: [10.1017/rsm.2025.12](https://doi.org/10.1017/rsm.2025.12); medRxiv [10.1101/2024.07.16.24310483](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.07.16.24310483); PMC12527486; PMID 41626932; OSF project [2phyt](https://osf.io/2phyt/); protocol [42dnb](https://osf.io/42dnb/).
- **Full text read**: Yes. Read the publisher’s full 18-page article, Europe PMC full-text XML, and the medRxiv full-text PDF text. The preprint reports the same core study; figures/tables/pagination differ from the journal version. The article says all three prompt templates were uploaded to OSF in advance (prompt 12 was the final main template), and the paper’s supplementary-material record was identified. However, the linked DOCX supplement and prompt files could not be retrieved in this environment; prompt details below therefore come from the full article and medRxiv full text, not an inspection of the prompt text itself.
- **Design**: Registered proof-of-concept validation study (OSF registration 11 September 2023); testing was in February 2024. Protocol changes: simplified search strategy, different PDF text-conversion tool, and additional exploratory analyses not prespecified.
- **Sample**: Random sample of 100 English-language, two-arm parallel RCTs published 2013–2022, across 78 Cochrane reviews. One selected outcome/comparison per trial (first suitable listed). Fifty non-pharmacological/non-surgical, 44 pharmacological, 6 surgical; 18 COVID-19 trials. Protocol found for 32, registry entry for 82, and neither for 16. Pilot set of 30 trials from 3 reviews excluded from validation.
- **Tool**: RoB 2, five domains plus overall. For Domain 2 the study assessed the effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat), not the effect of adhering to intervention (per-protocol).
- **Models and dates**: Claude 2, tested February 2024, temperature as low as possible, three iterations per RCT; tie/frequency selection used a random draw. Sensitivity analysis with Claude 3 Opus in March 2024. Two alternative prompts also tested.
- **Inputs**: Automatically injected full text extracted from the trial PDF; no additional trial reports. Compressed protocol/analysis plan if available, otherwise registry entry if available. Individual outcome and time point specified. Human Cochrane reviewers had access to more complete protocols and supplements and could contact authors (made explicit in the 2026 response).
- **Prompting / system design**: A prompt template based on detailed official RoB 2 guidance, deliberately avoiding the tool name to reduce risk of recalling Cochrane judgments in training data. Separate prompts for each domain; then five model responses concatenated and given to a separate overall-judgment prompt. Requested justifying text in JSON. A step-by-step chain-of-thought-style prompt and a shorter “minimal” prompt were sensitivity conditions. Patchbay automated prompt assembly and protocol compression; it is software orchestration, not an agent using tools against a validating server.
- **How domain judgements were produced**: Claude itself stated each of the five domain judgments and the overall judgment. The paper does **not** describe eliciting RoB 2 signalling-question answers and calculating domain judgments from those answers. Official guidance/instructions were included in the prompt, but there was no rule-based domain computation from structured signalling responses.
- **Rule-consistency checked?**: Yes, but narrowly: authors manually checked all 100 overall judgments for consistency with the RoB 2 overall algorithm. Two clearly violated it: Claude rated overall risk “low” while at least one domain was “some concerns.” This did not assess whether domain judgments followed the signalling-question/domain algorithms. The study does not report a systematic rule-consistency rate for domain outputs.
- **Evidence handling**: Asked for explanatory text per judgment. Authors reviewed justifications, but no per-answer source quotation requirement, quote verification, immutable stored source, or machine-auditable evidence trail. For all 18 extreme low-vs-high domain disagreements, authors consulted original reports; for a sample of 10 intermediate disagreements per domain, they compared supplied justifications without rechecking original reports, for feasibility.
- **Reference standard**: Published domain/overall judgments and supporting text from the relevant Cochrane reviews, treated as the human reference standard; the authors also call it “imperfect.” They acknowledge low and variable RoB 2 inter-rater reliability and cite human agreement results (including Fleiss κ 0.16 in one study and 0.42 after topic-specific implementation guidance/calibration in another). They say “true” RoB 2 judgments are unknown; Cochrane judgments were a pragmatic external reference given the perceived methodological quality of most Cochrane reviews. They propose a purpose-built expert reference standard as future work, while recognizing resource demands. No independent expert-panel reference was created in this study.
- **Adjudication of disagreements**: No blinded, prospective adjudication of model-vs-reference disagreements. Authors reviewed discrepancies; among 18 low-vs-high domain discrepancies, they judged 12 should instead be “some concerns.” That is exploratory author review, not a blinded consensus reference panel. In the other discrepant cells, only a random sample of 10 per domain was reviewed and not checked against source reports.
- **Headline results**: Agreement with Cochrane labels: 41% overall, domain range 58% (D5 selective reporting) to 71% (D4 outcome measurement). Weighted κ: overall 0.22 (95% CI 0.06–0.38); D1 0.11, D2 0.12, D3 0.31 (0.10–0.52), D4 0.15, D5 0.10. Their interpretation: slight-to-fair agreement. Low-risk sensitivity ranged 0.50–0.90 and specificity 0.16–0.75 across domains; high-risk sensitivity/specificity were relegated to supplement. Claude’s outputs skewed toward “low risk” for each domain (88, 77, 70, 78, 64 of 100, respectively) and emitted high-risk rarely. In the 18 extreme discrepancies, all but two were high risk by Cochrane vs low risk by Claude, hence the model usually rated less severe in those extreme disagreements. The article does not establish a general directional bias across all outcomes. Claude’s three runs disagreed for 32/100 RCTs.
- **Repeatability, time, cost**: One complete run (three iterations for each of 100 RCTs) took about 2 hours. No monetary cost reported. Three-run judgments varied for 32 trials. Authors note reproducibility limitations from model-output variation and model evolution.
- **Authors' stated limitations**: “comparing RoB 2 judgements of Claude to an ‘imperfect’ human reference standard” is problematic; the true judgments are unknown. Other limits include fluctuating outputs, differences in information available to model and reviewers, English-only/recent RCTs, and the study not testing other supportive workflows. Authors state that Claude should not be used as a stand-alone RoB tool.
- **Authors' conclusions**: Claude’s judgments cannot replace human RoB assessment; further work should explore support beyond stand-alone ratings, such as extracting relevant trial content. They suggest expert reference standards as one direction and note human RoB judgment is subjective and imperfect.
- **Quotable sentences**:
  - “Our prompt template asked Claude 2 to assess the RoB of the respective RCT, considering each of the five domains of the RoB 2 tool and to provide an overall judgement.” (Methods 2.3.2; confirms the model supplied ratings directly.)
  - “Therefore, we checked the 100 overall judgements of Claude for compliance with the algorithm provided in the RoB 2 guidance.” (Methods 2.4; this was an overall-only check.)
  - “Thus, comparing RoB 2 assessments by Claude to this ‘imperfect’ and variable reference standard obviously is problematic.” (Discussion, p. 504.)
  - “Future studies could explore the impact of choosing a different reference standard, for example, a purposely created expert reference standard.” (Discussion, p. 504.)
- **Bearing on G1–G5**:
  - **G1 supports gap, with qualification**: model output domain labels directly; detailed official algorithm guidance was in the prompt, and the authors checked overall algorithm compliance (2/100 violations), but no signaling-answer-to-domain computation or domain algorithm consistency audit.
  - **G2 supports gap**: narrative justifications, but no verified quotes, bound evidence, immutable source, or auditable record.
  - **G3 partly contradicts / qualifies gap**: uses Cochrane labels as reference, but explicitly discusses their imperfect reliability and uncertainty; does not measure/correct label error or replace them with independent consensus.
  - **G4 partly contradicts / qualifies gap**: reviewed disagreements against source material in 18 extreme cases and asserted 12 warranted “some concerns,” but no blinded expert adjudication of all disagreements.
  - **G5 supports gap**: Patchbay automates prompt assembly and compression; assessment itself is prompt-based, not a tool-using agent backed by a server that verifies evidence and applies algorithms.
- **Implication for ARBITER manuscript**: Avoid claiming prior work never checked the RoB 2 algorithm: this paper manually checked all overall ratings and found 2/100 violations. The precise gap is that it did not derive domain judgments from recorded signalling answers or audit domain-level rule consistency. Likewise, qualify G3/G4: the authors acknowledged reference noise and conducted limited discrepancy reviews, but did not create a blinded expert consensus reference or adjudicate all disagreements.

## Sahu 2026, Research Synthesis Methods: Five methodological considerations

- **DOI / IDs**: [10.1017/rsm.2026.10073](https://doi.org/10.1017/rsm.2026.10073); PMID 41641991.
- **Full text read**: Yes. Read the full 3-page open-access letter PDF from Cambridge Core.
- **Design**: Letter to the editor offering methodological critique and recommendations; no new empirical study or preregistration.
- **Sample**: None; discusses Eisele-Metzger et al. and prior reliability/LLM studies.
- **Tool**: RoB 2 in the target study; not an assessment study itself.
- **Models and dates**: None newly evaluated. Refers to Eisele-Metzger’s Claude 2 study and related work.
- **Inputs**: No model inputs; argues that Claude lacked supplements and complete protocols relative to Cochrane reviewers.
- **Prompting / system design**: Not applicable. Recommends transparent, ongoing validation and augmentation workflows.
- **How domain judgements were produced**: Not applicable; no new judgments.
- **Rule-consistency checked?**: Not applicable.
- **Evidence handling**: Not applicable. Raises information parity: the Claude evaluation used compressed protocols/registry entries and excluded supplementary materials, whereas Cochrane reviewers could consult wider sources.
- **Reference standard**: Criticizes use of existing Cochrane RoB labels as an imperfect reference because human agreement is low: cites RoB 2 overall κ 0.16 among experienced raters and κ 0.02 between Cochrane and external panels (the latter study used the original Cochrane tool, not RoB 2). Says it is unclear how much model-vs-Cochrane disagreement is model error versus noise in the reference. Recommends expert-panel consensus using a prespecified, piloted protocol, citing other evaluations. This is a proposal, not a reference standard applied by Sahu.
- **Adjudication of disagreements**: None conducted. Recommends human-in-the-loop and panel-based consensus standards.
- **Headline results**: No new metrics. Notes the target study’s overall κ 0.22 and domain κ 0.31 for missing data vs 0.10 for selection of reported result; questions performance heterogeneity and asks for qualitative analysis of disagreement causes and trial-level modifiers.
- **Repeatability, time, cost**: No new measurements. Notes augmentation could improve practical utility; cites review timelines and asks whether using AI as one of two assessors with human discrepancy resolution saves time.
- **Authors' stated limitations**: N/A as an empirical study. The critique identifies imperfect references, information asymmetry, limited exploration of performance heterogeneity, and temporal validity as methodological concerns.
- **Authors' conclusions**: Recommends coordinated validation with (1) standardized expert-consensus benchmarks, (2) assessment of augmentation/workflow integration, (3) strict information parity, (4) qualitative investigation of heterogeneity, and (5) continuous evaluation as models evolve.
- **Quotable sentences**:
  - “What proportion of disagreements stemmed from human inconsistency versus genuine model error?” (p. 1054.)
  - “A more valid approach would compare the LLM against a consensus from a panel of experts using a prespecified, piloted protocol” (p. 1054.)
  - “Future evaluations must ensure LLMs and human raters operate on identical information sets to draw valid conclusions about comparative performance.” (p. 1055.)
- **Bearing on G1–G5**:
  - **G1 not addressed**: no analysis of how domain decisions are computed from signalling answers.
  - **G2 not addressed**: discusses information available to reviewers, not passage verification or auditability.
  - **G3 contradicts any claim that the concern is wholly unrecognized**: explicitly challenges Cochrane labels as noisy reference standards, though it does not quantify/correct their error.
  - **G4 supports gap / proposes future direction**: calls for expert-panel consensus, but performs no blinded adjudication itself.
  - **G5 not addressed**: does not evaluate an agent/tool-server design.
- **Implication for ARBITER manuscript**: Cite Sahu as evidence that reference-label noise and expert-consensus references are already recognized methodological issues, not as evidence that previous work solved them. ARBITER’s blinded disagreement adjudication is a concrete implementation of the proposed need for a better reference, while its limitations should be distinguished from a full consensus gold standard.

## Eisele-Metzger et al. 2026, Research Synthesis Methods: Response to Sahu

- **DOI / IDs**: [10.1017/rsm.2026.10102](https://doi.org/10.1017/rsm.2026.10102); open-access Cambridge Core letter, pp. 1057–1059.
- **Full text read**: Yes. Read the complete publisher HTML full text.
- **Design**: Authors’ response to Sahu’s methodological letter; no new empirical study or preregistration.
- **Sample**: None; responds to the 100-RCT Claude 2 study.
- **Tool**: RoB 2 in the underlying study.
- **Models and dates**: No new model run. Clarifies that the underlying evaluation was conducted February 2024.
- **Inputs**: No new inputs; explicitly confirms the model had article full text plus a compressed protocol or registry entry when available. Human Cochrane reviewers could also consult supplements, contact authors, and inspect full protocols.
- **Prompting / system design**: No new prompt/system experiment. Acknowledges that Claude struggled with lengthy protocols and that compression helped focus the input on the study itself.
- **How domain judgements were produced**: No new judgments or changed account of the prior methods. In conjunction with the 2025 Methods, confirms the prior direct model ratings and overall algorithm check; it does not claim signaling-question-based code derivation.
- **Rule-consistency checked?**: No new check. The original study’s overall-only algorithm check remains the evidence (2 of 100 overall judgments clearly inconsistent); no domain-level computation/audit is described.
- **Evidence handling**: Acknowledges information imbalance between model and Cochrane reviewers; supplying every ancillary source for 100 trials was considered infeasible. No claim of verified quotations or audit trail.
- **Reference standard**: Explicitly acknowledges limitations of published Cochrane judgments given modest inter-rater reliability, but defends them as a feasible external reference in light of the high methodological quality of most Cochrane reviews. Reiterates that a purpose-built expert reference standard/panel could be used in future, at substantial time and resource cost. Thus, they accept the premise of reference noise while defending their pragmatic choice; no new panel is created and no errors are corrected.
- **Adjudication of disagreements**: No new adjudication. Agrees with Sahu that human-in-the-loop/semi-automated workflows may be practical and suggests initial rough assessment or flagging important report sections for human verification.
- **Headline results**: No new results. Restates domain heterogeneity (highest κ for missing data, next outcome measurement, lowest selective reporting) and says prompt/model/subgroup sensitivity analyses did not show consistently strong performance.
- **Repeatability, time, cost**: No new measurements. Acknowledges need for model/version/date reporting and plans for reevaluation; no cost analysis.
- **Authors' stated limitations**: Information imbalance, compressed rather than complete protocols, modest human inter-rater reliability, non-exhaustive heterogeneity analyses, and rapid model evolution. They say complete ancillary information for 100 RCTs was infeasible and lengthy protocols challenged Claude.
- **Authors' conclusions**: Accept augmentation as a practical direction and welcome further testing. Defend Cochrane assessments as the most suitable feasible external reference for their project, while recognizing expert consensus as a stronger possible future standard with added cost.
- **Quotable sentences**:
  - “As we suggested, future work could employ a purpose-built expert reference standard (e.g., from a panel of experts), although this would also require considerably more time and resources.” (section 1.)
  - “Given the high methodological quality of most Cochrane reviews, the assessments by various Cochrane authors were arguably best suited for our project to establishing an external and feasible reference standard.” (section 1.)
  - “We agree that a ‘human-in-the-loop’ or semiautomated approach may currently be the most practical way to harness the judgments made by a large language model (LLM).” (section 2.)
  - “This consequently resulted in a slight imbalance in the information available to LLMs and human reviewers.” (section 3.)
- **Bearing on G1–G5**:
  - **G1 supports gap**: response adds no domain-level rule execution or consistency audit; original study only checked final overall ratings.
  - **G2 supports gap**: confirms full-text plus compressed ancillary documents, but no verified evidence-binding/audit mechanism.
  - **G3 contradicts an absolute “without measuring or discussing error” version of the gap**: the authors explicitly acknowledge reference reliability limitations and discuss the tradeoff, but do not measure/correct Cochrane-label error or change their reference.
  - **G4 partly contradicts / qualifies gap**: supports human review/augmentation as a future workflow but offers no blinded adjudication; prior discrepancy review was limited and unblinded.
  - **G5 supports gap**: no agentic or server-verified workflow tested.
- **Implication for ARBITER manuscript**: State plainly that Eisele-Metzger et al. recognized label imperfection and proposed expert panels, so ARBITER’s novelty is not merely “noticing” reference noise. The contribution is empirically re-examining selected disagreements with blinded adjudicators, alongside deterministic scoring and evidence checks. Do not characterize their exploratory 12-of-18 discrepancy assessment as a blinded expert-panel standard.

## Worker summary

These papers support G1, G2, and G5: the Claude 2 study requested domain ratings directly, checked only overall algorithm consistency, and lacked evidence verification/audit tooling and an agentic server architecture. G3 needs a narrower claim: Eisele-Metzger et al. explicitly called Cochrane labels an imperfect reference and discussed the reliability problem, while Sahu emphasized it and proposed expert consensus. Yet none of these three papers measured or corrected the reference-label error using a blinded panel; the response defended Cochrane labels as a feasible external comparator and acknowledged the added cost of a purpose-built panel. G4 is also qualified: the 2025 authors reviewed extreme discrepancies and found 12 where they would choose “some concerns,” but this was not blinded or comprehensive adjudication. The key surprise is that the target article did perform an overall-rule check (2/100 violations), so the clean algorithm gap is domain-level derivation from signalling answers and rule-consistency checking—not a blanket claim that prior work ignored the algorithm.
