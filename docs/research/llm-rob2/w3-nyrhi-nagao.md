## Nyrhi et al. 2026, EBioMedicine: comparative validation study

- **DOI / IDs**: 10.1016/j.ebiom.2026.106238; PMC13054287; PMID 41905260; preregistration OSF DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/K45C7.
- **Full text read**: Yes, Europe PMC full-text XML. The article identifies a supplementary PDF (“eFigures and eTables”) and OSF project; the article XML itself was available, but the supplement contents were not retrieved in this run. The preregistration record was checked via OSF API (created 2025-03-11, modified 2026-03-10); the full protocol text was not downloaded. Thus details beyond the reported protocol description are not independently verified against the protocol/supplement.
- **Design**: Preregistered comparative validation/reliability study, data collected March 11–May 19, 2025. Primary outcome Cohen κ; secondary intraobserver agreement and diagnostic accuracy.
- **Sample**: 200 RCTs total: 100 for RoB 1 from recent Cochrane reviews and 100 for RoB 2 from meta-analyses in high-impact journals. RoB 2 selection used The Lancet, JAMA, Annals of Internal Medicine, and BMJ: chronologically first eligible meta-analyses (at least five RCTs), then alphabetically first 10 eligible studies from each until reaching 100. Results describe six RoB 2 reviews: internal medicine (2), vascular surgery (2), cardiology (1), pulmonary medicine (1), published 2023–2024. Compare ARBITER's 102 trials from 16 meta-analyses: Nyrhi selected chronologically/alphabetically within a fixed set of four journals; selection was not random and is clustered in six reviews. Nyrhi's RoB 2 corpus is therefore notably smaller and less broadly clustered by source review than ARBITER's.
- **Tool**: Cochrane RoB 1 and RoB 2; RoB 2 domains plus overall judgement. No assignment-effect subgroup is described in the accessible article text.
- **Models and dates**: ChatGPT o3 (May 2025 build), DeepSeek v3 (April 2025), Grok 3 (April 2025), Gemini Flash 2.0 (April 2025); base web-chat modes, no additional features.
- **Inputs**: Trial full-text PDFs and PDF protocols when available, directly uploaded. DeepSeek had a protocol deviation (article points to a “Deviations from Protocol” section). Protocol retrieval was a major limitation; authors report models failed to retrieve public protocols even after identifying registration numbers.
- **Prompting / system design**: Standardized prompt adapted from Lai et al.; full prompt in eAppendix 2. One assessment per model/trial, then a repeat in a new session/account for intraobserver reliability. No agent or tool/server workflow reported.
- **How domain judgements were produced**: Model generated RoB judgements under the prompted RoB framework; no indication that answers were algorithmically converted into ratings in code.
- **Rule-consistency checked?**: No reported check that final model judgements followed the RoB 2 algorithm given the model's signalling answers.
- **Evidence handling**: PDFs supplied; no passage-level quotation verification, immutable stored source, or audit trail described.
- **Reference standard**: Published human RoB judgements from included meta-analyses, called the “gold standard.” Authors explicitly did not audit the underlying review assessments. They acknowledge reference error as a limitation and note human reviewers' own low agreement (typically κ 0.15–0.20 for RoB 2).
- **Adjudication of disagreements**: No independent blinded adjudication of LLM-versus-reference disagreements reported.
- **Headline results**: RoB 2 overall κ ranged 0.06 (ChatGPT o3; 95% CI −0.07 to 0.18) to 0.13 (Gemini; −0.04 to 0.31). Across models, high-risk sensitivity 0.05–0.55, specificity 0.78–0.99, PPV 0.31–0.50, NPV 0.48–0.61. Sensitivity for any-concern/high was 0.47–0.96. The Stuart–Maxwell test found category distributions shifted toward “Some concerns/High” for every model (raw and adjusted p<0.001). Thus models over-flagged relative to published labels, mainly by excess Some concerns/High labels; this does not establish that models were wrong, because the reference labels were not audited. Authors say that imputing the published Domain 5 value where protocols were unavailable improved agreement markedly for some comparisons, but did not improve overall RoB 2 κ; they interpret the remaining discordance as “primarily from overly stringent classification rather than protocol retrieval failures.” This interpretation is relative to the published reference, not an adjudicated truth. Article text gives domain-specific κ in Fig. 1 and ChatGPT o3 discordance in Fig. 2, but does not state per-domain disagreement direction in prose; supplement was not accessible here, so no domain-by-domain direction is asserted.
- **Repeatability, time, cost**: RoB 2 intraobserver κ ranged 0.34–0.52. Time and cost were not reported.
- **Authors' stated limitations**: Reliance on published RoB judgements that may contain error; chronological/alphabetical sampling may introduce selection bias; no speciality/date subgroup analyses; clustering within few reviews; κ can understate agreement with prevalent categories; possible contamination from models' training on the meta-analyses used as reference.
- **Authors' conclusions**: Models were not reliable enough for independent RoB assessment; use should be supervised triage or a second assessor, with human resolution of disagreements. They call for protocol retrieval, task-specific tuning, calibrated thresholds, prospective validation, and monitoring.
- **Quotable sentences**:
  - “The protocol was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/K45C7) and is reproduced in eAppendix 1.” (Methods, Study design)
  - “We selected chronologically the first meta-analyses containing at least five RCTs and included alphabetically the first 10 studies per meta-analysis until we totalled 100 RTCs.” (Methods, Data sources and selection criteria; source typo “RTC”)
  - “Stuart–Maxwell rejected equality of category proportions for all models (raw and BH-adjusted p < 0.001), indicating a systematic shift toward ‘Some concerns/High’ relative to the gold standard.” (Results, Secondary outcomes)
  - “The main limitation is reliance on the gold standard reference, i.e., published RoB judgements that themselves may carry error.” (Discussion)
- **Bearing on G1–G5**:
  - **G1 supports gap**: prompted RoB judgements, but no code-applied algorithm or rule-consistency audit reported.
  - **G2 supports gap**: direct PDF input, without verified quote binding, stored-source verification, or audit record.
  - **G3 supports gap, with caveat**: published review judgements used as gold standard and not audited; authors do acknowledge their possible error.
  - **G4 supports gap**: no blinded expert adjudication of disagreements described.
  - **G5 supports gap**: standalone web chat and uploads, no tool-using agent/server architecture.
- **Implication for ARBITER manuscript**: Cite as a strong contemporary comparison using full reports/protocols, preregistration, repeated runs, and a similar high-impact-meta-analysis sampling frame. The key distinction is that its over-flagging is measured against unaudited published ratings; do not describe it as proven model error. State the selection-frame difference (four named journals, six RoB 2 reviews, 100 trials vs 16 meta-analyses and 102 trials) and avoid a claim about domain-specific error direction without inspecting the supplement.

## Nagao et al. 2026, American Journal of Perinatology: reliability of LLM RoB evaluation

- **DOI / IDs**: 10.1055/a-2793-9092; PMID 41713863.
- **Full text read**: No. Europe PMC full-text endpoint returned an error and the Thieme page was inaccessible to retrieval. PubMed abstract and bibliographic metadata were available; all details below are limited to those sources. No supplement or author manuscript located/accessed.
- **Design**: Retrospective reliability study; abstract does not report preregistration.
- **Sample**: 180 RCTs from systematic reviews published in the *American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology* (2021–2023) with complete human RoB 2 ratings.
- **Tool**: Cochrane RoB 2; abstract discusses five domains (D1–D5) and overall judgement. Assignment effect vs adherence not reported in abstract.
- **Models and dates**: ChatGPT-4o, ChatGPT-5, Claude 3.5 Sonnet. Model access/build dates not available in abstract.
- **Inputs**: Full-text PDFs, according to abstract. Whether protocols, registries, supplements, or appendices were included is not stated in the accessible abstract.
- **Prompting / system design**: Standardized prompt incorporating the complete RoB 2 algorithm; three independent runs per LLM. No chain-of-thought, retrieval, or agent/tool workflow specified in abstract.
- **How domain judgements were produced**: Model assessments under a prompt containing the complete algorithm. Whether model stated final judgements or a separate program applied the algorithm is unknown from the abstract.
- **Rule-consistency checked?**: Not reported in accessible abstract; cannot infer that including the algorithm in the prompt entailed a consistency check.
- **Evidence handling**: Full-text PDFs processed. Quote requests, verification, source storage, and audit trail unknown/not reported in abstract.
- **Reference standard**: Human RoB 2 ratings from the source systematic reviews. Whether their reliability was measured or challenged is unknown from the abstract.
- **Adjudication of disagreements**: Not reported in accessible abstract.
- **Headline results**: ChatGPT-5 agreement rates: D1 76%, D3 80%, D4 76%, D2 69%; selective reporting D5 agreement 47–51% across models. Overall ChatGPT-5 agreement 60–62%, κ=0.36–0.40; ChatGPT-4o 45%; Claude 3.5 Sonnet 43%. Abstract does not give direction of disagreement (over- vs under-flagging), sensitivity/specificity, or full domain breakdown by model. Authors attribute D5 limitation as likely due to inability to cross-reference external trial registries.
- **Repeatability, time, cost**: Three runs; ChatGPT-5 internal consistency described as substantial to near-perfect. Time and cost not reported in abstract.
- **Authors' stated limitations**: Abstract emphasizes selective reporting / lack of external registry cross-referencing and need for expert oversight. Full limitations unavailable because full text could not be obtained.
- **Authors' conclusions**: ChatGPT-5 most closely approximated human assessments and had strongest internal consistency; it may be a first-pass tool to reduce reviewer burden, but expert oversight remains necessary.
- **Quotable sentences**:
  - “Each LLM processed full-text PDFs using a standardized prompt incorporating the complete RoB 2 algorithm.” (Abstract, Study Design)
  - “However, all models struggled with selective reporting (Domain 5), where agreement dropped to 47 to 51%.” (Abstract, Results)
  - “persistent limitations in detecting selective reporting-likely due to the inability to cross-reference external trial registries-highlight that expert human oversight remains essential for accurate evidence synthesis.” (Abstract, Conclusion; punctuation as displayed in PubMed)
- **Bearing on G1–G5**:
  - **G1 indeterminate / likely supports gap**: complete algorithm was included in prompt; accessible material does not report code-applied judgements or rule-consistency checks.
  - **G2 supports gap on reported evidence, but full text unavailable**: PDF processing is reported; passage verification and auditability are not mentioned in abstract.
  - **G3 indeterminate / supports gap on reported evidence**: human review ratings are benchmark; abstract does not report auditing their reliability.
  - **G4 supports gap on reported evidence**: no adjudication is reported in abstract; full text unavailable.
  - **G5 supports gap on reported evidence**: standardized prompting and PDF processing are described; no agent/server or tools are described in abstract.
- **Implication for ARBITER manuscript**: This is a useful 2026 comparator because it uses the full RoB 2 algorithm in its prompt, three runs, and includes Claude 3.5 Sonnet. Keep the comparison bounded to abstract-level evidence until full text is obtained; do not claim its algorithm implementation, registry access, disagreement direction, or validation safeguards beyond what PubMed reports.

## Worker summary

Both papers support G2–G5 as stated on the accessible evidence; neither reports verified evidence binding, an auditable server, blinded adjudication, or a tool-using agent. Nyrhi directly supports G1: it used a standardized prompt and compared model-generated ratings to published labels without a reported algorithmic enforcement or answer-to-rating consistency check. Nagao includes the complete algorithm in its prompt, a meaningful prior-art qualification, but the abstract does not establish that judgements were computed in code. Nyrhi is especially relevant to the manuscript's reference-noise argument: it explicitly recognizes that published labels may be wrong, while still designating them the gold standard. Its over-flagging finding is relative to those labels, and its authors' “overly stringent” explanation is not adjudicated ground truth. Nagao's full text remains necessary to assess whether its methods contain additional safeguards or domain-level direction results.
