## Gandhi et al. 2026, PLoS One: “Evaluating large language model performance in Risk of Bias assessments: A cross-sectional validation study”

- **DOI / IDs**: 10.1371/journal.pone.0353155; PMC13349098; PMID 42424352.
- **Full text read**: yes; Europe PMC full-text XML. The linked S1 supporting DOCX could not be retrieved: the PMC binary route returned a reCAPTCHA page. Thus supplementary methods, figures, and tables (including the full prompt and per-trial model responses) were not independently inspected; the article text and its published tables were read.
- **Design**: Methodological validation; reported under TRIPOD-LLM. Not preregistered (“A formal study protocol was not pre-registered”). PubMed search for meta-analyses using RoB 2, then random selection of reviews and one RCT from each.
- **Sample**: 50 RCTs, one each from 50 published meta-analyses; trial publication years 1998–2025; diverse medical specialties. Full trial reports were assessed across five RoB 2 domains and overall judgment. One outcome per trial was implicitly the published review assessment basis; the methods describe trial-level RoB ratings.
- **Tool**: Cochrane RoB 2.0, with Low risk / Some concerns / High risk ratings for five domains and overall. The paper does not report evaluating effect-of-assignment versus effect-of-adhering variants as a separate factor.
- **Models and dates**: ChatGPT-o3, standard ChatGPT web interface, April 20–May 20, 2025; default generation settings. One new chat per trial.
- **Inputs**: Human panel consulted available trial registry entries, especially for selective reporting. The model received the complete trial PDF and had web browsing enabled; authors say it could theoretically find registries cited in the manuscript, but conclude the instruction to rely strictly on reported information may have inhibited registry use. The paper does not describe supplying protocols or supplementary files to the model.
- **Prompting / system design**: Structured prompt combining RoB 2 guidance and a template from Lai et al.; instructed model to use the RoB 2 cribsheet signaling questions and branching logic and avoid extrapolating beyond reported information. Independent single-shot chats. No RAG, agent, or server tools reported. The full prompt is in the inaccessible S1 supporting file.
- **How domain judgements were produced**: The model generated RoB assessments. No separate deterministic implementation of the RoB 2 algorithm applied in code to model signaling answers is reported. The study elicited judgments using the signaling questions/branching logic in the prompt, but the paper does not establish that the final labels were computed externally.
- **Rule-consistency checked?**: No explicit check that every domain judgment followed the RoB 2 algorithm given the model’s signaling answers; no rule-consistency result reported.
- **Evidence handling**: Complete PDFs were uploaded. The paper does not report requiring quotations, verifying quotations against stored source copies, or maintaining a machine-checkable evidence/audit record.
- **Reference standard**: Three-person masked human panel’s assessment, resolved by consensus or a fourth author, was the diagnostic reference for model metrics. The panel was masked to OSRA judgments; the model was masked to both panel and OSRA ratings. The authors explicitly state the panel was “a rigorous expert consensus rather than an indisputable objective ground truth” (Statistical analysis). They selected it to reduce possible optimism/conflicts affecting OSRAs, while recognizing the subjectivity of RoB judgments and low human interrater reliability. This directly measures disagreement between an independent re-assessment and published labels, rather than assuming published labels are truth.
- **Adjudication of disagreements**: The human panel re-assessed all 50 RCTs independently; this was not limited to model-versus-OSRA disagreements. Three named reviewers worked independently, masked to the OSRA assessments; their own disagreements were resolved by consensus discussion or a fourth author. They did not report a separate blinded adjudication restricted to disagreements between the model and OSRA labels. The panel’s final consensus was then compared with OSRAs and model.
- **Headline results**: ChatGPT-o3 called 34% of trials high risk, versus 22% for the panel and 12% for OSRAs (Results). Median weighted κ was 0.33 for model vs panel and 0.14 for model vs OSRA; overall three-rater Gwet AC2 was 0.30. Table 2 gives overall κ 0.23 (95% CI 0.00–0.43) for model–panel, 0.36 (0.15–0.55) for OSRA–panel, and 0.14 (−0.04–0.30) for model–OSRA. The discrepancy between abstract median κ and overall κ is because the abstract reports median agreement across six judgments; use table-specific values when comparing an overall rating. High-risk detection against panel: sensitivity 0.46, specificity 0.69, balanced accuracy 0.57 (Table 3). Low-risk detection: sensitivity 0.47, specificity 0.86, balanced accuracy 0.66 (Table 4). Model was more conservative than both human sources. The authors characterize OSRAs as tending to be more lenient, and speculate that implicit incentives may contribute (Discussion); the observed high-risk prevalence is consistent with this, but does not establish why.
- **Repeatability, time, cost**: Fresh chat per trial to avoid context carryover. No repeated runs, inter-run reproducibility, time, or cost measured. Authors cite general reproducibility/update concerns in their discussion.
- **Authors' stated limitations**: The paper discusses model/reference subjectivity and human-panel variability; near-random agreement in Domain 2; possible underuse of registry data by the model; prompting may drive conservative classifications; and the panel is not objective truth. It also notes that the work is a sample of 50 and compares a single model/version. Verbatim: “Such inherent subjectivity complicates the designation of any single human rating as an objective gold standard” (Discussion). The model’s use of web browsing was theoretical and not shown to retrieve registries.
- **Authors' conclusions**: ChatGPT-o3 is unsuitable as a sole assessor but may be an adjunct. Its conservative flags may prompt human verification, while moderate sensitivity means it will miss some high-risk trials.
- **Quotable sentences**:
  - “However, we acknowledge that RoB assessments possess inherent subjectivity; therefore, this reference represents a rigorous expert consensus rather than an indisputable objective ground truth.” (Methods, Statistical analysis)
  - “ChatGPT-o3 classified 34% of trials as high risk, compared with 22% by our panel, and 12% by the OSRAs.” (Abstract, Results)
  - “Such inherent subjectivity complicates the designation of any single human rating as an objective gold standard.” (Discussion)
  - “OSRAs tended toward more lenient ratings, possibly reflecting implicit incentives to classify studies as lower risk, thereby increasing overall certainty and strength of conclusions.” (Discussion; authors’ interpretation/speculation)
  - “While unsuitable to be used as a sole assessor, ChatGPT-o3 may serve as an adjunct tool to enhance the efficiency and consistency of RoB assessments in systematic reviews.” (Abstract, Conclusion)
- **Bearing on G1–G5**:
  - **G1 Algorithm enforcement — supports gap**: signaling logic was described in the prompt, but no code-applied algorithm or consistency audit is reported.
  - **G2 Evidence traceability — supports gap**: PDFs and possible web browsing, but no verified source-bound quotations or audit record reported.
  - **G3 Reference noise — contradicts the broad version; narrows gap**: this study explicitly recognizes published OSRA labels may be biased/lenient and uses an independent masked panel as its reference. It does not claim an objective gold standard.
  - **G4 Adjudication — partially contradicts / narrows gap**: a masked expert panel independently re-assessed every trial and internally resolved reviewer disagreements; however, it did not adjudicate only model–published-label disagreements in a dedicated blinded process.
  - **G5 Agentic/tool design — supports gap**: independent chat sessions with prompt and browser access, no tool-using agent/server workflow reported.
- **Implication for ARBITER manuscript**: Gandhi is the closest prior design for addressing reference-label noise: it demonstrates that an independent masked panel can rate more trials high risk than published reviewers and explicitly rejects an unquestioned gold-standard framing. Differentiate ARBITER by its targeted adjudication of discordant domain cells, executable rule enforcement, verified evidence binding, and audit file; avoid claiming the first independent human re-assessment or first recognition that OSRA labels can err.

## Taneri 2025, Cochrane Evidence Synthesis and Methods: “Human Versus Artificial Intelligence: Comparing Cochrane Authors' and ChatGPT's Risk of Bias Assessments”

- **DOI / IDs**: 10.1002/cesm.70044; PMC12407253; PMID 40908961.
- **Full text read**: yes; Europe PMC full-text XML, including article tables. Supplementary Table S1 is identified in the full text, but its linked DOCX was not independently retrievable (the PMC binary route returned a reCAPTCHA page). Main-paper domain-specific accuracy and kappa tables were available.
- **Design**: Feasibility/validation comparison of ChatGPT-4o ratings with Cochrane authors’ ratings. No formal sample size calculation; assessments by one researcher. No preregistration reported in the article text.
- **Sample**: Eight eligible Cochrane reviews yielded 84 RCTs, published 1993–2021. Primary outcome in each review was assessed at the outcome level. Sixty of 84 (71.4%) came from one Parkinson’s exercise review. Twenty-nine reviews were excluded for using the older RoB tool; additional exclusions included protocols and non-intervention/alternative-tool reviews.
- **Tool**: RoB 2, five domains plus overall, with low / some concerns / high labels; primary outcome assessment, consistent with RoB 2’s outcome-level framing.
- **Models and dates**: ChatGPT-4o, queried January 2025. The model is described as continuously updated, limiting exact reproducibility.
- **Inputs**: ChatGPT received published study reports only. The authors explicitly say it was not given trial protocols, registry entries, or related publications. Cochrane authors’ assessments were the comparison reference. Prompts/data form were shared in OSF.
- **Prompting / system design**: A sequence of structured prompts corresponding to RoB 2 signaling questions; ratings plus predicted direction of bias; a final prompt summarized and re-evaluated consistency. Prompt adapted from Lai et al. and RoB 2 cribsheet. Single researcher conducted model assessments sequentially. No agent, tools, RAG, or external-source retrieval.
- **How domain judgements were produced**: ChatGPT returned ratings after answering signaling-question-based prompts; the paper does not report applying the RoB 2 decision algorithm in code. A final model prompt checked/summarized consistency, rather than an external executable rules engine.
- **Rule-consistency checked?**: A final prompt asked ChatGPT to summarize its assessments “for consistency,” but no test against the formal RoB 2 algorithm or rate of algorithm-inconsistent labels is reported.
- **Evidence handling**: No request for verified quotations or passage-level evidence binding is reported. The model had study reports only; no source storage verification or audit trail reported.
- **Reference standard**: Cochrane authors’ consensus-based assessments were treated as the comparison reference; the study calculated agreement/diagnostic measures against these labels. It recognizes human interrater reliability may be low and, in its conclusion, recommends establishing that reliability before direct comparisons. It does not re-assess labels using a separate blinded panel.
- **Adjudication of disagreements**: None reported. The LLM judgments were compared with review-author ratings. The paper notes that Cochrane RoB 2 assessments normally involve reviewer discussion and consensus, whereas ChatGPT operated independently; individual human reliability was not established.
- **Headline results**: Overall weighted κ 0.51 (95% CI 0.36–0.66), accuracy 0.59 (0.48–0.70). Domain κ: D1 0.57, D2 0.21, D3 0.22, D4 0.59, D5 0.20 (Table 2). High-risk sensitivity 53%; low-risk specificity 99% (Table 3 text); for three-class overall measures, Table 3 reports high-risk specificity 0.86 and low-risk sensitivity 0.33. Model did not make any observed low-to-high or high-to-low extreme misclassifications, but escalated 19/52 (36.5%) Cochrane “some concerns” cases to high. This indicates high specificity for low-risk labels but only moderate sensitivity for high-risk labels; model’s errors largely concern middle-category cases.
- **Repeatability, time, cost**: No repeat runs, time, or cost analysis. The article raises response variation and model updates as reproducibility concerns.
- **Authors' stated limitations**: One assessor, no independent duplicate assessment; possible low reliability among Cochrane reviewers; 71% clustered in one review/topic; small and constrained sample; static prompts; no protocols, registry entries, or related publications provided to ChatGPT; possible model exposure to open Cochrane Reviews in training; model outputs can vary and the service changes. The authors caution that human and LLM ratings are not directly comparable without considering human collaboration/consensus.
- **Authors' conclusions**: Fair-to-moderate agreement; improve and standardize prompts; establish human interrater reliability in future comparisons; treat direct AI–author comparisons as exploratory.
- **Quotable sentences**:
  - “ChatGPT‐4o's sensitivity for detecting high‐risk studies was 53%, with a specificity of 99% for identifying low‐risk studies.” (Results)
  - “The majority of studies (60 out of 84, 71.4%) were derived from a single Cochrane Review examining the effects of physical exercise for individuals with Parkinson's disease.” (Results)
  - “Since RoB 2 is typically applied through discussion and consensus among reviewers, it is important to acknowledge that tools like ChatGPT operate independently and do not benefit from this collaborative process.” (Conclusion)
  - “With this in mind, comparisons between AI and human assessments should be viewed as exploratory and interpreted within the context of these differences.” (Conclusion)
- **Bearing on G1–G5**:
  - **G1 Algorithm enforcement — supports gap**: signaling-question prompts and an LLM consistency pass, but no code application or algorithm-consistency audit.
  - **G2 Evidence traceability — supports gap**: no verified quotes, source-bound answers, or audit file reported; only article reports supplied.
  - **G3 Reference noise — supports gap, with caveat**: Cochrane author labels are the sole comparator; the paper raises human reliability concerns but neither measures nor corrects label error in its sample.
  - **G4 Adjudication — supports gap**: no independent blinded adjudication of model–author disagreements.
  - **G5 Agentic/tool design — supports gap**: structured prompting and a final summary prompt, not an agent/tool/server system.
- **Implication for ARBITER manuscript**: Taneri is a stronger multi-step-prompt comparator than a simple one-shot study, so describe ARBITER’s novelty as external deterministic adjudication and verifiable execution, not merely asking signaling questions or adding a consistency prompt. Its reported 53% high-risk sensitivity and 99% low-risk specificity also show why a single headline agreement statistic can conceal asymmetric error.

## Worker summary

Gandhi narrows G3 and G4: an independent, masked human panel re-assessed every included trial, questioned published OSRA labels as a possible lenient reference, and explicitly framed expert consensus as an imperfect benchmark. But neither Gandhi nor Taneri reports executable RoB 2 rule enforcement, rule-consistency measurement, verified passage binding, auditable evidence records, or an agent-plus-server workflow (supporting G1, G2, and G5 as stated gaps). Taneri also flags limitations of using consensus-based review labels without independently measuring human reliability. The surprising point is that Gandhi found OSRAs labeled only 12% high risk versus 22% for its panel and 34% for o3; it speculates about leniency/incentives but does not prove the cause. ARBITER’s gap should therefore be framed around algorithmic enforcement, evidence provenance, and targeted adjudication of disagreements, not the general idea that published RoB labels are noisy or that a blinded panel can reassess trials.
