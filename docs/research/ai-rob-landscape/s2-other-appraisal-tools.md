# LLMs on other critical-appraisal and certainty tools

## Search log

Search date: 19 October 2026. I searched PubMed-indexed records and Europe PMC via web search, then followed primary-paper full text, PubMed/PMC metadata, and DOI landing pages where available. Queries included: `LLM ROBINS-I risk of bias assessment`, `ROBINS-E QUADAS-2 PROBAST AMSTAR 2 appraisal study`, `LLM GRADE certainty of evidence ratings`, `ChatGPT Newcastle Ottawa Scale CASP JBI checklist`; and exact-tool searches for `QUADAS-2 ChatGPT`, `PROBAST+AI LLM`, `AMSTAR 2 ChatGPT`, `JBI CASP`, `GRADE ChatGPT`, and `Newcastle-Ottawa ChatGPT`. I screened the result titles/abstracts and followed the relevant primary reports; 8 empirical reports/tools papers are summarized below. The search was targeted and reproducible at query level, but not a formal database search with exportable record counts. Full text was read for the ROBINS-I report, QUADAS-2 report, CASP/JBI pilot, multi-model CASP/JBI comparison, ROBIS/AMSTAR-2 comparison, and Yao AMSTAR-2 study (the last three via accessible PMC/JMIR/CUAJ full text); the EvidenceGRADEr report was read at abstract/landing-page level. No LLM evaluation study specific to ROBINS-E, PROBAST/PROBAST+AI, GRADE rating, Newcastle-Ottawa, QUADAS-C, or AMSTAR 2 beyond the included reports was located in this bounded search. Absence is not evidence that none exists.

## Key sources (8–20)

1. **Saadi S, et al. “Comparison of three large language models' ability to assess the risk of bias using ROBINS-I tool.”** *BMJ Digital Health & AI*. 2026. [doi:10.1136/bmjdth-2026-000034](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjdth-2026-000034). Full text read. On 171 nonrandomized intervention studies rated concordantly by two human teams, Claude, Gemini, and GPT were run twice using “agent-based structured implementations.” Reliability was often high, but agreement with the human reference varied widely: Gemini had the best overall agreement (40.0%, AC1 0.672); GPT overall was 23.0% (AC1 0.267), and Claude varied by domain (14.4–68.5%). Accuracy was calculated only on studies where the model repeated the same rating, which can inflate apparent agreement. The eligible set and human reference both exclude disagreement cases; no adjudication of model-human differences was reported. The report does not establish that the ROBINS-I decision algorithm was enforced in software or that evidence quotations were independently checked. **Direction:** no general tendency to stricter ratings is established; the study primarily reports agreement, and the reference is preselected concordant human ratings.

2. **Kreuzberger N, et al. “Risk of Bias Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies Using QUADAS 2 by Large Language Models.”** *Diagnostics*. 2025;15(12):1451. [doi:10.3390/diagnostics15121451](https://doi.org/10.3390/diagnostics15121451); [PMID 40564772](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40564772/). Full text read. Four models assessed 10 diagnostic-accuracy articles (plus two irrelevant papers) against human expert ratings; mean item-level accuracy was 72.95%, with model range 67.27–74.45%. Correctness required both matching the expert answer and giving an appropriate rationale. The paper identifies reasoning errors, but the accessible report does not describe a blinded adjudication of disagreement or a coded QUADAS-2 algorithm enforcing the domain-level combination rules; reference evidence was not systematically quote-verified. **Direction:** no demonstrated global stricter/lenient shift; accuracy is reported at item level and does not provide a severity-direction estimate. The small, recent, one-database sample limits generalization.

3. **Forero DA, et al. “Automated analyses of risk of bias and critical appraisal of systematic reviews (ROBIS and AMSTAR 2): a comparison of the performance of 4 large language models.”** *J Am Med Inform Assoc*. 2025;32(9):1471–1476. [doi:10.1093/jamia/ocaf117](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocaf117); [PMID 40680299](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40680299/). Full text read. Four chatbots assessed a sample of 20 systematic reviews with available expert assessments. For ROBIS overall agreement ranged from 0% to 58% (Gemini highest); the authors explicitly observed both underestimation of risk of bias and near-fixed outputs. AMSTAR-2 overall agreement ranged 30–70%; outputs sometimes overestimated confidence in review findings. Human experts were the comparison reference; this was not an adjudicated, blinded reference construction. No evidence that the formal ROBIS/AMSTAR critical-appraisal rules were computed in code or that evidence was independently verified. The direction is mixed: ROBIS could be too lenient, AMSTAR-2 confidence too high, undermining a universal “stricter” characterization.

4. **Yao B, et al. “Assessing the methodologic quality of systematic reviews using generative large language models.”** *Can Urol Assoc J*. 2025;19(12):E427–E433. [doi:10.5489/cuaj.9243](https://doi.org/10.5489/cuaj.9243); [PMID 40893099](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40893099/). Full text read. Evaluated AMSTAR 2 in 114 reviews from five urology journals. Human assessments were duplicated and differences adjudicated by a third expert; consensus was the reference. Prompting that followed the human workflow performed better than zero-shot assessment, with overall quality classification described as accurate. The reference had adjudication, but models did not have a separate algorithm service enforcing AMSTAR-2 decision rules, and no independent evidence-verification layer was reported. It shows prompt/workflow dependence and a stronger reference design than published-label comparisons, not a general direction of strictness.

5. **Shereefdeen H, et al. “A Pilot Study on Generative Artificial Intelligence’s Reliability in Qualitative Research Quality Appraisal Using CASP and JBI Checklists.”** *INQUIRY*. 2025;62. [doi:10.1177/00469580251399374](https://doi.org/10.1177/00469580251399374). Full text read. Two reviewers independently appraised 21 qualitative studies and resolved disagreements into consensus; GPT-5 was compared with that consensus on CASP and JBI. CASP item κ ranged <0 to 0.46 (raw agreement 23.8–100%); JBI κ ranged <0 to 0.83 (4.8–95.2%). Direction differed by item: on JBI, GPT-5 called “yes” much more often on worldview-methodology congruity (42.9% vs human 0%) and design-analysis congruity (90.5% vs 52.4%), while it shifted some human “no” judgments toward “unclear.” This is a clear counterexample to universal strictness, with possible leniency/affirmation bias. Prompt requested explicit evidence and location, but no independent quote check, checklist algorithm, or adjudication of AI-human disagreements was reported.

6. **Landerholm A, et al. “AI in Qualitative Health Research Appraisal: Comparative Study.”** *JMIR Formative Research*. 2025;9:e72815. [doi:10.2196/72815](https://doi.org/10.2196/72815). Full text read. Five models applied CASP, JBI, and ETQS to three qualitative papers. Inter-model “Yes” responses were high overall (59.9–85.4% across models), and the authors call this systematic affirmation bias; agreement across models was tool dependent (Krippendorff α .653 for CASP, .477 for JBI). This is not agreement with a human gold standard: no external expert reference or adjudication was the central design. No rule enforcement or evidence verification was reported. Its direction points toward leniency/affirmation rather than stricter appraisal, but it must not be treated as validity evidence.

7. **Šuster S, et al. “Automating Quality Assessment of Medical Evidence in Systematic Reviews: Model Development and Validation Study.”** *J Med Internet Res*. 2023;25:e35568. [doi:10.2196/35568](https://doi.org/10.2196/35568); [PMID 36722350](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36722350/). Full text read. EvidenceGRADEr is a supervised machine-learning system predicting GRADE ratings and downgrade criteria from structured/unstructured information in Cochrane reviews. This is adjacent automation, not a study of a general-purpose LLM’s stated GRADE judgments, and its benchmark cannot establish whether LLMs are stricter than human GRADE panels. It does show that certainty grading is a body-of-evidence/outcome-level synthesis with domain-specific inputs, rather than a direct analogue of a per-study checklist. No LLM-specific adjudication or quote-verification conclusion follows from it.

8. **PROBAST+AI development paper. Wolff RF, et al. “PROBAST+AI: an updated quality, risk of bias, and applicability assessment tool for prediction models using regression or artificial intelligence methods.”** *BMJ*. 2025;388:e082505. [doi:10.1136/bmj-2024-082505](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-082505). Full text read for tool structure; not an LLM performance study. PROBAST+AI uses four domains and 34 signalling questions, distinguishes model development quality from model evaluation risk of bias, and gives structured domain judgments. The development/consensus process establishes the instrument, not an LLM reference standard. I found no direct LLM benchmark for PROBAST/PROBAST+AI in the targeted searches. It is therefore premature to claim ARBITER generalizes by implementation alone: its decision map, evidence requirements, and target estimand would need to be specified for prediction-model studies.

## What this literature says about our story

### Supports

- ROBINS-I has domain signaling questions and an overall judgment structure; an LLM comparison exists, so ARBITER’s design principle is portable in principle. But Saadi et al. report “agent-based structured implementations,” not evidence that a separately coded ROBINS-I algorithm was made the only route to a rating. The study’s restriction to concordant human ratings also means it does not test whether published/human disagreement cells favor a stricter assessment.
- QUADAS-2 has also been tested, with moderate item accuracy but documented reasoning errors. This gives a direct case for separating answer generation from rule application and checking the rationale, although the small study does not establish the benefit of that architecture.
- Reference construction matters outside RoB 2 too. The AMSTAR-2 Yao study used duplicate human ratings and third-reviewer adjudication; the CASP/JBI pilot used human consensus. These are stronger references than treating a single published label as unquestionable, while still not validating whether a disputed model judgment is correct.

### Complicates

- **The strictness pattern does not appear general across appraisal tools.** The strongest direct counterexamples are ROBIS/AMSTAR-2, where models both underestimated ROBIS risk and overestimated AMSTAR-2 confidence, and CASP/JBI, where affirmation/“yes” shifts were seen. CASP/JBI have different constructs and item scales, so they are not direct severity comparisons with RoB 2, but they make “LLMs are generally stricter than human or published references” indefensible without tool-specific evidence.
- Agreement varies by domain and model, and high internal repeatability (ROBINS-I) is not accuracy. Saadi’s filtering to model-stable judgments further makes reported agreement optimistic relative to one-pass deployment.
- GRADE is not just another study-level risk-of-bias instrument. A systematic review team rates certainty for an outcome across a body of evidence, integrating risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, then applies context-sensitive judgments. A per-paper “high risk” label or a predicted GRADE category cannot validate the synthesis judgment.

### Threatens

- Avoid a broad claim that ARBITER’s rule-based design generalizes unchanged across appraisal tools. ROBINS-I, AMSTAR 2, QUADAS-2/C, and PROBAST(+AI) all have structured signaling questions, but their domains, conditional paths, applicability judgments, and overall-rating rules differ. GRADE adds a body-of-evidence target and outcome-level synthesis; CASP/JBI often demand reflexive and interpretive judgments that are not reducible to a mechanical algorithm. Generalize the **architecture** (source locking, structured evidence capture, explicit criteria, auditable decision logic and adjudicated reference) and say each tool needs its own validated decision engine.
- Do not treat “stricter than Published” as inherently more accurate. These cross-tool studies show both optimistic/lenient shifts and weak agreement; they do not establish a common direction. ARBITER’s adjudication findings are specific to its RoB 2 benchmark and its adjudicators’ offered choices.

## Reviewer objections we should pre-empt

1. **“You imply stricter LLM ratings are common across appraisal tools.”** A reviewer can cite Forero et al. (ROBIS underestimation and AMSTAR-2 confidence overestimation) and Shereefdeen/Landerholm (affirmative “yes” tendency). Answer: narrow the claim to the RoB 2 studies in our evidence map; report strictness as a pattern in that subset, not a field-wide law.
2. **“Your rule-enforcement novelty ignores ROBINS-I or other algorithms.”** Saadi et al. implemented a structured ROBINS-I agent. Answer: credit that work; distinguish a structured agent/prompt from a separately executable algorithm that validates every answer path and computes the only permitted rating. State that cross-tool generalization is an architectural hypothesis, not demonstrated transfer.
3. **“Human consensus is not a gold standard.”** Saadi et al. required concordant teams; Yao et al. adjudicated discrepancies; Shereefdeen et al. used consensus. None proves perfect validity. Answer: call our reference a blinded adjudicated comparator, show independent-rater reliability, and report reference limitations (agreed cells and overall ratings not adjudicated) as already planned.
4. **“A rule engine could encode the wrong rule or suppress expert judgment.”** This concern is especially strong for ROBINS-I, where target-trial specification and confounder selection matter, and GRADE, where downgrading/importance judgments are contextual. Answer: emphasize tool-versioned rules, transparent exceptions/unknown states, and validation against domain experts before exporting ARBITER beyond RoB 2.
5. **“Your evidence traceability claim is too broad.”** Some studies asked for a rationale or source location; QUADAS-2 correctness included a proper argument. Answer: distinguish requested/model-generated rationales from quotes programmatically matched to a locked source before accepting a rating; claim only the latter for ARBITER.

## Recommended analysis directions for future rounds

1. **MUST — Recode the manuscript’s cross-study strictness statement to be RoB 2-specific.** Existing data only. In Table 4, separate “direction reported” from agreement and mark “not reported/mixed” for non-RoB 2 tools rather than infer direction from κ/accuracy.
2. **MUST — Add a table footnote distinguishing structured prompting from executable rule enforcement.** Existing study extraction only. Include ROBINS-I Saadi et al. as “agent-based structured implementation; rule enforcement not demonstrated in reported methods,” and do not equate tool name in prompt with algorithm compliance.
3. **SHOULD — Add a compact transferability paragraph covering ROBINS-I and GRADE.** No new data. State that architecture may transfer, while criteria, evidence units, algorithms, and reference design need tool-specific work; GRADE operates at outcome/body-of-evidence level.
4. **SHOULD — If ARBITER is extended to ROBINS-I, benchmark algorithm conformance separately from rating agreement.** Requires new data. Predefine causal question/target trial, effect of interest, confounders, signaling-answer paths and overall-rating logic; validate every item against source passages and test edge cases, missing information, and “no information” handling.
5. **COULD — Run an exploratory stricter-versus-lenient audit on existing RoB 2 disagreement cells by domain and published-label severity.** Existing adjudication data. Keep it descriptive, show denominators and uncertainty, and avoid extrapolating to other tools.

## Recommended text changes

- **Introduction:** Add a short sentence that LLM appraisal evidence spans tools with different targets and rating logic; available comparisons report variable or even lenient/affirmative errors, so strictness should not be presumed general (Forero et al.; Shereefdeen et al.; Saadi et al.).
- **Methods:** Define the comparator as Published RoB 2 ratings and the adjudicated reference as study-specific to this benchmark. Specify that only disagreement cells were adjudicated and adjudicators chose among the blinded labels. Do not call published labels a gold standard.
- **Discussion:** Add: “The greater severity of model ratings reported here should be interpreted as specific to RoB 2 and our benchmark. Studies using ROBIS/AMSTAR 2 and CASP/JBI report heterogeneous errors, including lower risk or more affirmative judgments. Although ROBINS-I and several other tools have structured decision rules that may suit a rule-constrained architecture, transfer requires a separately specified and validated implementation; GRADE additionally requires outcome-level synthesis across a body of evidence.” Cite Saadi, Forero, Shereefdeen, and the GRADE handbook or EvidenceGRADEr.
- **Limitations:** State that we did not evaluate ARBITER on ROBINS-I, ROBINS-E, QUADAS-2/C, PROBAST(+AI), AMSTAR 2, JBI/CASP, Newcastle-Ottawa, or GRADE, and cannot infer cross-tool performance or a general direction of LLM strictness.

## Report

**ISSUES** — Covered direct studies for ROBINS-I, QUADAS-2, ROBIS/AMSTAR 2, CASP/JBI and adjacent GRADE automation; read full text for the main empirical comparisons and verified DOI/PMID metadata for the core sources. The targeted search did not locate direct LLM evaluations for ROBINS-E, QUADAS-C, PROBAST(+AI), GRADE ratings as a target task, or Newcastle-Ottawa, and it was not a formal database export search, so absence claims and comprehensiveness are limited. Confidence is high that strictness should not be generalized across tools, moderate for the descriptions of the included tool-specific studies, and low that every relevant niche or newly indexed study has been captured.
