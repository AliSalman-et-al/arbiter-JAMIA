# PRE-LLM and machine-learning automation of risk-of-bias assessment

## Search log

Search conducted 2026-10-19. Sources searched: PubMed, Europe PMC/PMC, Oxford Academic, SpringerLink, Wiley, AHRQ/NCBI Bookshelf, and web search for citation chaining and evaluation studies. Queries included `RobotReviewer evaluation risk of bias`, `RobotReviewer real-world systematic reviews Arno Soboczenski Hirt Jardim`, `machine learning risk of bias sentence extraction classifier`, `Cochrane RobotReviewer adoption`, and `Cochrane machine learning risk of bias automation`. Approximately 40 unique records from result pages and citation lists were screened by title/abstract; 12 primary studies/reviews and 2 Cochrane or implementation sources were retained. This was a targeted, reproducible query log, not a registered systematic review; duplicate and irrelevant hits were excluded. Key full texts were read for Marshall 2016, Soboczenski 2019, Hirt 2021, and Jardim 2022. Gates 2018, Arno 2022, Armijo-Olivo 2020 and the 2024 Tian comparison were available only as abstracts or indexed summaries for this extraction. The AHRQ evidence map and the 2025 rapid-review update were read in full for their RobotReviewer evidence tables and synthesis.

## Key sources (8–20)

1. **Marshall IJ, Kuiper J, Wallace BC. RobotReviewer: evaluation of a system for automatically assessing bias in clinical trials. *J Am Med Inform Assoc*. 2016;23(1):193–201. PMID: 26104742. DOI: [10.1093/jamia/ocv044](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocv044).** Full text: yes (PMC full text).
   - Foundational system: supervised multi-task SVM classifies RoB by domain and ranks candidate rationale sentences. It was trained using distant supervision over Cochrane reviews and linked trial reports (12,808 PDFs); ratings and sentence selection are separate but interacting tasks.
   - Against assessments in two or more Cochrane reviews, document-level accuracy was 71.0% vs 78.3% for review labels. Top-three rationale text was rated highly relevant 60.4% vs 56.5% for review-author text, a nonsignificant difference. The authors thus established evidence retrieval as a co-equal deliverable, while showing labels did not match human references as well as rationales matched them.
   - Quote (Abstract, Conclusion): “Automatically identified text supporting bias assessment is of equal quality to the manually identified text in the CDSR.” This refers to panel-rated text relevance, not proof that every judgment was supported by its selected quote.

2. **Gates A, Vandermeer B, Hartling L. Technology-assisted risk of bias assessment in systematic reviews: a prospective cross-sectional evaluation of the RobotReviewer machine learning tool. *J Clin Epidemiol*. 2018;96:54–62. PMID: 29289761. DOI: [10.1016/j.jclinepi.2017.12.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2017.12.015).** Full text: abstract only for this extraction.
   - Large retrospective benchmark of 1,180 trials found domain-varying agreement with human assessments: κ 0.48 for sequence generation, 0.45 allocation concealment, 0.42 blinding participants/personnel; overall κ 0.34, selective reporting κ 0.02. Performance varied by topic and domain. This warns against treating a single overall score as general capability.

3. **Soboczenski F, Trikalinos TA, Kuiper J, et al. Machine learning to help researchers evaluate biases in clinical trials: a prospective, randomized user study. *BMC Med Inform Decis Mak*. 2019;19:96. PMID: 31068178. DOI: [10.1186/s12911-019-0814-z](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-019-0814-z).** Full text: yes (PMC).
   - Forty-one volunteers completed manual and RobotReviewer-assisted assessments. Assistance reduced mean time (755 vs 824 seconds); reviewers accepted 91% of suggested ratings and 62% of sentence highlights unchanged. Suggested highlight precision and recall against users’ final text were 0.87 and 0.90. These are workflow acceptance and text-selection measures, not independent evidence that accepted ratings were correct.
   - The trial made the interface-level lesson concrete: expose suggestions in the article and let reviewers edit them. Adoption was tested as a user interaction, not as autonomous replacement.

4. **Armijo-Olivo S, Craig R, Campbell S. Comparing machine and human reviewers to evaluate the risk of bias in randomized controlled trials. *Res Synth Methods*. 2020;11(3):484–493. PMID: 32065732. DOI: [10.1002/jrsm.1398](https://doi.org/10.1002/jrsm.1398).** Full text: abstract only for this extraction.
   - In 372 RCTs, agreement varied substantially by domain; cited rates of irrelevant supporting quotations were 54.3% for allocation concealment, 72.6% for participant/personnel blinding, and 70.4% for outcome-assessor blinding. The authors concluded RobotReviewer could help but could not replace humans, and users should check source articles when quotations are irrelevant. This is direct evidence that quote presence alone does not establish quote relevance.

5. **Hirt J, Meichlinger J, Schumacher P, Mueller G. Agreement in Risk of Bias Assessment Between RobotReviewer and Human Reviewers: An Evaluation Study on Randomised Controlled Trials in Nursing-Related Cochrane Reviews. *J Nurs Scholarsh*. 2021;53:246–254. DOI: [10.1111/jnu.12628](https://doi.org/10.1111/jnu.12628).** Full text: yes (publisher full text).
   - Compared RobotReviewer with Cochrane labels for 190 RCTs across four RoB1 domains. Kappa ranged from 0.04 (outcome-assessor blinding) to 0.60 (allocation concealment), with missing output/reference assessments and domain-specific sensitivity/specificity. Authors describe support in some domains and explicitly recommend human supervision.
   - This is a useful discipline-specific real-review check, but its convenience sample and historical RoB1 reference limit generalization.

6. **Arno A, Thomas J, Wallace B, Marshall IJ, McKenzie JE, Elliott JH. Accuracy and Efficiency of Machine Learning-Assisted Risk-of-Bias Assessments in “Real-World” Systematic Reviews: A Noninferiority Randomized Controlled Trial. *Ann Intern Med*. 2022;175(7):1001–1009. PMID: 35635850. DOI: [10.7326/M22-0092](https://doi.org/10.7326/M22-0092).** Full text: abstract only for this extraction.
   - In seven completed review teams (145 studies), reviewers given RobotReviewer-prepopulated forms were noninferior in overall accuracy to unassisted reviewers (88.8% vs 90.2%; risk difference −0.014, 95% CI −0.093 to 0.065). Time evidence was inconclusive: estimated saving 1.40 minutes, CI −5.20 to 2.41. This supports supervised integration under real review conditions, not automation alone or a reliable efficiency gain.

7. **Jardim PSJ, Rose CJ, Ames HM, et al. Automating risk of bias assessment in systematic reviews: a real-time mixed methods comparison of human researchers to a machine learning system. *BMC Med Res Methodol*. 2022;22:167. PMID: 35676632. DOI: [10.1186/s12874-022-01649-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12874-022-01649-y).** Full text: yes (publisher full text).
   - In two ongoing Norwegian reviews, project leaders declined a manual-only arm; the study compared two RobotReviewer workflows with human consensus. Reviewers accepted RobotReviewer judgments about as often as another reviewer’s judgment, but acceptance was mixed and experienced researchers valued human discussion. Time against fully manual practice could not be estimated; sample was small (26 RCTs), and the tool was not adapted to RoB 2.
   - Quote (Discussion): “reviewers’ ambivalence to automation reduces acceptance of a tool, and quantitative evidence of performance is not necessarily sufficient to overcome this ambivalence.” The authors also noted that sentence snippets might increase transparency and trust, while stressing that snippet presence is not a full explanation.

8. **Marshall IJ, Kuiper J, Banner E, Wallace BC. Automating Biomedical Evidence Synthesis: RobotReviewer. *ACL* (System Demonstrations). 2017:7–12. DOI: [10.18653/v1/P17-4002](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/P17-4002).** Full text: yes (author manuscript).
   - Describes the system architecture and rationale extraction more broadly. It makes explicit that evidence synthesis should make subjective judgments checkable by extracting source quotes as rationales; sentence ranks help connect the model’s classification to source language.

9. **Tian Y, Yang X, Doi SA, Furuya-Kanamori L, Lin L, Kwong JSW, Xu C. Towards the automatic risk of bias assessment on randomized controlled trials: A comparison of RobotReviewer and humans. *Res Synth Methods*. 2024;15(6). PMID: 39327803. DOI: [10.1002/jrsm.1761](https://doi.org/10.1002/jrsm.1761).** Full text: abstract only for this extraction.
   - Evaluated 1,955 trials. Abstract reports moderate agreement for sequence generation (κ 0.46) and participant/personnel blinding (κ 0.59), but poor agreement for allocation concealment (κ 0.25) and outcome-assessor blinding (κ 0.27). This later comparison confirms domain heterogeneity rather than a single stable RobotReviewer accuracy profile.

10. **AHRQ Evidence-based Practice Center. Machine Learning Tools to (Semi-)Automate Evidence Synthesis: A Rapid Review and Evidence Map, Version 2. 2025.** Full text: yes (report and evidence table). No DOI/PMID assigned to the report.
   - Its evidence map summarizes seven RobotReviewer studies of original Cochrane RoB and finds performance tends to be stronger for sequence generation and allocation concealment than blinding. Its table also makes reference standards and accuracy definitions visible, showing why a pooled headline number is misleading. Secondary synthesis; cite primary studies for specific claims.

11. **Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, version 6.5, 2024, chapter 8.** Full text: yes (official handbook online). No DOI/PMID.
   - The handbook is the authoritative guidance for RoB 2 methods. RobotReviewer was trained for the original Cochrane RoB tool, which uses domain classifications unlike RoB 2’s signaling questions and algorithmic judgments. This is a methods compatibility limit, not evidence of an institutional decision rejecting machine assistance wholesale.

12. **Marshall IJ, Wallace BC. Toward systematic review automation: a practical guide to using machine learning tools in research synthesis. *Syst Rev*. 2019;8:163. PMID: 31288817. DOI: [10.1186/s13643-019-1074-7](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-019-1074-7).** Full text: yes (PMC).
   - The authors recommend human input for RobotReviewer and explicitly frame its predictions as suggestions. The paper synthesizes early evidence that labels were less accurate than review labels while retrieved sentences had similar relevance, favoring triage and review support over autonomous judgment.

## What this literature says about our story

### Supports

- **Evidence-linked decision support is established prior art.** RobotReviewer’s core contribution already coupled domain classification with candidate text rationales, trained sentence-level evidence retrieval, and rendered the suggestions for reviewer correction (Marshall 2016; Soboczenski 2019). ARBITER must position itself as a successor in this lineage, not claim novelty for attaching quotations or for a human-in-the-loop interface.
- **Evidence needs evaluation in its own right.** Marshall’s blinded panel compared relevance of extracted sentences with human-selected review text. Armijo-Olivo later found many irrelevant RobotReviewer quotations and advised checking the article. ARBITER’s stored-source quotation checks can advance provenance and auditability, but the current evaluation did not assess semantic support; do not imply that source-found quotes prove the reasoning.
- **The real-review evidence favors supervised use, with bounded conclusions.** Arno’s randomized study found noninferior accuracy in a limited real-world sample; Soboczenski found faster task completion in a user study; Jardim found workflow feasibility but mixed acceptance and no adequate manual-time comparison. The credible claim is that assistive integration can work, not that users will adopt it or that it always saves time.
- **Human labels are a noisy comparator.** Gates, Hirt and Tian show domain-level disagreement and variability across topics/samples. Marshall itself used multiple review labels as an imperfect proxy for human performance. This supports the study’s choice to adjudicate disagreements with published labels, while making clear that adjudication only in discordant cells cannot resolve agreed-cell errors.
- **JAMIA lineage is a strength.** The founding RobotReviewer evaluation appeared in JAMIA and framed the exact pairing of automated RoB classification with extracted support text. Cite that lineage early and distinguish ARBITER’s architectural increment: tool-enforced RoB 2 decision logic, source quotation checks, and an audit bundle, assessed with blinded disagreement adjudication. Do not imply direct algorithmic continuity or that ARBITER merely modernizes RobotReviewer’s model.

### Complicates

- RobotReviewer already used rationales as prediction features, retrieved candidate sentences, and evaluated text relevance. A novelty claim such as “first evidence-linked RoB system” is untenable. ARBITER’s differentiator is the constrained path from validated source evidence and signaling answers through rule-based computation to the recorded rating.
- The earlier system targeted RoB1, commonly dichotomized low vs high/unclear, and largely trial-level labels. RoB 2 is outcome-specific, uses signaling-question answers and a decision algorithm. Avoid implying direct performance comparison with RoB1 results.
- User acceptance was not automatic even when machine ratings were accepted at rates comparable to reviewers. Trust is social and workflow-dependent; a trace or quote display can help explain a suggestion but does not establish trustworthiness.
- ARBITER compares two model workflows, not an ML system with an unaided human team. The older user studies support design lessons and precedent, not estimates of ARBITER’s effect on human productivity or quality.

### Threatens

- Any claim that source-linked rationales, sentence extraction, human review, or real-world assessment are new is contradicted by Marshall, Soboczenski, and Arno/Jardim.
- Any claim that a mechanically generated citation or stored quotation is equivalent to evidential support is threatened by Armijo-Olivo’s irrelevant-quote findings and by the gap between sentence relevance and whether a specific quote entails a specific answer.
- Any statement that Cochrane rejected machine assistance should be removed. RobotReviewer was built using Cochrane data and openly evaluated in Cochrane-oriented workflows; Cochrane’s adoption of RoB 2 changed the target method, while the literature reports that RobotReviewer was not adapted to RoB 2. The evidence supports limited compatibility and lack of demonstrated broad adoption, not formal rejection by Cochrane.

## Reviewer objections we should pre-empt

1. **“RobotReviewer already provided evidence-linked RoB support; what is new?”** Cite Marshall 2016 (JAMIA; DOI above). Answer: agree, and credit it as lineage. Our claim is narrower: ARBITER couples stored-source quote checking with a separate RoB 2 rule engine that must produce the rating and audit record; it does not claim first evidence-linked assessment.
2. **“Why not use RobotReviewer or compare directly?”** Cite Marshall 2016 and Jardim 2022. Answer: RobotReviewer targets RoB1 and older low/high-unclear domains; RoB 2 uses signaling questions and algorithms. This is a design-generation distinction, not an apples-to-apples performance comparison.
3. **“Does a quote beside a judgment make it trustworthy?”** Cite Armijo-Olivo 2020 and Marshall 2016. Answer: no; a quote can be irrelevant or fail to support the specific answer. Our measured property is that quotations are found in stored sources. Semantic entailment/support was not evaluated and is a limitation.
4. **“Published Cochrane labels are an accepted gold standard; why adjudicate them?”** Cite Marshall 2016, Gates 2018, and Arno 2022. Answer: prior evaluations themselves describe human/review labels as variable or operationalize human performance via duplicated reviews; ARBITER treats them as a comparator and separately adjudicates disputed cells. We cannot infer the correctness of cells where published and system labels agree.
5. **“RobotReviewer was noninferior in actual reviews; is this contribution just another tool demo?”** Cite Arno 2022 and Jardim 2022. Answer: those studies address assisted RoB1 assessments by humans, with noninferior accuracy in a small sample and inconclusive time results; ARBITER asks a different system question about constrained RoB2 outputs and compares its ratings with an unassisted model under identical inputs.
6. **“Why should reviewers trust or adopt ARBITER?”** Cite Jardim 2022 and Soboczenski 2019. Answer: prior acceptance varied by experience and workflow; evidence snippets can improve transparency but do not remove ambivalence. Our evaluation addresses rating choices and audit properties, not user trust, usability, or adoption; these need prospective user studies.

## Recommended analysis directions for future rounds

1. **MUST — Compare evidence quality as a distinct outcome.** For each signaling answer, have blinded reviewers rate whether the quoted passage is relevant and whether it supports the answer; separately record missing/multiple-source support. New annotation required. Do not infer semantic support from quotation-found status.
2. **MUST — Preserve reference uncertainty.** Clearly distinguish published labels, adjudicators’ preferred labels, and independent ratings; report the adjudicator reliability and the limitation that only published-label disagreements were adjudicated. Existing data suffice.
3. **SHOULD — Add a lineage-aware comparison table row for RobotReviewer.** Include RoB1 vs RoB2, model classification vs rule computation, sentence rationale extraction, source verification, audit trail, and prospective workflow evaluation. Existing published evidence suffices; cite primary studies and avoid ranking across noncomparable datasets.
4. **SHOULD — Evaluate human use before making trust or productivity claims.** A prospective usability study should measure time, corrections, ignored/accepted suggestions, evidence consultation, and trust calibration across experience strata. Requires new participants and data.
5. **COULD — Test evidence interaction on existing corpus.** Sample ARBITER signaling answers and audit whether tool-retrieved support changes the model response, versus an unassisted answer with identical papers. Existing run traces may permit a descriptive analysis; causal attribution would need a designed intervention.
6. **COULD — Report missingness and failures alongside accuracy.** RobotReviewer studies report missing model outputs and uneven domain coverage; show ARBITER’s rejected saves, incomplete answer paths, and quote-verification failures as workflow outcomes. Existing data suffice.

## Recommended text changes

- **Introduction:** Add a lineage sentence: “JAMIA previously published RobotReviewer, which paired supervised trial-level risk-of-bias classification with sentence-level extraction of candidate supporting text; subsequent evaluations established its role as reviewer assistance while identifying domain variation, irrelevant rationales, and uncertain workflow gains (Marshall et al.; Gates et al.; Armijo-Olivo et al.; Arno et al.).”
- **Methods:** State directly that ARBITER targets RoB 2, whereas RobotReviewer evaluations discussed here concern the original RoB1 tool and are not direct performance comparators. Describe quote checks as source-location verification, not semantic validation.
- **Discussion:** Credit the predecessor before explaining the advance: “ARBITER extends a line of evidence-linked decision support represented by RobotReviewer. Its contribution is to constrain each final RoB 2 rating to a validated signaling-answer path and rule-based calculation, then preserve the source and audit trail.” Keep claims at system/workflow level as the analysis plan requires.
- **Discussion:** Contrast prior real-world results cautiously: “RobotReviewer-assisted RoB1 assessments were noninferior in one limited real-review trial, while time savings remained uncertain; a separate real-time study found mixed acceptance despite perceived utility (Arno et al.; Jardim et al.). Our study did not evaluate human workflow time, usability, or adoption.”
- **Limitations:** Add that verified quotation location does not establish relevance or entailment; earlier RobotReviewer evaluations found irrelevant rationales, and ARBITER did not independently rate semantic support (Armijo-Olivo et al.).
- **Citations:** Cite Marshall et al. 2016 as the JAMIA lineage; include Soboczenski 2019 for prospective user evidence, Gates 2018 and Hirt 2021 for domain variation, Armijo-Olivo 2020 for quote relevance, and Arno 2022/Jardim 2022 for real-review implementation and acceptance.

Identifiers for all primary sources listed with DOI/PMID were checked against indexed or publisher records. Full text was read for four primary papers and the evidence-map syntheses; other included primary papers are explicitly marked abstract-only. The search was targeted rather than exhaustive, and Cochrane-wide implementation/adoption was not quantifiable from the available literature. Confidence is high for the lineage and reported study outcomes, moderate for the broader adoption interpretation, and low for any claim about formal institutional acceptance or rejection.
