# Quality of published risk-of-bias judgements and why it matters

## Search log

Search conducted 2026-10-19. Sources searched: PubMed-indexed records via web search, Europe PMC/PMC full text, Cochrane Handbook and Cochrane abstracts, BMJ/BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine, and targeted web searches for citation chaining. Queries included: `RoB 2 systematic review application reporting adequacy non-Cochrane 2020`; `RoB 2 misapplication overall judgment domains outcome-level`; `Cochrane risk of bias same trial different reviews discordance`; `Hartling 2019 risk of bias assessments same trials`; `missing outcome data meta-epidemiological treatment effects`; `selective reporting analysis plan treatment effect exaggeration`; and `GRADE RoB 2 downgrading certainty`. Search counts: 4 combined query batches; approximately 25 returned records screened at title/snippet level; 11 sources included below. This was a targeted landscape search rather than a reproducible systematic review; no de-duplication or formal screening flow was performed. Full text was read for 7 sources, abstract/record only for 4, as marked.

## Key sources (11)

1. **Babic A, et al. Frequency of use and reporting adequacy of Cochrane RoB 2 tool in non-Cochrane systematic reviews published in 2020: meta-research study. Cochrane Colloquium Abstracts, 2023.** DOI/PMID: not assigned in the abstract record; [Cochrane record](https://abstracts.cochrane.org/2023-london/frequency-use-and-reporting-adequacy-cochrane-rob-2-tool-non-cochrane-systematic). **Full text:** conference abstract only. Of 3,880 reviews, 267 claimed RoB 2 use, but only 213 (79.8%) used the tool; 180/213 used all domains; only 20/213 (9.4%) supplied all signalling answers; 158/213 (74.2%) did not justify judgements. The most direct evidence that nominal use does not ensure faithful, auditable application in non-Cochrane reviews.

2. **Sterne JAC, et al. RoB 2: a revised tool for assessing risk of bias in randomised trials. BMJ. 2019;366:l4898.** DOI: [10.1136/bmj.l4898](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l4898). **Full text:** yes. Defines RoB 2 as an assessment of a particular result, with domain and overall judgements generated by algorithms from signalling answers; notes review practice often applied earlier RoB tools non-standardly and identifies missing outcome data/selective reporting as difficult domains. Overall judgements should inform synthesis and certainty.

3. **Higgins JPT, et al. Chapter 8: Assessing risk of bias in a randomized trial. Cochrane Handbook, v6.5, 2024.** DOI: none; [official chapter](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-08). **Full text:** yes. Explicitly states each assessment targets a specific result and that the overall judgement is determined by the least favourable domain (with justified reviewer overrides possible). This makes study-level or outcome-free labels and overall/domain mismatches interpretable as potential departures, while allowing justified overrides.

4. **Higgins JPT, et al. Using Risk of Bias 2 to assess results from randomised controlled trials: guidance from Cochrane. BMJ Evid Based Med. 2023;28:260–266.** DOI: [10.1136/bmjebm-2022-112102](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjebm-2022-112102). **Full text:** yes. Explains that outcome-level assessment matters: the same trial may be low risk for a full intention-to-treat population and high risk for a post-randomisation subset. Gives practical protocol and reporting advice for Cochrane and non-Cochrane authors.

5. **Hartling L, et al. Disagreements in risk of bias assessment for randomized controlled trials included in more than one Cochrane systematic review: a research on research study using cross-sectional design. BMJ Open. 2019;9:e028382.** DOI: [10.1136/bmjopen-2018-028382](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-028382). **Full text:** abstract/record only in this search. Directly documents discordance for identical trial publications assessed across Cochrane review teams. It predates RoB 2 and should support the general claim that published judgements vary, not be treated as a RoB 2 misapplication rate.

6. **Zhang Y, et al. Disagreements in risk of bias assessment for randomized controlled trials in hypertension-related Cochrane reviews. Trials. 2024;25:405.** DOI: [10.1186/s13063-024-08145-2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13063-024-08145-2); PMID: 38907276. **Full text:** abstract/PMC record only (PMC full-text endpoint challenged access). Compares judgements of the same RCTs across hypertension-related Cochrane reviews and documents disagreement even within one review system. The same-trial comparison is important: labels vary across author teams and review questions, although RoB 2 legitimately allows variation when the result/outcome/estimand differs.

7. **Babic A, et al. Assessments of attrition bias in Cochrane systematic reviews are highly inconsistent and thus hindering trial comparability. BMC Med Res Methodol. 2019;19:76.** DOI: [10.1186/s12874-019-0717-9](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12874-019-0717-9). **Full text:** abstract/record only in this search. A further same-trial/within-Cochrane discordance example focused on attrition, a precursor construct to RoB 2 Domain 3. It cautions that inconsistency is not confined to non-Cochrane or inexperienced reviewers.

8. **Kahale LA, et al. Potential impact of missing outcome data on treatment effects in systematic reviews: imputation study. BMJ. 2020;370:m2898.** DOI: [10.1136/bmj.m2898](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.m2898). **Full text:** yes. Reanalysed 100 published meta-analyses with missing-data assumptions; under plausible assumptions, as many as 22% crossed the null and up to a quarter had at least an 18% relative-effect change under the more stringent plausible scenario. Sensitivity to missingness can alter inference, but the direction is not mechanically exaggeration in every setting.

9. **Higgins JPT, et al. The effects of excluding patients from the analysis in randomised controlled trials: meta-epidemiological study. BMJ. 2009;339:b3244.** DOI: [10.1136/bmj.b3244](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.b3244). **Full text:** yes. Empirical evidence on exclusions/non-ITT analyses shows effect estimates can be biased, with apparent overestimation particularly in settings with high heterogeneity, large estimated benefits and complementary medicine. Direction and magnitude are context-dependent; “missing data means exaggerated effect” is too broad.

10. **Page MJ, et al. Chapter 13: Assessing risk of bias due to missing evidence in a meta-analysis. Cochrane Handbook, v6.5, 2024.** DOI: none; [official chapter](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-13). **Full text:** yes. Distinguishes missing-study/result bias at synthesis level from selection of the reported result within a trial (RoB 2 D5). States non-reporting may depend on p-value, magnitude or direction and can lead to ineffective or harmful interventions being recommended.

11. **Cochrane Handbook, Chapter 14: Completing Summary of findings tables and grading certainty.** DOI: none; [official chapter](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-14). **Full text:** yes. RoB assessments feed the outcome-level GRADE study-limitations domain. Low risk generally means no downgrade; “some concerns” may mean none or one level depending on whether limitations plausibly lower confidence; high risk may warrant one or two levels according to how much information is affected and likely impact. This is not a mechanical conversion from a trial label to a certainty grade.

## What this literature says about our story

### Supports

- **Published RoB labels are an imperfect reference.** Across-review discordance is shown by Hartling 2019, Zhang 2024, and attrition-bias comparisons by Babic 2019. The Cochrane Handbook’s result-specific definition also means apparent disagreement must be checked against outcome, time point, analysis, and estimand before calling it error.
- **Published assessment quality can be thin.** The Babic 2023 non-Cochrane survey found low completeness of signalling answers and rationales, and some reviews claiming RoB 2 did not actually use it. Published labels therefore cannot be assumed to embody the full algorithm.
- **Leniency in D5 is a credible and likely important concern.** RoB 2 D5 asks whether the reported result was selected from multiple eligible measurements or analyses based on results, informed by a pre-specified analysis plan. Detecting this often requires registry/protocol/SAP comparison, not simply reading the results paper. The tool developers identify selective reporting as difficult, and the Cochrane Handbook separates result-selection bias from synthesis-level missing results. This aligns with our Unassisted model’s dominant question 5.1 failure: it often fails to establish or use the analysis plan.
- **D3 needs more qualified wording.** Missing outcome data can materially shift estimates: Kahale 2020 found plausible sensitivity assumptions changed statistical interpretation in a substantial minority of sampled significant meta-analyses. But D3 is context-specific (amount, reasons, arm imbalance, outcome, analysis and sensitivity evidence), and older meta-epidemiology does not support a universal one-direction inflation claim. Our D3 strictness may be justified in particular trials but should not be generalized as proof that all published D3 labels are wrong.
- **The consequence is downstream.** GRADE uses the body of evidence contributing to each outcome and asks whether limitations are likely to reduce confidence; it does not mechanically map every individual High/Some concerns rating to a downgrade. Under-rating important D3/D5 limitations can preserve confidence in a biased estimate; over-rating can unnecessarily reduce certainty. Both errors can change recommendations or synthesis interpretation.

### Complicates

- Disagreement is not by itself evidence of leniency or misapplication. RoB 2 ratings are result-level, so different outcomes, time points, estimands, analysis populations and source documents may legitimately produce different ratings. Zhang/Hartling comparisons require careful harmonization before treating differences as contradictions.
- “Cochrane versus non-Cochrane” should not be framed as a proven binary quality ranking. The 2020 survey provides direct evidence of weak RoB 2 uptake/reporting in non-Cochrane reviews, while Hartling 2019 and Babic 2019 demonstrate inconsistency among Cochrane reviews. The available sources do not isolate the causal effect of publisher on judgement accuracy.
- There is stronger direct support for **under-documentation and inconsistent application** than for systematic author optimism, conscious leniency, or reviewer conflicts of interest as the cause. The searched evidence did not establish that review-author COI predicts lenient RoB ratings. Do not conflate primary-trial sponsor COI, which may relate to results, with review-author conflicts.
- Our labels need the same scrutiny as published labels. Adjudication of disputed cells demonstrates which label blinded adjudicators preferred in those cells, not a universal gold standard. Agreed cells were not adjudicated, and overall labels were not adjudicated; do not generalize observed strictness to all ratings.

### Threatens / key answer

The literature supports the proposition that published labels can be incomplete and discordant, and it particularly supports concern about D5 if authors do not inspect pre-specified plans. It does **not yet establish** that published judgements are systematically lenient specifically in both D3 and D5, nor that review-author conflicts cause this pattern. Our data can support the narrower claim that in this benchmark, adjudicators often preferred stricter ratings on disputed cells and that the unassisted model’s largest localized error was failure to apply the pre-specified analysis-plan logic in D5. Evidence that these domains can alter effect estimates and GRADE confidence makes the issue consequential, but the literature does not show that correcting our benchmark labels would change a specific clinical recommendation.

## Reviewer objections we should pre-empt

1. **“RoB 2 is result-level; a trial-level comparison is invalid.”** Cite RoB 2 BMJ 2019 and Cochrane Handbook Chapter 8. State which outcome/result was targeted, acknowledge published reviews may target other outcomes or estimands, and perform a harmonized subset analysis only where endpoint, time point, and analysis match.
2. **“Strictness against published labels is not accuracy.”** Cite Hartling 2019, Zhang 2024, and Babic 2019. Agree: treat published labels as a noisy comparator; foreground blinded adjudication of disagreements and avoid making meta-label disagreement the accuracy endpoint.
3. **“D3 is not a simple dropout threshold.”** Cite Cochrane Handbook Chapter 8 and Kahale 2020. Explain that missingness must be judged by amount, reasons, differential missingness, analysis assumptions and robustness; report the decisive evidence and avoid asserting universal exaggeration.
4. **“The D5 analysis-plan judgement is a subjective inference from sparse registration records.”** Cite Cochrane Handbook Chapter 8/13 and the 2023 implementation guidance. Report what sources were available (protocol, registry, SAP), whether they predated unblinding, and distinguish missing documentation from evidence of selection. A missing plan can create concerns, but does not prove selective choice occurred.
5. **“You are accusing reviewers of optimism or conflicts without evidence.”** The search found evidence of incomplete application, not causal attribution to author motives or review-team COI. Use “lenient relative to adjudicated judgements” as a descriptive result; omit claims that authors are optimistic or conflicted unless separately measured.
6. **“Any different RoB label changes GRADE.”** Cite Cochrane Handbook Chapter 14. Clarify that GRADE is outcome-level and integrates how much evidence is affected and the likely impact; individual study categories are inputs, not a one-to-one downgrade rule.

## Recommended analysis directions for future rounds

1. **MUST — quantify adjudicated strictness by domain and direction.** Existing data. Report D3 and D5 strata with denominators and uncertainty; avoid broad “published labels are lenient” unless direction-specific intervals support it. Keep discordant-only selection explicit.
2. **MUST — make D5 question 5.1 an explicit error analysis.** Existing model answers and domain labels. Show how often the analysis plan was unavailable, not retrieved, misread, or ignored; distinguish those mechanisms. Add trial registration/protocol/SAP source availability if already in the evidence packets.
3. **MUST — harmonize endpoint-specific comparisons.** Existing benchmark metadata plus source review as feasible. For comparisons to published labels or between review labels, limit the primary diagnostic subset to matching outcome, time point, analysis population and estimand; classify mismatches separately.
4. **SHOULD — report a GRADE relevance bridge, not an unsupported clinical-impact claim.** Existing adjudicated trial/domain labels and meta-analysis membership may allow a descriptive table of whether stricter D3/D5 labels affect trials contributing to key pooled outcomes. New review-level GRADE data are needed to quantify actual downgrades or recommendation changes.
5. **SHOULD — sensitivity analysis for possible threshold effects.** Existing labels. Recompute outcome-level evidence summaries under published versus adjudicated risk strata only if meta-analysis effect data and weights are available. If unavailable, say clinical conclusion impact was not estimated.
6. **COULD — audit review-team COI and publisher status.** Requires new data: extract review authors’ declared financial/intellectual conflicts and compare judgements after controlling for review question and result-level differences. Do not infer COI from domain leniency.

## Recommended text changes

- **Introduction:** Add a short framing sentence: “RoB 2 is intended to assess a specific trial result, and its judgements can influence outcome-level certainty ratings; however, published reviews have shown incomplete reporting and discordant ratings for the same trials.” Cite RoB 2 (Sterne 2019), Babic 2023, Hartling 2019, Zhang 2024.
- **Methods:** Explicitly define the target result for each trial and what counts as the published comparator label. State whether differences in outcome, time point, analysis population or estimand were harmonized or remained a limitation. Define D5 question 5.1 as requiring comparison with an available pre-specified analysis plan; document how “no plan found” was treated.
- **Discussion:** Use: “The benchmark’s disagreement with published labels is consistent with prior evidence that risk-of-bias assessments can be incompletely reported and can differ across reviews. Our blinded adjudication suggests that, among the disputed cells, stricter ratings were often preferred. This is evidence about this benchmark’s disputed judgements, not proof of systematic leniency across published reviews.”
- **Discussion, D5:** “The Unassisted model’s recurrent difficulty with the pre-specified analysis-plan question is consequential because result selection can favor estimates according to their statistical significance, magnitude, or direction. This supports scrutiny of D5 source use; it does not establish that every unreported plan concealed selective analysis.” Cite Cochrane Handbook Chapters 8 and 13.
- **Discussion, implications:** “Risk-of-bias judgements inform GRADE study limitations at the outcome level, but a trial-level label does not mechanically determine a downgrade. We did not test whether replacing published labels with adjudicated ratings changed pooled estimates, certainty ratings, or recommendations.” Cite Cochrane Chapter 14 and Kahale 2020.
- **Limitations:** State that only disputed labels were adjudicated, reviewers’ motives/conflicts were not measured, comparator reviews may have assessed non-identical results, and this evaluation did not re-run meta-analyses or GRADE assessments.

## Report

**PASS.** This slice covers RoB 2 result-level requirements, evidence of incomplete non-Cochrane reporting, Cochrane/non-Cochrane implementation context, same-trial discordance (Hartling 2019, Zhang 2024, and Babic 2019), missing-data and selective-reporting implications, and GRADE pathways. Full text was available for the RoB 2 article, handbook chapters and Kahale 2020; several comparison studies were accessible only as abstracts/records, so conclusions about comparative rates and author optimism remain cautious. Confidence is high that published labels are imperfect and that D5 plan scrutiny matters, moderate that D3 sensitivity can change pooled inference, and low that current evidence proves systematic domain-specific author leniency or reviewer-COI causation.
