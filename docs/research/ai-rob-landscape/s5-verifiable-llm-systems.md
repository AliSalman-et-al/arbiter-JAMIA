# Verifiable and rule-constrained LLM systems in medicine and informatics

## Search log

Search date: 2026-10-19. Sources searched: PubMed, Europe PMC/PMC, Crossref-indexed publisher pages (JAMIA/Oxford, Nature Portfolio, JMIR), arXiv, and web search. Queries combined: `medical LLM hallucinated references citation accuracy`; `SourceCheckup source attribution support medical LLM`; `clinical guideline LLM decision tree executable CPGPrompt`; `LLM clinical calculator deterministic tool code interpreter`; `MCP agent clinical research EHR audit`; `LLM provenance audit trail tamper evident healthcare`. ## Key sources (8–20)

1. **Chelli M, et al. Hallucination Rates and Reference Accuracy of ChatGPT and Bard for Systematic Reviews: Comparative Analysis.** *J Med Internet Res*. 2024;26:e53164. DOI [10.2196/53164](https://doi.org/10.2196/53164) (DOI resolves to title). **Full text: yes.** In 33 prompts covering 11 systematic reviews, only 9.4% and 13.4% of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 references were in the target reviews; hallucination rates were 39.6%, 28.6%, and 91.4% for GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Bard. Even authentic references often failed review eligibility criteria. This is an older memory-only retrieval task, not an estimate of current web-grounded citation quality. Useful quote (Discussion, “Principal Findings”): “the convincing verisimilitude of the references generated by LLMs presents a risk for incautious researchers”.

2. **Wu K, et al. An automated framework for assessing how well LLMs cite relevant medical references.** *Nat Commun*. 2025;16:3615. DOI [10.1038/s41467-025-58551-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58551-6); PMID 40240349. **Full text: yes.** SourceCheckup decomposes outputs into claims and claim-source pairs, then estimates relevance/support. Across 800 questions and about 58,000 statement-source pairs, 50–90% of responses were not fully supported; even GPT-4o web search had about 30% unsupported individual statements and nearly half of answers not fully supported. Physician checks validated question-source alignment and source-verification judgments. It evaluates semantic support, a stronger and distinct property than confirming that a quotation string occurs in a stored document. It also relies substantially on model-based grading, with expert validation on samples, so does not make automated support decisions infallible.

3. **Goodell AJ, Chu SN, Rouholiman D, Chu LF. Large language model agents can use tools to perform clinical calculations.** *npj Digit Med*. 2025;8:163. DOI [10.1038/s41746-025-01475-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-025-01475-8). **Full text: yes.** In 212 trials across 48 calculations, ChatGPT gave incorrect responses in about one-third. In a 10,000-trial comparison, task-specific OpenMedCalc tools reduced incorrect answers versus no augmentation from 16% to 88% accuracy for Llama and from 64% to 4.8% incorrect for GPT-4o (5.5-fold and 13-fold reductions in errors, respectively). RAG and a general code interpreter were also evaluated; task-specific calculation tools performed best. This is direct evidence for delegating bounded arithmetic to known code while retaining the model for interpretation/input selection. It does not show that tools repair incorrect clinical extraction or tool selection.

4. **Deng R, et al. CPGPrompt: translating clinical guidelines into large language model-executable decision support.** *J Am Med Inform Assoc*. 2026;33(4):855–862. DOI [10.1093/jamia/ocag026](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocag026); PMID 41746783. **Full text: yes (PMC full-text record and publisher article).** Converts narrative guidelines to structured decision trees and has an LLM dynamically navigate them; it is not a deterministic rule engine executing the whole tree. On synthetic vignettes in three domains, referral F1 was 0.85–1.00 with recall 1.00, but multiclass pathway F1 was 0.47 for headache, 0.72 for back pain, and 0.77 for prostate cancer. Negation and temporal reasoning remained failure modes; quantifiable lab criteria were easier. This is close architectural lineage and a strong JAMIA precedent, while its results caution that formalizing the path does not remove model interpretation errors.

5. **Anjos de Almeida V, et al. Large language models as medical code selectors: a benchmark using the International Classification of Primary Care.** *JAMIA Open*. 2026;9(1):ooag017. DOI [10.1093/jamiaopen/ooag017](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamiaopen/ooag017). **Full text: no (abstract/issue metadata only for this slice).** Relevant adjacent informatics evidence on model interpretation and mapping into a controlled coding system. A taxonomy/code selection result is not evidence that a downstream rule set is correctly applied; keep concept extraction and rule execution as separately evaluated stages.

6. **MedCP authors. An agentic AI framework connecting language models to electronic health records and a biomedical knowledge graph for real-world evidence.** *Front Artif Intell*. 2026. DOI [10.3389/frai.2026.1883853](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2026.1883853); PMID 42761240. **Full text: yes.** An MCP-mediated agent accesses OMOP EHR data and SPOKE through audited, read-only tools. Evaluation spans 100 clinical research tasks, 617 biomedical factual questions, a case study, and a replication protocol. It demonstrates the utility of tool-mediated access and workflow auditability in research. Its auditability means inspectable calls/queries and supervision, not cryptographic tamper evidence; it does not establish that MCP itself improves correctness or that its outputs are rule-compliant.

7. **Hevia A, et al. ROBoto2: A tool for systematic risk of bias assessment with language models.** *EMNLP 2025 System Demonstrations*; arXiv:2511.03048. **Full text: yes (as reported in `docs/research/llm-rob2/SYNTHESIS.md`; not re-extracted).** Applies a RoB 2 flowchart in code after model answers, with retrieved passages and human edits stored. It is the key near-neighbor: the field already has a tool that computes RoB 2 decisions in code. ARBITER’s differentiator must therefore be the enforced end-to-end path (required/validated answers, quotations checked against stored source before acceptance, no model-authored rating), plus its benchmark and adjudication—not “first to apply rules in software.”

8. **Choi J, Swan H, Kim MJ, Kim HJ.** *J Evid-Based Pract*. 2026;2(2):91–97. DOI [10.63528/jebp.2026.00011](https://doi.org/10.63528/jebp.2026.00011). **Full text: yes (as summarized in `docs/research/llm-rob2/SYNTHESIS.md`; not re-extracted).** Single-trial pilot measured RoB 2 conformance by a model and a coded algorithm; the coded system had higher conformance (43%, 51%, 60% across reported conditions), and rule checking caught transcription errors. Very small, different workflow and tools: useful mechanistic precedent, not a magnitude comparison with ARBITER.

9. **Huang J, et al.** *J Med Internet Res*. 2025. DOI [10.2196/70450](https://doi.org/10.2196/70450). **Full text: yes (as summarized in `docs/research/llm-rob2/SYNTHESIS.md`; not re-extracted).** Ratings derived from the model’s signalling answers in Excel agreed better with trained reviewers than the model-stated ratings did for D2 (55% to 95%) and overall (70% to 90%). This is particularly relevant because it suggests a gain can come from moving the decision function out of free-form model generation, even without changing the evidence reader. The derivation was post hoc and did not enforce valid answer paths during assessment.

10. **Liang, et al.** *JMIR AI*. 2026;5:e93761. DOI [10.2196/93761](https://doi.org/10.2196/93761). **Full text: yes (as summarized in `docs/research/llm-rob2/SYNTHESIS.md`; not re-extracted).** Post hoc passage retrieval and quote-based claim checking in a RoB 2 evaluation found only 60–65% source support despite 90–98% binary accuracy. This shows why agreement/accuracy alone cannot stand in for evidence attribution, and why source occurrence and semantic support must not be conflated.

11. **NIST. HL7 AI Transparency on FHIR.** Implementation-guide project, accessed 2026-10-19. [NIST project page](https://www.nist.gov/programs-projects/hl7-ai-transparency-fast-healthcare-interoperability-resources-fhir). **Full text: yes (project description).** Describes a FHIR-based provenance record identifying AI system, human/automated participants and roles, inputs/prompts, and model-card linkage. This is a standardization effort, not clinical outcome evidence. It supplies vocabulary for provenance and traceability; tamper evidence requires additional storage/signing controls and is not implied by a provenance record.

12. **Nyrhi et al.** *EBioMedicine*. 2026. DOI [10.1016/j.ebiom.2026.106238](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ebiom.2026.106238). **Full text: yes (as summarized in the existing synthesis; not re-extracted).** Four models applied RoB 2 to 100 trials; all shifted toward Some concerns/High relative to published ratings. This is part of the background showing that disagreements may reflect reference-label leniency, not simply erroneous strict model judgments. ARBITER’s blinded adjudication addresses that problem; the current evidence does not establish universal superiority of stricter judgments.

## What this literature says about our story

### Supports

- **The architectural claim should be separation of responsibilities with enforcement.** ARBITER uses the model to read and map evidence, while ordinary software validates the response path, applies the RoB 2 algorithm, and produces the rating. The strongest framing is a constrained evidence-to-decision pipeline in which the LLM supplies fallible interpretations but cannot choose the final rating outside the declared algorithm. The enforceable boundary and measured consequences matter more than calling the system “agentic” or “neurosymbolic.”
- **Evidence that deterministic tools can improve reliability exists for bounded, typed operations.** OpenMedCalc substantially reduced calculation errors in a large vignette experiment. Huang’s post hoc recomputation improved agreement with trained reviewers, and Choi’s one-trial pilot showed imperfect model conformance but better rule adherence after computation in code. Together, these support the mechanism (the answer-to-decision mapping is a separate error source), though strengths and benchmarks differ.
- **Traceability deserves separate evaluation.** Chelli found fabricated/incorrect references in memory-based outputs; SourceCheckup demonstrates that a real, retrieved reference can still fail to support a claim. ARBITER’s quotation presence verification protects a narrower property: that the cited words exist in the locked source. It should be described as source-location/quotation integrity, never proof that the quotation supports the judgment.
- **The study’s adjudication is a substantive contribution to evaluation.** As the existing synthesis notes, published RoB labels are imperfect and recent work often interprets stricter LLM labels as errors without adjudicating those discordances. The 54/62 blinded preference when arms disagreed addresses which workflow was more often consistent with adjudicators. It does not prove clinical truth or generalize beyond sampled trials and this protocol.
- **MCP is infrastructure, not a reliability guarantee.** MedCP shows MCP can expose auditable, read-only resources to a supervised research agent. ARBITER’s contribution is the domain-specific invariant and decision gate behind its tools, not use of an agent protocol per se.

### Complicates

- CPGPrompt is now a direct JAMIA precedent for converting prose guidelines into structured paths. Its LLM still navigates those paths, and performance varies widely by task. Avoid implying that ARBITER is novel because it puts a decision tree next to an LLM or because it makes a guideline “executable.”
- Retrieval and citation widgets alone do not ensure claim-level support (SourceCheckup); quotation presence alone is still narrower. The system claim must match its validator: ARBITER checks quotations against stored source text, while human reviewers remain responsible for whether the source passage justifies the signaling answer.
- Deterministic execution cannot correct mistaken extraction, missing/ambiguous evidence, wrong tool choice, a miscoded rule, or version drift. In CPGPrompt, negation and temporality remain hard even with a structured tree; in calculators, input comprehension and calculator selection also fail.
- Audit logs provide reconstructability only to the extent that inputs, model/version, prompt, tool outputs, source versions, and decision events are retained. A regular audit JSON is not inherently tamper-evident. Do not claim immutability or cryptographic integrity unless ARBITER implements and tests it.
- “LLM as reader, deterministic software as judge” is useful shorthand but not a literal division in every case: the LLM often interprets clinical concepts and chooses/extracts values, which are judgment-heavy. Say deterministic software *executes the declared decision logic*; do not imply that it is an independent clinical adjudicator.

### Threatens

- ROBoto2 already computes RoB 2 flowcharts in code; Choi reports code-based algorithm checking; Huang recomputed model answers. The broad novelty claim “we apply rules in code” is untenable. State the combination and measured implementation: rule execution as the only route to a saved rating, required answer validation, stored-source quotation checking, auditable records, and head-to-head blinded adjudication.
- CPGPrompt is a recent JAMIA counterexample to any claim that prior LLM-guideline systems are merely unstructured prompting. Cite it and differentiate its LLM-driven tree navigation from ARBITER’s independent deterministic RoB 2 scoring.
- SourceCheckup makes “every quote is found” insufficient as a general guarantee of evidence grounding. Use precise language, and foreground semantic judgment as a remaining human/model task.

## Reviewer objections we should pre-empt

1. **“This is not novel: ROBoto2 already uses code for RoB 2.”** Cite Hevia et al. Answer that the contribution is not the existence of code-based scoring; it is making the checked answer path, quote/source check, and rule engine an enforced condition of accepting the rating, and testing that system against an identical-input unassisted arm with blinded adjudication. Include a feature-by-feature table and do not use “first.”
2. **“CPGPrompt already translates guidelines into executable decision support.”** Cite Deng et al. Acknowledge it. Distinguish LLM navigation of the structured decision tree (with residual negation/temporal errors and lower multiclass F1) from deterministic execution of an established rating algorithm after evidence interpretation. Avoid calling ARBITER the first executable guideline system.
3. **“Citation checking can still leave unsupported claims.”** Cite Wu et al. and Liang et al. Clarify that verified quotation presence is not semantic entailment or correct interpretation. Report exactly what the checker tests, its failure cases, and how the study adjudicates substantive labels.
4. **“The improvement may be prompt, time, retrieval, or workflow—not the code.”** Cite Goodell as a mechanistic analogy, not a direct attribution result. Our comparison is at system level and does not isolate components. Existing recomputation of the unassisted arm changed almost none of its ratings, suggesting the main observed difference was upstream answers/workflow, not arithmetic; do not claim a causal effect of the rule engine alone. A factorial ablation would be needed to isolate components.
5. **“A deterministic judge is only as good as extraction and encoded rules.”** Cite CPGPrompt and calculator papers. State that deterministic logic guarantees algorithmic consistency conditional on the supplied answers and correct implementation; it does not ensure those answers are true or guideline encoding is correct. Report rejected/invalid paths, rule tests, and manual code verification.
6. **“Your audit file is not tamper-evident, and a quote match does not establish provenance.”** Cite the NIST/HL7 AI Transparency-on-FHIR project as a provenance model, not efficacy evidence. Describe audit content and integrity controls factually; say “auditable/reviewable” unless hashes/signatures and tamper checks exist. Clarify that quotation is located in the stored source, not that the text necessarily supports the judgment.
7. **“Published labels are accepted reference standards.”** Cite the RoB landscape synthesis (including Gandhi’s masked panel and the adjudication precedent) and acknowledge the limits of partial adjudication. Answer with independent ratings followed by blinded choice among three labels on discordant cells, and report adjudicator agreement and splits. Do not equate consensus with ground truth.

## Recommended analysis directions for future rounds

1. **MUST — Make a system-feature comparison table.** Include ROBoto2, CPGPrompt, Choi, Huang, Liang, Goodell, MedCP, and ARBITER; distinguish rules represented in prompts, algorithm executed in code, enforcement at runtime, source quotation existence check, semantic support check, provenance contents, audit integrity, independent reference/adjudication, and identical-input baseline. Existing literature synthesis and published methods suffice; no new data.
2. **MUST — Report validator scope and audit fields with testable terms.** Enumerate what quote verification checks (exact substring/normalization; source identity/version), what it does not check (entailment, relevance, sufficiency), what fields are stored, and whether edits can be detected. Use current implementation and logs; no new data, but inspect code/run artifacts.
3. **SHOULD — Report rule-conformance by domain and failure class for the unassisted arm.** Separate invalid answer paths from logical rule violations and identify which could change the final rating. Existing 102-trial answers can support it; no new data.
4. **SHOULD — Add a conditional “same answers, different decision engine” analysis.** Recompute both arms from their own signaling answers, report rating flips and rule-conformance; retain explicit label that the unassisted recomputation changed almost nothing. Existing data; no new data. This probes, but does not isolate, the scoring contribution.
5. **SHOULD — Measure quote verification false-accept/false-reject on a blinded sample.** Have reviewers compare accepted/rejected quotation checks with source text; separately score semantic support. Needed to claim validator reliability beyond system logs. Requires new annotations on sampled existing outputs.
6. **COULD — Add ablations for component attribution.** Hold the prompt/model/input fixed and vary runtime validation, source locking/quote checking, and coded scoring in a factorial or staged design. This is the right evidence for a causal claim about which component improves outcomes; requires new runs and adjudication.
7. **COULD — Add tamper-detection/replay tests if making an integrity claim.** Modify/remove audit rows and source files, then test detection and reconstruction using pinned model, prompt, source, and code versions. New engineering/test data required. Without this, describe audit records as reviewable rather than tamper-evident.

## Recommended text changes

- **Introduction:** “LLMs can produce plausible but fabricated references, and the presence of a citation does not establish that it supports the associated claim. In clinical workflows that apply explicit scoring rules, this creates two separable failure points: interpretation of source evidence and execution of the decision algorithm.” Cite Chelli 2024, Wu 2025, and Goodell 2025.
- **Introduction (positioning):** “Recent systems have retrieved and checked source passages, computed RoB 2 flowcharts, or translated guidelines into structured trees navigated by an LLM. ARBITER combines source-locked quotation checks with runtime validation and deterministic RoB 2 scoring, then evaluates the complete workflow against an identical-input unassisted model using blinded adjudication.” Cite Hevia, Wu, Deng, Huang, and Liang. Phrase the novelty as this combination and evaluation, not as the first use of code or decision trees.
- **Methods:** Define the LLM’s function as evidence interpretation/structured answer production and software’s function as input validation, rule execution, and audit-row generation. State how quotation matching works and define “found in a stored source.” Do not use “supported” for lexical match. Give exact model, prompt, source snapshot, algorithm/software versions, and audit-file contents.
- **Discussion:** “The results are consistent with a division of labor in which language models interpret source material and deterministic software applies explicit decision rules. Prior calculator experiments and RoB 2 recomputation pilots support the plausibility of this mechanism, but our system-level comparison does not isolate the contribution of rule execution from prompting, tools, or workflow.” Cite Goodell, Huang, and Choi.
- **Discussion:** Acknowledge CPGPrompt as a JAMIA precedent; explain that it has the LLM navigate structured guideline trees and report its task-specific performance, whereas ARBITER invokes fixed RoB 2 scoring after structured answers. State that both retain interpretation errors.
- **Limitations:** “Quotation verification establishes that the quoted text is present in the retained source, not that the quotation is relevant, sufficient, or correctly interpreted. Our audit file supports review of recorded events; absent cryptographic integrity controls, it should not be described as tamper-proof. Correct scores remain conditional on correct evidence interpretation and correct software encoding.”

## Report

**PASS** — Covered verified medical-reference hallucination, SourceCheckup-style semantic attribution evaluation, JAMIA CPGPrompt, deterministic clinical calculator tools, RoB 2 code-enforcement near-neighbors carried forward from the repository synthesis, and MCP clinical/research workflows. DOI records for central studies were checked against PubMed/publisher records. Full text was read for Chelli, Wu, Goodell, CPGPrompt, and MedCP; existing repository extractions support the four RoB-focused neighboring papers. The provenance section uses NIST’s implementation-guide project cautiously and found no direct empirical evidence that tamper-evident logging by itself improves clinical decision accuracy. Search was targeted rather than exhaustive, and emerging 2026 papers may remain. Confidence is high in the central distinction (quotation presence vs semantic support; LLM interpretation vs deterministic execution) and moderate in claims of architectural novelty, which require a final dated search and precise feature comparison before submission.
