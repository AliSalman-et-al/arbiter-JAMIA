# Evaluation and reporting standards for LLM studies

## Search log

Web search conducted 19 October 2026. Queries covered TRIPOD-LLM/CHART/MI-CLAIM, DECIDE-AI/GRRAS/RAISE, JAMIA and LLM evaluation editorials, benchmark contamination, training cut-offs, and repeated-run reliability. Sources screened: 4 search batches (about 16 query results examined); 11 primary or official sources included. Full text was read for TRIPOD-LLM, CHART, DECIDE-AI, MI-CLAIM, the Cochrane AI position statement and RAISE-related material where openly available; JAMIA editorial/author guidance and the GRRAS checklist were inspected at journal/checklist level. Some publisher full texts were inaccessible in this search. This is a targeted standards search, not a systematic review; counts are search-result screens, not deduplicated database records.

## Key sources (8–20)

1. **Gallifant J, et al. The TRIPOD-LLM reporting guideline for studies using large language models. Nature Medicine. 2025;31:60–69.** DOI [10.1038/s41591-024-03425-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-03425-5). Full text: yes. Living 19-item checklist for studies that develop, tune, prompt-engineer or evaluate healthcare LLMs. Items most material here: 1 title identifies LLM evaluation; 3 objectives; 5a/5b data sources and eligibility; 5c dates of oldest/newest text used in development and evaluation; 6a/6b model identity/version and technical details; 7 prompting/querying; 8a outcomes and reference standard; 8b sample-size rationale; 9 missing data; 10 analysis; 12 protocol/registration; 13 data/code availability; 16a participant flow; 17 performance with precision; 18 limitations; 19 interpretation. The checklist explicitly asks for training/development and evaluation text dates (item 5c), directly supporting temporal contamination checks. Quote (item 5c): “Specifically state the date of the oldest and newest item of text used in the development process (training, fine-tuning, reward modeling) and in the evaluation datasets.”

2. **Huo B, et al. Reporting guideline for chatbot health advice studies: the CHART statement. BMJ. 2025;390:e083305.** DOI [10.1136/bmj-2024-083305](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-083305). Full text: yes. CHART is written for chatbot health-advice systems, so only partly transfers to a RoB assessment tool; its items 3–6 are particularly useful: model identifiers/details, prompt engineering, and query strategy (including timing and repeated queries). Items 7–10 cover evaluation, sample size, analysis, and results; item 12 asks for disclosures, protocol and data availability. E&E says generative outputs vary and prompts need detailed documentation. Quote: “Generative AI outputs vary considerably because of context … requiring detailed documentation of the prompts used to elicit model output.”

3. **Kottner J, et al. Guidelines for Reporting Reliability and Agreement Studies (GRRAS) were proposed. J Clin Epidemiol. 2011;64:96–106.** DOI [10.1016/j.jclinepi.2010.03.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2010.03.002). Full text: checklist and abstract yes. Fifteen items cover purpose, subjects and raters, sample-size rationale, study design, statistical methods, estimates with uncertainty, and interpretation. For our ratings, report the unit/case and raters clearly; distinguish agreement with published labels from reliability between repeated model runs and agreement among adjudicators. Do not give κ alone: show contingency/distribution and raw agreement, define weighting and prevalence-sensitive measures, and state the sampling/paired structure.

4. **Liu X, et al. Reporting guideline for the early-stage clinical evaluation of decision support systems driven by artificial intelligence: DECIDE-AI. Nature Medicine. 2022;28:924–933.** DOI [10.1038/s41591-022-01772-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-01772-9). Full text: yes. DECIDE-AI applies to early, small-scale live clinical evaluation; ARBITER is an offline benchmark and therefore outside its core scope. Useful concepts if discussing future workflow deployment: system version, human-AI interaction, operator training, workflow context, safety, and how outputs affect decisions. It does not establish requirements for this offline validation or substitute for TRIPOD-LLM.

5. **Luo W, et al. Minimum information about clinical artificial intelligence modeling: the MI-CLAIM checklist. Nature Medicine. 2020;26:1320–1334.** DOI [10.1038/s41591-020-1041-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-020-1041-y). Full text: yes. General AI reporting guidance: articulate intended use, data provenance and cohort construction, comparator/reference, analysis, and limitations; release code/data where possible. TRIPOD-LLM is the closer fit for this generative LLM evaluation, with MI-CLAIM as a cross-check rather than a second mandatory checklist.

6. **Cochrane, Campbell, JBI and CEE. Position statement on artificial intelligence use in evidence synthesis. 2025.** DOI [10.1002/14651858.ED000178](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.ED000178). Full text: publisher statement inspected. It supports RAISE and says uses that make or suggest judgements, including risk-of-bias appraisal, should be declared and transparently reported; report guidance used and modifications. This is directly relevant to intended use and transparent auditability, though it is governance guidance, not an accuracy threshold.

7. **Responsible use of AI in evidence synthesis (RAISE) recommendations.** OSF [10.17605/OSF.IO/FWAUD](https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/FWAUD). Full text: recommendations inspected via the position statement and linked materials. A lifecycle framework for responsible AI/automation in evidence synthesis; highlights human oversight, task-specific justification, testing, transparent reporting, and performance evaluation. Do not imply RAISE mandates a particular number of repeated runs or makes a particular reference standard valid.

8. **Liang et al. JMIR AI. 2026;5:e93761.** DOI [10.2196/93761](https://doi.org/10.2196/93761). Full text: reported in project synthesis, not independently re-read for this slice. In RoB 2 extraction, binary agreement with Cochrane labels was high (90–98%) while only 60–65% of generated claims were supported by source text. Supports reporting evidence support/traceability alongside classification agreement.

9. **Gartlehner G, et al. Ann Intern Med. 2025;178:1763–1771.** DOI [10.7326/ANNALS-25-00739](https://doi.org/10.7326/ANNALS-25-00739). Full text: project synthesis, not independently re-read. In a systematic-review data-extraction comparison, human-only extraction was not treated as an unquestionable gold standard; discordant items were adjudicated. This is a useful precedent for explaining the partial adjudication design, but the task differs from RoB appraisal.

10. **Roberts M, Thakur H, Herlihy C, White C, Dooley S. Data Contamination Through the Lens of Time. arXiv:2310.10628 (2023).** DOI [10.48550/arXiv.2310.10628](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2310.10628). Full text: yes. Uses benchmark item dates around known/assumed training cut-offs as a natural experiment in Codeforces and Project Euler and finds performance associations consistent with exposure. Temporal splits are informative signals, not proof of memorization: content popularity and task drift also change over time.

11. **NLP Evaluation in Trouble: On the Need to Measure LLM Data Contamination for Each Benchmark. (2023).** [arXiv:2310.18018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18018). Full text: abstract and preprint record inspected. Argues benchmark-specific contamination measurement is needed because contamination can inflate apparent performance; relevant as a threat framework, not direct biomedical evidence.

12. **A statistical framework for evaluating the repeatability and reproducibility of large language models.** (2025). [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12637745/). Full text: yes. Distinguishes repeatability (same conditions) from reproducibility (changed conditions) and proposes measuring output variability. The literature does not establish a universal run count reviewers require; one run estimates neither within-case variability nor robustness to service drift.

13. **“Is one run enough? Reproducibility of flagship large language models across temperature and reasoning settings in biomedical text processing.”** (2026). [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13197179/). Full text: yes. Notes constrained binary tasks may show less variability than open-ended tasks and recommends reporting decoding/reasoning settings and, where feasible, at least a minimal replication-based stability check. Not a consensus standard or universal run-count rule.

14. **JAMIA author guidance and editorial practice.** JAMIA’s journal author instructions and recent JAMIA/JAMIA Open empirical LLM evaluations were checked. No JAMIA-specific LLM benchmark checklist or universal repeat count was identified. The applicable expectations are transparent methods, clear evaluation against an appropriate reference, reproducible disclosure, and journal-wide AI-use disclosure/policy; cite TRIPOD-LLM and GRRAS explicitly rather than claim a special JAMIA rule. Example of a JAMIA Open study with prompt/model details: [Using artificial intelligence to expedite and enhance plain language summary abstract writing of scientific content](https://academic.oup.com/jamiaopen/article/8/2/ooaf023/8105629). The publisher page is an example study, not an editorial standard.

## What this literature says about our story

**Supports.** TRIPOD-LLM makes model identity, prompt/query strategy, dates of evaluation material, reference standard, analysis and open-science details core reporting items. GRRAS fits the agreement estimands and calls for transparent sampling, rater and uncertainty reporting. RAISE/Cochrane support declaring that ARBITER makes risk-of-bias judgements and documenting safeguards and human oversight. A reference label should be examined rather than silently equated with truth; the project’s blinded adjudication of discordant cells is more informative than agreement with published labels alone.

**Complicates.** Reporting guidance is not a methodological quality score and does not validate the adjudication scheme. CHART and DECIDE-AI have scopes (chatbot health advice; live early clinical evaluation) that do not exactly match offline RoB 2 benchmarking. One run per trial leaves stochastic instability unmeasured. Also, publication before a stated training cut-off only means possible exposure; exact training corpus contents are generally unavailable, and evidence published after a cut-off can still share repeated trial facts with earlier sources.

**Threatens.** Reviews and their published RoB tables may themselves have been in model pretraining. The model could retrieve/memorize a prior label or review narrative, making agreement with that label circular. Conversely, the task supplies trial reports and asks a rule-based judgement, so a memorized label is not by itself evidence that the model used it. Published review dates and model cut-offs can support a temporal sensitivity analysis. Do not describe this as a definitive decontamination test.

## Reviewer objections we should pre-empt

1. **“Where is the TRIPOD-LLM checklist and are you reporting prompt/model/date details?”** Cite Gallifant et al. (2025), DOI above. Report checklist completion in supplement. The planned reporting already has model ID, effort, run dates, software and commit, and quotes the baseline prompt. Add the complete `/arbiter-assess` prompt/skill and relevant harness instructions or a versioned archive; identify vendor/API model snapshot if known, and state explicitly if provider training cut-off is undisclosed.
2. **“The published review rating is not ground truth.”** GRRAS and RAISE do not solve reference validity; relevant evidence includes the project’s own prior synthesis (Eisele-Metzger 2025; Gandhi 2026; Hartling 2019; Zhang 2024) showing published labels are imperfect and sometimes differ across reviewers. Answer with the blinded adjudication and keep published-label agreement, adjudicator choice, and adjudicator reliability as separate outcomes. State that only published-label disagreements were adjudicated, which creates verification/selection limitations.
3. **“Could the model have memorized the review or its RoB table?”** Identify each review/publication date and compare it with the best available model training-data cut-off. Report strata and counts, including post-cut-off cases where ascertainable. Cut-off splits are indirect evidence and cannot exclude ingestion before the nominal cut-off, later retrieval, or memorized trial-report content.
4. **“A single run cannot show reproducibility.”** State plainly, as required by Analysis Plan section 8, that each arm assessed each trial once and run-to-run variability was not estimated. There is no standards-backed magic number of runs; repeated calls on a representative subset, ideally ≥3 independent calls per case to estimate elementary instability and more if precision permits, is a practical follow-up recommendation, not an established reviewer mandate.
5. **“Can anyone reconstruct what the model did?”** Provide exact provider/model identifier and version, execution dates, effort/decoding settings, full prompts, tool/harness and software versions, ARBITER commit, any retries/continuations and their policy, plus immutable source hashes. A public model alias alone may be mutable. Plan specifies much of this; clarify any unavailable API sampling parameters rather than imply they were controlled.
6. **“Why can we not reproduce this on the public benchmark?”** Release analysis code, adjudication data, prompt/skill version, structured outputs and data dictionary, with trial PDFs excluded as planned; include DOI/archive and a machine-readable manifest of trial identifiers, publication dates and PDF hashes. Include a benchmark card explaining inclusion, split, intended use, exclusions, and adjudication/reference construction. Public release improves auditability but also increases future contamination risk; record release date and preserve a temporally held-out update set if the benchmark will be reused.

## Recommended analysis directions for future rounds

1. **MUST — complete TRIPOD-LLM item-by-item supplement.** Map every item to manuscript/supplement page and mark justified N/A. Existing data and planned reporting suffice for most items; add missing prompt, data-source/date, sample-size rationale and access statements.
2. **MUST — distinguish the three reference comparisons.** Present agreement with published labels, blinded adjudication on eligible discordant cells, and adjudicator reliability/prior independent ratings as separate constructs. Existing data. Report the conditional nature of adjudication and avoid interpreting adjudicator choice as a full-cohort gold standard.
3. **SHOULD — temporal contamination sensitivity analysis (existing data plus metadata lookup).** For each trial, record trial report publication date and source review/table publication date. Determine model training cut-off from official provider documentation where available; otherwise label it unknown. Stratify primary metrics/disagreement direction by review date before/after cutoff and trial report date before/after cutoff, with n and intervals. Most discriminating is review publication after cutoff; trial report after cutoff also reduces direct source memorization. If sparse, descriptive estimates only. Do not infer absence of contamination from a post-cutoff result.
4. **SHOULD — report per-trial dated benchmark inventory.** Add publication year/date and source review ID to released metadata (no PDFs needed). Existing bibliography and source PDFs should enable this; check missing dates manually. This also supports a future independent post-cutoff evaluation.
5. **SHOULD — run a replication subset in the next study.** Sample cases across domains, published risk categories, and date strata; repeat identical prompt/configuration at least three times per arm and report within-case transition rates and uncertainty. Requires new model calls. Three is a pragmatic pilot floor, not a consensus number; power/precision should set a definitive run count.
6. **SHOULD — freeze versioned reproducibility materials.** Archive exact prompt, skill, harness settings, model ID/snapshot, run manifest, code and adjudication sheets; include a Zenodo DOI and benchmark data dictionary. Existing planned release covers code/adjudication; add prompt/skill and dated manifest.
7. **COULD — protect a future benchmark holdout.** Keep a post-release update set or rotate a prospective temporal holdout. Requires new trials and prospective collection; current public benchmark cannot be made retrospectively contamination-free.

## Recommended text changes

**Methods.** “We report this LLM evaluation in accordance with TRIPOD-LLM and report agreement analyses with reference to GRRAS (completed checklists in Supplementary Table X). We assessed each trial once per arm; therefore, this evaluation does not estimate run-to-run variability. We recorded trial-report and source-review publication dates and compared results across dates relative to the model’s publicly documented training cut-off where available; these temporal comparisons are indirect indicators of possible exposure, not tests that establish or exclude memorisation.”

**Methods, model disclosure.** Include the precise provider-facing model identifier/version and date of access, Claude Code version, effort, ARBITER commit, complete prompts and skill version, number of calls/retries, and all controllable decoding settings. If the API does not expose a setting or the vendor does not state a cut-off, say so directly.

**Discussion/limitations.** “Because evaluations were single-run and publication dates do not reveal the provider’s complete training corpus, we cannot quantify run-to-run variability or rule out memorisation of published trial reports, reviews, or risk-of-bias tables. Temporal strata assess whether conclusions depend on cases potentially available before training; they do not establish contamination.”

**Open science.** Cite the archived benchmark manifest, source hashes, code, exact prompts/skill, and adjudication data; explain why trial PDFs are not redistributed and how reviewers can access them.

## Report

**ISSUES.** The major standards are covered and the most important additions are actionable from current data: item-level TRIPOD-LLM reporting, dated trial/review metadata, and temporal sensitivity strata. The brief’s known once-per-trial design means repeatability is not estimable in this dataset; current reporting guidance does not set a universal repeated-run count. The temporal contamination search supports a plausible analysis but cannot prove what was included in the model’s training corpus. JAMIA-specific searchable material did not yield a distinct LLM evaluation checklist or an editorially mandated number of repetitions, so claims here are limited to general author/editorial expectations and observed published practice. Confidence: moderate-high for reporting standards, moderate for the fast-moving contamination/reproducibility literature.
