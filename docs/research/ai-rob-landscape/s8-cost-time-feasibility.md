# Cost, time and feasibility of risk-of-bias assessment and its automation

## Search log

Search conducted 19 October 2026. I searched PubMed (including DOI/PMID title checks), Europe PMC/full-text records linked from PubMed, and web-indexed publisher/repository full text. Query groups covered: `risk of bias RoB 2 time minutes per outcome`, `Minozzi 28 minutes RoB 2`, `RoB2 time per study outcomes resource intensive`, `systematic review production resource use critical appraisal costs`, `systematic review total cost labor hours`, `LLM RoB assessment time cost`, `LLM data extraction systematic reviews time tokens API cost`, and named studies Huang, Gartlehner, Crocker, and Minozzi. I screened 20 distinct records from the returned results and included 9 core sources below; a further 3 known LLM RoB studies were checked in the repository synthesis for reported time/cost. Search interfaces did not provide stable database hit counts, so the screening count is a count of distinct records reviewed, not a reproducible total hit count. No independent direct RoB 2 study reporting a monetary LLM cost or energy/carbon estimate was identified.

Full-text status is stated per source. Full text was read for the most relevant empirical workflow studies where accessible; a few exact timing figures are available only in the PubMed abstract or repository abstract and are labelled accordingly.

## Key sources (9)

1. **Minozzi S, et al. “The revised Cochrane risk of bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) showed low interrater reliability and challenges in its application.” J Clin Epidemiol. 2020;126:37–44. PMID: 32562833; DOI: [10.1016/j.jclinepi.2020.06.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2020.06.015). Full text: no (PubMed record/abstract read).** Four experienced raters applied RoB 2 to primary outcomes; average assessment time was 28 minutes (SD 13.4) per study outcome. This is a per-result/outcome assessment, not a trial-level package covering several outcomes. The abstract states: “Mean time to apply the tool was 28 minutes (standard deviation 13.4) per study outcome.”

2. **Minozzi S, et al. “Reliability of the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials (RoB2) improved with the use of implementation instruction.” J Clin Epidemiol. 2022;141:99–105. PMID: 34537386; DOI: [10.1016/j.jclinepi.2021.09.021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2021.09.021). Full text: no (PubMed abstract read).** In 16 trials / 80 results across seven outcomes, time was 168.5 minutes per study during calibration and before review-specific implementation guidance; it fell to 41 minutes per study after the team built and adopted the guidance. Developing and calibrating that document took about 40 hours. The study demonstrates that training and review-specific operational rules can change both reproducibility and time; its 41-minute figure is not a universal RoB 2 constant.

3. **Crocker T, et al. “Risk-of-bias assessment using RoB2 was useful but challenging and resource-intensive: observations from a systematic review.” J Clin Epidemiol. 2023;161:39–45. DOI: [10.1016/j.jclinepi.2023.06.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2023.06.015). Full text: yes (repository full text/PDF read).** Two reviewers independently assessed 860 results in 113 studies and reached consensus. Staff resource averaged 358 minutes per study (SD 183); the number of results and reports per study and team experience significantly predicted time. The article abstract specifies “staff resource,” while the methods describe independent assessment and consensus: treat this as a review-workflow total, not a one-person or one-outcome estimate. The mean works out to about 7.6 results per included study, so comparisons with one-outcome benchmarks need that caveat.

4. **Huang J, et al. “Evaluating the Accuracy of Large Language Models in Risk-of-Bias Assessment Using Version 2 of the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias Tool for Randomized Trials: Exploratory Feasibility Study.” J Med Internet Res. 2025. DOI: [10.2196/70450](https://doi.org/10.2196/70450). Full text: yes (Europe PMC XML and main-text tables read; see repository extraction `docs/research/llm-rob2/w2-jmir-structured-prompts.md`).** For RoB 2, mean human assessment time was 31.5 minutes (SD 12.9) and LLM assessment time 1.9 minutes (SD 0.2). The article reports time, not money/API expenditure. The authors also derived algorithm-based ratings from model answers, so the reported model time should not be read as time for human verification or a complete evidence-audit workflow. Verbatim, Results/Efficiency: “The mean assessment time of human reviewers was 31.5 (SD 12.9) minutes, while the LLMs completed assessments in 1.9 (SD 0.2) minutes.”

5. **Lai H, et al. “ChatGPT for assessing risk of bias of randomized trials using the RoB 2.0 tool: a feasibility study.” JAMA Netw Open. 2024. DOI: [10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.12687](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.12687). Full text: yes (repository extraction `docs/research/llm-rob2/w2-jmir-structured-prompts.md`; published article checked).** The repository’s full-text extraction records model completion times of 77 seconds (ChatGPT) and 53 seconds (Claude) per assessment; the study did not report monetary cost. These are machine response times, not human review time or an independently measured fully loaded workflow. The user interface/model and task design also differ from ARBITER.

6. **Gartlehner G, et al. “Artificial Intelligence-Assisted Data Extraction With a Large Language Model: A Study Within Reviews.” Ann Intern Med. 2025;178:1763–1771. PMID: 41183336; DOI: [10.7326/ANNALS-25-00739](https://doi.org/10.7326/ANNALS-25-00739). Full text: yes (PubMed full text/PMC manuscript read).** In six ongoing reviews, 63 studies and 9,341 data elements, human-verified LLM-assisted extraction took a median 84 minutes/study versus 125 minutes/study human-only, saving 41 minutes median. It measured labor time and accuracy, but not API cost. This is a workflow analogue, not a RoB study. Adjudicators were blinded and checked discordant extraction items against source reports, providing a strong design precedent for measuring real-world reviewer time rather than model latency alone.

7. **Nussbaumer-Streit B, et al. “Resource use during systematic review production varies widely: a scoping review.” J Clin Epidemiol. 2021;139:287–296. DOI: [10.1016/j.jclinepi.2021.05.019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2021.05.019). Full text: yes (repository/publisher PDF read).** This review found substantial variation in reported resources and poor comparability across review stages. Its synthesis identifies critical appraisal among the larger resource-consuming tasks, but it does not support a stable pooled percentage of total review labor attributable to risk-of-bias appraisal. This is the best citation for saying the stage is material but its share depends on review scope and measurement.

8. **Michelson M, Reuter K. “The significant cost of systematic reviews and meta-analyses: A call for greater involvement of machine learning to assess the promise of clinical trials.” Contemp Clin Trials Commun. 2019;16:100443. PMID: 31497675; DOI: [10.1016/j.conctc.2019.100443](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.conctc.2019.100443). Full text: yes (PMC full text read).** Their modeled estimate is US$141,194.80 per review, calculated as 1.72 scientist-years times US$82,090 per scientist-year. This is a broad, assumption-based labor-cost estimate, not an observed invoice and not a decomposition by review stage. It gives context for review production costs but cannot be used to claim a percentage saved by automating RoB.

9. **Vivekanantha P, et al. “Automated data extraction for systematic reviews using GPT-5.2 and Google Gemini Pro 3: A dual-large language model approach in orthopaedic research.” Knee Surg Sports Traumatol Arthrosc. 2026. DOI: [10.1002/ksa.70412](https://doi.org/10.1002/ksa.70412). Full text: no (publisher abstract/search record only).** The indexed report gives GPT-5.2 usage of 382,244 input and 92,600 output tokens, US$1.97 estimated API cost, and 1,617.9 seconds wall-clock time. This is an emerging reporting pattern for extraction studies: report token counts and the price calculation, alongside latency. It is not RoB, and abstract-level access prevents checking whether all retries, human checking, or setup costs were included; use it only as an illustration of a more auditable compute-cost report.

## What this literature says about our story

### Supports

- Human RoB 2 assessment can be slow, especially when multiple outcomes/results and reports are assessed and consensus is included. Minozzi’s 28-minute estimate is explicitly **per outcome**; Crocker reports 358 staff minutes **per study**, with 860 results across 113 studies; and Minozzi’s review-specific implementation document took 40 hours to build but reduced mean time from 168.5 to 41 minutes per study. These data support describing RoB 2 as resource-intensive, with workload strongly dependent on outcomes, reports, calibration, and local decision rules.
- Prior work shows a substantial speed advantage for the model’s assessment stage: Huang reports 1.9 versus 31.5 minutes. But it does not establish the time or labor cost of an audit-producing workflow. Extraction studies show why this distinction matters: Gartlehner measured end-to-end human-assisted time and accuracy, not just model latency.
- Resource use is distributed across multiple review stages and varies widely. Appraisal is a material workload, but there is no defensible literature-based single percentage of total systematic-review cost to assign to it.

### Complicates

- Avoid comparing ARBITER’s 7.6 minutes/trial directly with “28 minutes” as though the denominator were matched: Minozzi is per outcome and Crocker’s per-study figure aggregates numerous results plus two-reviewer consensus. Huang’s 1.9 minutes is the LLM assessment stage. Report all denominators and process boundaries beside the values.
- ARBITER’s US$3.78/trial API-list-equivalent exceeds the unassisted arm’s US$0.09/trial. Thus, a claim that the auditable workflow is cheaper in raw model charges would be false. The defensible economic claim is that the higher list-price-equivalent compute buys a different system output (source-linked quotations, rule-constrained ratings, validation/audit artefacts); whether this additional expenditure is worthwhile depends on human verification time saved, the value of auditability, and downstream error costs, none of which the current API-cost comparison alone establishes.
- The study runs used a subscription. Therefore US$3.78 is a standardized counterfactual/API-list-equivalent estimate, not the cash paid for these runs or necessarily their marginal cost. A subscription’s average cost per trial depends on total usage and the period/accounting denominator; do not divide the subscription fee by benchmark trials unless the allocation method is stated.
- Total systematic-review cost estimates are coarse and model-based. Michelson and Reuter’s US$141,194.80 estimate is not an observed production cost and does not allocate any share to appraisal. Nussbaumer-Streit et al. find broad variation and stage-level reporting gaps.

### Threatens

- A “cost-saving” or “more efficient overall” claim is not supported without measuring reviewer checking/adjudication time in both arms and valuing it with a stated wage basis. API-list-equivalent expense is not total economic cost.
- The 7.6-minute figure must be labelled accurately (elapsed model/system run time versus active staff time). If it includes waiting or unattended processing, it cannot be directly compared with active human assessment time. Likewise, the Unassisted 0.5 minutes and ARBITER 7.6 minutes are system-time measures unless a person was actively engaged for the full interval.
- No energy, water, emissions, or hardware allocation was reported in the direct RoB or key workflow sources reviewed. Do not imply an environmental advantage or burden from token prices or latency alone.

## Reviewer objections we should pre-empt

1. **“You call this expensive/cheap relative to what?”** A reviewer could cite Minozzi’s 28 minutes per outcome, Minozzi’s 41 minutes per study after implementation guidance, Crocker’s 358 staff minutes per study, Huang’s 1.9-minute model stage, or Gartlehner’s 41-minute extraction saving. Answer with a denominator/process-boundary table: RoB outcome vs trial, number of outcomes, whether two reviewers/consensus are included, machine time vs human active time, and model call cost vs total workflow cost.
2. **“Your system costs more than the baseline.”** State plainly that ARBITER’s modeled API-list-equivalent is $3.78/trial versus $0.09/trial Unassisted. Its premium is $3.69/trial; 102 trials implies $375.78 versus $9.18, a $366.60 difference (using the rounded reported per-trial values). Explain that this is a reference-price calculation for list-price reproducibility, not actual subscription spend. Frame the premium as the compute price of the auditable workflow, not as an established cost-effectiveness result.
3. **“What labor did the model replace or add?”** The reported comparison needs active human time for source checking, exception resolution, and final sign-off in each arm. If not collected, acknowledge that this study cannot estimate net staff savings or cost-effectiveness. Huang and Gartlehner show the distinction between AI runtime and reviewer-in-the-loop time.
4. **“Why use API list price when you used a subscription?”** Report model/version and run date, input/output/cached/reasoning token counts as applicable, call counts, retries, and the public list-rate formula used to reconstruct the counterfactual. Then state subscription use and avoid calling the list-price equivalent actual expenditure. If the subscription is a flat fee, report it separately and provide an average-cost scenario only if total eligible usage and allocation are known.
5. **“Is risk-of-bias a large share of review cost?”** Do not assign it a percentage from a broad review-cost estimate. Cite Nussbaumer-Streit for resource variability, Michelson/Reuter for the whole-review model estimate, and present the RoB-specific measured times separately. An actual share would need full project-level labor and overhead by stage.
6. **“Does the automation cost include build and maintenance?”** Distinguish marginal per-trial inference from fixed engineering, prompt/tool development, maintenance, software hosting, and training. For a deployed tool, amortize fixed costs over a prespecified number of trials/reviews or show a break-even range; for this benchmark, label omitted setup/maintenance costs.
7. **“Can you claim green or lower-compute AI?”** No. No environmental metric was found in these core studies, and tokens or wall-clock time do not directly determine energy/carbon across provider hardware and regions. State that environmental impacts were not measured if this is raised.

## Recommended analysis directions for future rounds

1. **MUST — make the current cost arithmetic auditable (existing data).** For each arm report per-trial median/mean and spread for input/output (and cached/reasoning, if available) tokens, model calls, retries, subscription/API status, model/version/date, and the formula/rates used to calculate API-list-equivalent dollars. Reconcile per-trial totals to full benchmark totals and preserve unrounded values. Label $3.78 and $0.09 as API-list-equivalent, not cash paid.
2. **MUST — identify what 7.6 and 0.5 minutes mean (existing logs if recorded).** Separate elapsed wall-clock duration, model latency, automated processing, and human active time. Report median, IQR/range, and whether concurrent work or waiting is included. If time was not logged at this granularity, label the current number as system runtime and do not treat it as labor saved.
3. **MUST — add an explicit cost-boundary statement (existing information).** Note that the subscription was used and that list-price cost is a standardized counterfactual; exclude or separately list fixed development, maintenance, hosting, software, and reviewer verification unless measured.
4. **SHOULD — show outcome-count-sensitive human context (existing literature, no new data).** Put Minozzi’s per-outcome benchmark beside the per-study estimates from Crocker and Minozzi’s implementation study, with calibration, number of outcomes/results and consensus stated. Do not derive a single human time saved from unlike denominators.
5. **MUST for a cost-effectiveness claim — measure human checking prospectively (new data).** In a future study, time task-specific human active minutes for unassisted assessment, ARBITER evidence/quotation checks, exception handling, adjudication, and final sign-off; use paired cases or randomized workflow assignment, blind outcome adjudicators, and report accuracy alongside time. Convert labor to cost using stated salary/fringe/overhead assumptions and price year/currency. Include uncertainty intervals and sensitivity analyses across wage rates and review sizes.
6. **SHOULD — report incremental cost per useful audit (new data plus current logs).** Define a useful output before analysis (e.g., trial assessment meeting prespecified evidence/audit criteria and adjudicator quality threshold). Report incremental system cost and reviewer minutes per such output, plus a break-even equation: additional API-list-equivalent cost versus reviewer time saved at a stated hourly wage. Avoid monetizing “auditability” without an agreed value or outcome measure.
7. **COULD — amortize fixed costs and model deployment scenarios (new cost inventory).** Inventory engineering, review-specific guidance, maintenance, training, and hosting; show per-trial costs at low/central/high deployment volumes and distinguish academic subscription use from API billing. Minozzi’s approximately 40-hour review-guidance development makes setup costs a plausible reviewer concern, but is not directly comparable to software engineering.
8. **COULD — add environmental accounting only if data can be obtained (new provider/compute data).** Request provider energy/region information or run on infrastructure with measured power and grid mix. Report functional unit (e.g., per trial assessed) and boundaries; do not infer emissions from tokens or price alone.

## Recommended text changes

- **Methods (cost):** “Model usage was delivered through a subscription. To make compute usage comparable and reproducible, we calculated an API-list-equivalent cost for each trial from recorded token use and the provider’s published per-token rates on [date]; this is a counterfactual reference price, not the amount paid for the study runs. Human verification time, subscription allocation, and fixed development/maintenance costs were [not measured / reported separately].” Fill the brackets with the actual model, pricing date, token categories, and coverage before manuscript submission.
- **Results (time and cost):** “Median system runtime was 7.6 minutes per trial for ARBITER and 0.5 minutes for Unassisted. The corresponding API-list-equivalent costs were US$3.78 and US$0.09 per trial. These figures measure system runtime and standardized compute price; they do not estimate net reviewer time saved or total deployed cost.” Adjust “median” if the study statistic differs; current brief only supplies the values, so verify the statistic label.
- **Discussion:** “The additional list-price-equivalent compute for ARBITER (US$3.69 per trial using rounded values) should be interpreted as a resource cost for producing a rule-constrained, source-traceable assessment and audit artefact. The study did not measure whether that premium is offset by less human checking or fewer downstream errors, and therefore does not establish cost-effectiveness. Human RoB 2 assessment time varies substantially with outcome count, reports, review-specific guidance and consensus (Minozzi 2020, 2022; Crocker 2023).”
- **Discussion/context:** Cite Huang for rapid model assessment time while noting it omits an end-to-end cost comparison; cite Gartlehner as evidence that reviewer-in-the-loop extraction can be evaluated prospectively using accuracy and staff time. Avoid implying those extraction results transfer quantitatively to RoB.
- **Limitations:** “We report API-list-equivalent costs because the benchmark used a subscription; they are not actual marginal cash costs. We did not measure active human verification time, allocate the subscription fee, or include software development, maintenance, or infrastructure overhead. Consequently, we cannot estimate net labor savings, total cost per completed review, cost-effectiveness, or environmental impact.”

## Report

**PASS** — I covered measured RoB 2 human time per outcome and per study, effects of review-specific calibration, an additional multi-outcome review-level time study, direct LLM RoB time, LLM-assisted extraction time, whole-review cost estimates and their limitations, API-list-equivalent reporting, subscription accounting, and missing environmental metrics. DOI/title resolution was confirmed for the included DOI-bearing sources. Full text was read for Crocker, Huang, Gartlehner, Michelson/Reuter, and Nussbaumer-Streit; the Minozzi timing figures were available and checked in PubMed abstracts, and the 2026 token/cost example was abstract-level only. No stable, field-wide convention for LLM compute-cost accounting or a defensible percentage of total review cost attributable to appraisal emerged. Confidence is high on reported time/cost figures and their denominators, moderate on search completeness because result counts were not exposed consistently and full text was unavailable for two abstracts.
