# Human–AI collaboration in evidence synthesis and appraisal

## Search log

Search date: 2026-10-19. Searches used PubMed (title/abstract terms for AI/LLM plus systematic review, screening, data extraction, risk of bias, and human-AI collaboration), Europe PMC/PubMed records, Google/web search for primary papers and SWAR protocols, and arXiv for computing/HCI studies. Example queries: `("large language model" OR LLM OR AI) AND (systematic review OR evidence synthesis) AND (human OR reviewer OR collaboration)`; `("automation bias" OR overreliance OR anchoring) AND (clinical OR explanation OR rationale)`; `"study within a review" AI data extraction`; `LLM risk of bias second reviewer human`. Search engines returned ranked results rather than stable deduplicated result sets; I screened the first 20–30 relevant results per query family (roughly 90 titles/abstracts), followed citation links for primary studies, and included 12 sources. This was a focused landscape search, not a PRISMA-complete review. Full text was read for 8 core sources; 4 were assessed from abstracts/records only (stated per item). Identifiers were checked against PubMed, journal, or Crossref-linked primary records.

## Key sources (8–20)

1. **Gartlehner G, et al. Artificial Intelligence-Assisted Data Extraction With a Large Language Model: A Study Within Reviews. Ann Intern Med. 2025;178:1763–1771. DOI: [10.7326/ANNALS-25-00739](https://doi.org/10.7326/ANNALS-25-00739); PMID 41183336. Full text: yes.** Prospective parallel-group SWAR across six ongoing reviews, comparing AI-assisted with human-only extraction over 63 studies and 9,341 data elements, with blinded adjudication. Errors were 9.0% vs 11.0%; major errors 2.5% vs 2.7%; AI assistance saved median 41 minutes per study. This supports feasibility and modest time savings under real workflow conditions, not replacement of independent RoB assessors. Adjudicators checked discrepancies against source reports; the human-only process was not treated as a perfect gold standard. Quote (Abstract, Results): “The AI-assisted method reduced data extraction time by a median of 41 minutes per study.”

2. **Jacobs M, et al. How machine-learning recommendations influence clinician treatment selections: the example of the antidepressant selection. Transl Psychiatry. 2021;11:108. DOI: [10.1038/s41398-021-01224-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-021-01224-x); PMID 33542191. Full text: yes.** In a within-subject experiment with 220 clinicians, recommendations did not improve treatment accuracy overall; incorrect recommendations with simple interpretable explanations reduced accuracy. Explanations did not reliably neutralize over-reliance. This is a relevant analogue for reviewers confronted with plausible AI ratings and rationales, though the clinical vignette task is not evidence appraisal. Quote (Abstract): “explanations are insufficient for addressing overreliance on imperfect ML algorithms.”

3. **Gaube S, et al. Do as AI say: susceptibility in deployment of clinical decision-aids. npj Digit Med. 2021;4:31. DOI: [10.1038/s41746-021-00385-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-021-00385-9); PMID 33608629. Full text: yes.** Experiments with clinical decision aids show that users can be influenced by recommendations, including when the recommendation is incorrect; susceptibility varies by user and task. Supports treating AI advice as a potential anchor, not a neutral second opinion. The study does not test systematic-review decisions.

4. **Buçinca Z, Malaya MB, Gajos KZ. To Trust or to Think: Cognitive Forcing Functions Can Reduce Overreliance on AI in AI-assisted Decision-making. Proc ACM Hum-Comput Interact. 2021;5(CSCW1):188. DOI: [10.1145/3449287](https://doi.org/10.1145/3449287). Full text: yes.** In an experiment (N=199), forcing users to make an initial judgment or otherwise engage before seeing/accepting AI advice reduced over-reliance more than simple explanations, with usability/acceptance trade-offs. Implication: a reviewer should record a provisional judgment before exposure to the AI output if the study aims to measure independent judgment and limit anchoring. This does not prove that such a protocol improves RoB accuracy.

5. **Beck J, Eckman S, Kern C, Kreuter F. Bias in the Loop: How Humans Evaluate AI-Generated Suggestions. Harvard Data Science Review. 2026. DOI: [10.1162/99608f92.0e98898d](https://doi.org/10.1162/99608f92.0e98898d). Full text: yes.** Randomized experiment with 2,784 participants verifying AI-extracted greenhouse-gas values. Demonstrates experimentally that the evaluation process itself can be shaped by AI suggestions; the work varies error conditions and reviewer/task features. It is not biomedical evidence synthesis, but closely resembles verification of extracted values and warns that plausible suggestions can bias error detection.

6. **Gartlehner G, et al. Benchmarking Human–AI collaboration for common evidence appraisal tools. J Clin Epidemiol. 2024;175:111533. DOI: [10.1016/j.jclinepi.2024.111533](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2024.111533). Full text: yes.** Compared five LLMs with human consensus on PRISMA, AMSTAR and PRECIS-2, then explored potential human-AI efficiency. Model performance differed substantially across tools and individual criteria; the work is a benchmark/simulation rather than a prospective workflow trial. It motivates criterion-level rather than global replacement claims, and does not establish that a human checking AI scores is more accurate or faster.

7. **Huotala A, Kuutila M, Ralph P, Mäntylä M. The Promise and Challenges of Using LLMs to Accelerate the Screening Process of Systematic Reviews. arXiv:2404.15667. DOI: [10.48550/arXiv.2404.15667](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2404.15667). Full text: yes.** Study of LLM-assisted title/abstract screening in software-engineering reviews. Simplifying abstracts reduced screening time but did not improve performance; full automation performance depended on task/model. Cross-domain and preprint evidence, useful mainly to show that AI involvement does not automatically increase human accuracy.

9. **Gartlehner G, Affengruber L, Titscher V, et al. Prompt engineering of large language models for paper screening in medical meta-analyses and systematic reviews: a prospective comparative study. Res Synth Methods. 2026. DOI: [10.1017/rsm.2026.10093](https://doi.org/10.1017/rsm.2026.10093). Full text: abstract/record only.** Prospective comparison in medical reviews reports that a single human screener and LLM screening had similar sensitivity in its setting, while humans had higher specificity; it estimates a large reduction in second-reviewer workload. This is screening, not appraisal. It does not establish that AI qualifies as an independent human reviewer or that consequential exclusions can safely be accepted without human verification.

10. **“Transforming literature screening: the emerging role of large language models in systematic reviews.” PNAS. DOI: [10.1073/pnas.2411962122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2411962122). Full text: yes.** Three review case studies explored models as screeners and workload reduction. Estimated reductions in one reviewer’s workload were large but varied by review (40%–92%); authors caution setup/validation overhead and recommend a second human reviewer independently establish criteria. This is exploratory workflow evidence, not prospective randomized human-AI outcome evidence.

11. **Trad et al. Analysis of article screening and data extraction performance by an AI systematic literature review platform. Front Artif Intell. 2025. DOI: [10.3389/frai.2025.1662202](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2025.1662202). Full text: yes.** Platform evaluation found disagreement between human reviewers for a portion of AI errors (20.7% of abstract screening, 35.7% full-text screening, 61.0% extraction errors). The authors call for measurement of time needed to correct AI errors in human-in-the-loop workflows. Strong reminder that human consensus is noisy, but also that headline automated time savings can omit verification burden.

12. **Gandhi et al. Large language models for risk-of-bias assessment in RCTs. PLoS One. 2026. DOI: [10.1371/journal.pone.0353155](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0353155). Full text: yes (also in the existing llm-rob2 synthesis; direct RoB results are not re-extracted here).** Particularly relevant to the adjudicator-blinding issue: masked panel re-rated all trials, not only disagreements. As documented in the existing synthesis, published labels classified 12% high risk, the panel 22%, and o3 34%. A model’s rating format, characteristic rationale, or strictness may reveal provenance even when an arm label is hidden. The full paper should be cited for its exact masking protocol; this slice uses it only as context.

13. **Gartlehner G, et al. Responsible Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Rapid Reviews: A Position Statement From the Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methods Group. Cochrane Evid Synth Methods. 2025. DOI: [10.1002/cesm.70063](https://doi.org/10.1002/cesm.70063). Full text: yes.** Recommends human verification or validation within the review (SWAR) when using AI tools. Guidance, not evidence that verification prevents bias.

## What this literature says about our story

### Supports

- **ARBITER can safely claim a structured AI assistant/system that makes auditable, reviewable RoB suggestions. It cannot claim to be a validated second reviewer or to replace one.** Its current study compared two model workflows; it did not randomize human reviewers to AI assistance, measure reviewer accuracy after assistance, or compare a human+ARBITER team against dual independent human assessment. The prospective Gartlehner SWAR supports testing AI assistance in live review work and suggests time savings can coexist with residual errors.
- The distinction between **machine accuracy** and **team performance** matters. An AI may improve a human’s throughput while anchoring them on wrong suggestions. Clinical collaboration experiments (Jacobs; Gaube) show that a rationale does not guarantee correction of erroneous advice; forcing an independent initial judgment may help (Buçinca).
- Evidence of efficiency should include verification time and corrections, not just model runtime or first-pass screening time. Gartlehner’s 41-minute median saving per study is a useful benchmark for extraction, not a transferable ARBITER estimate.
- Human adjudication is not a noiseless reference. Gartlehner’s dual workflows and the existing direct RoB literature both support reporting human disagreement and treating published labels as fallible. ARBITER’s disagreement-only adjudication is efficient but leaves concordant-with-publication cells assumed correct and risks inflating absolute accuracy (as already acknowledged in the analysis plan).

### Complicates

- Rationales and citations can be **persuasive cues** as well as evidence. A correct-looking quote audit establishes that text is present in a source; it does not establish that the quote entails the signalling answer, that the answer is contextually correct, or that the algorithmic rating is right. A polished audit trail could create greater confidence and reduce scrutiny unless reviewers are explicitly asked to verify the link.
- Strictness that adjudicators endorse in disputed cells is promising, but the current evidence supports “adjudicators preferred ARBITER more often on the selected disputed cells,” not generalized human-AI superiority. Ratings were chosen from three offered labels; both arms’ outputs may be recognizable through style or rationale, compromising effective blinding.
- Screening results (including prospective screening papers) cannot justify replacing a second reviewer for risk-of-bias appraisal: the task and error costs differ. Risk-of-bias judgments are interpretive, domain-specific and consequential for certainty assessments.

### Threatens

- Any language implying **“second reviewer,” “independent reviewer,” or “replaces one reviewer”** is threatened by lack of prospective team-level evidence, lack of repeated runs, shared model and prompt dependence, and potential automation bias. AI output generated from the same report and shared appraisal rules is not an independent human judgment.
- Claims that “showing rationale/evidence improves accuracy” are unsupported. Evidence indicates effects depend on design and may be neutral or harmful when recommendations are wrong. Source traceability and decision support are separate properties.
- The recognition problem is methodological: a blinded label or anonymous arm code does not ensure blinding when output prose, formatting, cited quotations, or distinctive strictness reveals which workflow produced it. Because adjudicators in ARBITER saw only disputed cells and chose among labels, the audit and style may cue the preferred arm. Current data cannot quantify that cueing effect.

## Reviewer objections we should pre-empt

1. **“You call this a second reviewer, but there is no independent human+AI workflow evaluation.”** Cite Gartlehner 2025 SWAR and the direct benchmark (Gartlehner et al. 2024). Answer: describe ARBITER as an AI assessment system / assistant whose outputs require expert review; current evaluation is system-level against an unassisted model, not human replacement or team benefit.
2. **“Human verification will rubber-stamp fluent, auditable-looking errors.”** Cite Jacobs 2021, Gaube 2021, Buçinca 2021, Beck et al. 2026. Answer: acknowledge automation bias and that auditability is inspectability, not proof of correctness. Require reviewers to verify the cited passage against the source and independently assess whether it supports the signalling answer; measure acceptance of planted wrong answers in the next study.
3. **“Showing explanations or evidence should improve decisions.”** Cite Jacobs and Buçinca. Answer: explanation effects are mixed; a rationale can increase trust without improving calibration. Do not infer a causal accuracy benefit from quotation verification, and state that ARBITER checks presence of quotations, not their entailment/support (per Round 14 claim discipline).
4. **“You saved time by shifting work into verification or adjudication.”** Cite Gartlehner 2025 and Trad et al. 2025. Answer: current study did not evaluate human workflow time. Report runtime/cost separately from review labor, and propose prospective time logging including setup, source checking, error correction and adjudication.
5. **“Your adjudicators could infer which output was ARBITER.”** Cite the blinded adjudication precedent in Gartlehner 2025 and discuss the information leakage in this study. Answer: call the process label-blinded only if arm names were concealed; do not claim successful blinding. Report whether raters were asked to guess source and whether guesses were correct (new data needed). For future ratings, normalize layout and paraphrase or display only identical structured fields while preserving evidence; test blinding by source-guess accuracy.
6. **“An AI second reviewer is not independent and can share the first reviewer’s biases.”** Cite PNAS 2025 screening analysis and the 2024 appraisal benchmark. Answer: ARBITER has no independent human reviewer role in this dataset; future work should compare independent human ratings before exposure, AI-only suggestions, and post-exposure final ratings, with disagreement adjudicated by a separate panel.
7. **“A prospective SWAR is needed before operational claims.”** Cite Gartlehner 2025 and Cochrane Rapid Reviews position statement. Answer: agree; propose a preregistered, multicentre SWAR nested in ongoing RoB 2 reviews before asserting savings, preserved accuracy, or reviewer replacement.

## Recommended analysis directions for future rounds

1. **MUST — Tighten claims now (existing data; no new data).** Replace any “second reviewer” or “replacement” wording with “AI assistant/system for RoB 2 assessment” and explicitly say the benchmark tested system outputs, not human-AI team performance. This is necessary to align claims with the design.
2. **MUST — State adjudication blinding limits (existing data plus methods clarification).** Explain exactly what was concealed (arm names, system identity, reports/quotes, output style) and whether adjudicators could see rationales. Do not describe this simply as blinded if the output itself could reveal source. Ask adjudicators after the fact whether they inferred provenance only if that question was actually collected; otherwise say it was not measured.
3. **MUST — Separate auditability from substantive validity (existing data; no new data).** Say quotations were found in stored sources, not that they supported the judgement. Avoid implying human verification or rationales improve accuracy because neither was experimentally isolated.
4. **SHOULD — Prospective SWAR of human+ARBITER workflow (needs new data).** Embed in several ongoing reviews; preregister one review question per study or a set of distinct trials/domains. Randomize review units (e.g., trial-domain cells or studies) within review and stratify by domain/review to: (A) human-only, independent assessment; (B) human sees ARBITER answer plus rationale/evidence; (C) human sees ARBITER answer/evidence after recording their own provisional answer. Keep two independent human assessments in a validation sample, with a third expert panel resolving disagreements. Avoid treating published labels as truth.
5. **SHOULD — Measure anchoring and accuracy, not agreement alone (needs new data).** Capture human first-pass and final ratings, AI correctness against a prespecified expert reference, whether the human changes toward/away from AI, acceptance of deliberately seeded AI errors, and domain-specific sensitivity for High/Some concerns. Analyze interaction between AI correctness and exposure; report calibration and reviewer-level clustering.
6. **SHOULD — Measure net workload and harms (needs new data).** Instrument time for setup, reading, first rating, checking each quote, correcting errors, resolving disagreement and training. Report total person-minutes per eligible trial/domain and error severity, with confidence intervals. Compare net time and final accuracy against conventional dual-human review.
7. **SHOULD — Test whether evidence presentation changes outcomes (needs new data).** Factorially randomize structured rating only vs rating+rationale vs rating+verbatim quote/source locator, while holding the model answer fixed; include a no-AI condition. This isolates whether explanation/evidence helps reviewers detect errors or simply increases acceptance. A further arm with a required independent first judgment tests cognitive forcing.
8. **COULD — Audit style unblinding (needs new data; can use current output examples).** Give adjudicators anonymized, format-normalized examples and ask them to guess workflow/source and confidence. Quantify accuracy versus chance; repeat after removing prose/style cues. Current data cannot retrospectively identify how much recognition affected preferences.
9. **COULD — Design an adjudicator experiment (needs new data).** Randomize adjudicators to see no AI output, normalized AI label only, or full rationale/evidence on the same randomly sampled disagreements. This estimates the causal effect of the visible AI style and rationale on adjudication, separately from rating quality.

## Recommended text changes

- **Introduction:** “We evaluated ARBITER as an auditable AI assessment system for RoB 2. The study compares its outputs with those of an unassisted model; it does not test whether ARBITER can replace an independent human reviewer or improve a human–AI team.”
- **Methods:** “On cells where the two model workflows differed from the published rating, adjudicators selected among the three RoB 2 categories after an independent rating. We concealed the workflow labels [specify exactly]; because output features may have revealed workflow identity, successful source blinding was not established.” Use the bracket only after checking the adjudication protocol; do not claim concealment that did not occur.
- **Results:** “Adjudicators chose ARBITER more often than the unassisted model on cells where the workflows disagreed.” Avoid “ARBITER was the better second reviewer.”
- **Discussion:** “These results support ARBITER as an assistant for producing structured, inspectable assessments. They do not establish that a human reviewer using ARBITER is more accurate, faster overall, or able to replace one member of a dual-reviewer team. Prospective study-within-review testing is needed.”
- **Limitations:** “Reviewers may anchor on an AI recommendation or its rationale; a traceable quotation can be mistaken for evidence that supports a judgement. We measured quotation presence in stored sources, not entailment, human verification behavior, or automation bias. Output style may also have compromised adjudicator blinding; source recognition was not measured.”
- **Citations:** Add Jacobs et al. (2021), Gaube et al. (2021), Buçinca et al. (2021), Gartlehner et al. (2025 SWAR), and Gartlehner et al. (2024 appraisal benchmark) to support the collaboration limits and proposed next study. Cite the Cochrane position statement for SWAR guidance, not as empirical proof.

## Report

PASS — Covered replacement/second-reviewer claims across screening, extraction and RoB; verification workload; automation bias and anchoring; rationale/evidence effects; SWAR design; and possible unblinding from recognizable AI outputs. Read full texts for the prospective extraction SWAR, clinical decision aid experiments, cognitive-forcing experiment, HCI screening study, screening platform evaluation, and linked evidence-appraisal literature; four included records were available only as abstracts/records and are explicitly marked. DOI/PMID identifiers were confirmed against PubMed or primary journal records. Confidence is high for the recommendation that ARBITER’s present evidence supports an auditable assistant/system claim only, and moderate for extrapolation from clinical or screening tasks to RoB 2 human collaboration; no prospective ARBITER-in-review outcome data were found.
